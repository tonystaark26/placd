## Card C14 · Double Entry: The Rules of Debit and Credit

**In one line**
Every transaction touches at least two accounts, one debited and one credited for the same amount, so the books always balance.

**Explain it simply**
**Debit (Dr.)** is the left side of an account; **credit (Cr.)** is the right side. Neither word means "good" or "bad".

**Modern (accounting-equation) rules:**
| Increase in… | Record as |
|---|---|
| Assets, expenses | **Debit** |
| Liabilities, capital, incomes | **Credit** |
Decreases go the other way.

**Traditional (golden) rules:**
- **Personal accounts** (people, firms, banks): debit the **receiver**, credit the **giver**.
- **Real accounts** (things: cash, stock, machines): debit **what comes in**, credit **what goes out**.
- **Nominal accounts** (expenses, incomes): debit **expenses and losses**, credit **incomes and gains**.

Entries are first written in the **journal** (book of original entry), then posted to the **ledger**.

**The ₹100 example: Riya's chai stall, in journal form**
| Transaction | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Starts with ₹50,000 of her own cash | Cash ₹50,000 | Capital ₹50,000 |
| Buys a stove for ₹8,000 cash | Stove ₹8,000 | Cash ₹8,000 |
| Buys tea leaves worth ₹5,000 on credit from Mondal Traders | Purchases ₹5,000 | Mondal Traders ₹5,000 |
| Pays ₹2,000 rent | Rent ₹2,000 | Cash ₹2,000 |
| Sells tea for ₹5,000 cash | Cash ₹5,000 | Sales ₹5,000 |

Every row has equal debits and credits. That's double entry.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr 2026)
In April 2026, after a Supreme Court order allowed the government to reassess them, the Department of
Telecommunications reportedly **cut Vodafone Idea's AGR (licence-fee) dues by about ₹23,649 crore** and
allowed staggered payments until FY2041. In double-entry terms, a **liability falling is a debit** to the
dues account; the matching credit goes to the other side of the entry (exactly which account is a matter
for the company and its auditors).
*Sources: Business Today, 30 Apr 2026; coverage of the Supreme Court order, 27 Oct 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What happens in the books when a company repays a ₹10 lakh loan?"*

**Strong answer:** "Two accounts change. The loan, a liability, falls, so we debit the loan account
₹10 lakh. The bank balance, an asset, falls too, so we credit bank ₹10 lakh. Debits equal credits, and
the accounting equation still balances: assets and liabilities both go down by the same amount."

**Common trap**
Thinking "credit" means money coming in, as on a bank statement. In the business's books, cash coming in is a **debit** to cash. A bank statement shows the bank's view, which is reversed.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* An increase in an expense is recorded as a… **Answer: Debit.**
2. *(Application)* The stall pays ₹10,000 to Mondal Traders, a creditor. What's the entry? **Answer:** **Debit Mondal Traders** (liability falls), **credit Cash** (asset falls), ₹10,000 each.
3. *(Mini case)* A business takes a ₹5 lakh bank loan, credited to its bank account. Which accounts are debited and credited?
   **Answer:** **Debit Bank** (asset rises), **credit Bank Loan** (liability rises), ₹5 lakh each.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 2 Unit 1, paras 1.1–1.9 (`93942bos-aps6170-ch2u1.pdf`)

## Card C15 · The Trial Balance, and the Errors It Can't Catch

**In one line**
A trial balance lists every ledger balance to check that total debits equal total credits, but agreement proves only arithmetic accuracy, not that the books are right.

**Explain it simply**
After posting to the ledger, all debit balances and credit balances are listed; the two totals should match.
**Errors it does NOT reveal** (the trial balance still agrees):
- **Complete omission:** the transaction isn't recorded at all.
- **Error of principle:** recorded against accounting principles, like buying an asset but debiting an expense.
- **Wrong account on the correct side** (an error of commission), or a wrong amount written in both columns.
- **Posting twice** to both sides.
- **Compensating errors:** two mistakes that cancel each other out.

**Errors it does reveal:** one-sided mistakes, like posting to the wrong side, wrong totals, or partial omission.
If the difference can't be found in time, it's parked in a temporary **suspense account**, which is cleared when the errors are rectified.

**The ₹100 example: the chai stall's year-end**
- Riya buys a **₹30,000 laptop** for the stall but books it as "office expenses". The trial balance still agrees, but profit is **understated by ₹30,000** and assets are missing a ₹30,000 laptop (error of principle).
- She posts a ₹500 rent payment to the rent account but forgets the cash side. The debits now exceed the credits by **₹500**, so the trial balance doesn't agree. Until she finds it, ₹500 sits in **suspense**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Mar–Apr 2025)
In 2025, **IndusInd Bank** found that internal derivative trades had been accounted for incorrectly,
recording "notional profits" that weren't real. An external review put the impact at **₹1,979 crore**,
about **2.27% of its net worth**. Such problems are errors of principle and judgement, the kind a trial
balance can never catch: the books can balance perfectly while being wrong.
*Sources: IndusInd Bank filing to NSE, 15 Apr 2025; Outlook Business, Apr 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"If a company's trial balance agrees, can we be sure its accounts are correct?"*

**Strong answer:** "No. Agreement only proves that debits equal credits. Errors of principle, complete
omissions, compensating errors and postings to the wrong account on the correct side all leave the trial
balance agreeing. That's why we still need reconciliations, internal controls and audits."

**Common trap**
Treating the suspense account as permanent. It's a **temporary** holding account, and must be cleared once the errors are found.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which error does NOT affect the agreement of the trial balance? **Answer: An error of principle** (e.g., an asset purchase debited to expenses).
2. *(Application)* The trial balance's debit side is ₹500 more than the credit side, and the error can't be found yet. What do you do? **Answer:** Put ₹500 on the credit side of a **suspense account** until the error is found and rectified.
3. *(Mini case)* A ₹2,000 credit sale to Mr Sen is left out of the books completely. Will the trial balance agree, and what's the effect?
   **Answer:** **It will agree** (complete omission). But sales and debtors are both **understated by ₹2,000**, and so is profit.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 2 Unit 3, paras 3.1–3.5; Unit 6, paras 6.2–6.5
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card C16 · Subsidiary Books, the Cash Book and Petty Cash

**In one line**
Instead of one giant journal, businesses keep separate day books for each common type of transaction, and a cash book that is both a journal and a ledger.

**Explain it simply**
The usual **subsidiary books** (books of original entry):
1. **Cash book:** all cash and bank receipts and payments
2. **Purchases book:** **credit** purchases of goods dealt in
3. **Purchase returns book**
4. **Sales book:** **credit** sales of goods dealt in
5. **Sales returns book**
6. **Bills receivable book**
7. **Bills payable book**
8. **Journal proper:** everything else (opening entries, adjustments, rectifications)

They allow division of work, specialisation and easier checking.
The **cash book** comes as simple (cash only), **two-column** (cash + discount) or **three-column** (cash, bank and discount).
**Petty cash book (imprest system):** a petty cashier gets a fixed float for small payments, and is reimbursed exactly what was spent at the end of each period.

**The ₹100 example: the canteen's petty cash**
The petty cashier starts each week with an imprest of **₹2,000**. This week she spends ₹300 on autos, ₹850 on cleaning and ₹500 on stationery, **₹1,650** in all. On Saturday the main cashier hands her **₹1,650**, bringing the float back to ₹2,000. Small spending is controlled without clogging the main cash book.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2026)
UPI handled about **24.07 billion transactions worth ₹29.37 lakh crore in September 2026**, and for the
first time averaged more than **800 million payments a day**. For a shop, most "cash" sales now arrive in
the **bank column** of the cash book, not the till. Small cash payments (autos, tips, repairs) still need
a petty cash system.
*Sources: MediaNama and Entrackr, Oct 2026 (NPCI data).*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why do businesses keep subsidiary books instead of recording everything in one journal?"*

**Strong answer:** "Volume and control. Most transactions are repetitive: cash, credit purchases and
credit sales. Grouping them into separate books lets different staff work in parallel, makes each person
efficient at one book, makes totals easy to post to the ledger, and makes errors and fraud easier to
spot. The journal proper is kept only for unusual entries."

**Common trap**
Recording a **cash** purchase in the purchases book. The purchases book is only for **credit** purchases of goods; cash purchases go in the cash book.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* A credit purchase of goods for resale is recorded in the… **Answer: Purchases book.**
2. *(Application)* Where does a cash sale of goods go? **Answer: The cash book** (not the sales book, which records only credit sales).
3. *(Mini case)* A petty cashier holds an imprest of ₹5,000 and spends ₹4,200 during the month. How much does the main cashier give her at month-end?
   **Answer: ₹4,200**, exactly what was spent, restoring the float to ₹5,000.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 2 Unit 4, paras 4.1–4.6; Unit 5, paras 5.1–5.4
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card C17 · Accounting for GST: Input Tax Credit

**In one line**
Under GST, tax paid on purchases (input tax) is set off against tax collected on sales (output tax), so each business pays tax only on the value it adds.

**Explain it simply**
- GST is levied on **supply** of goods and services. It's a **destination-based** consumption tax, and the final consumer bears it.
- **Intra-state** supply: **CGST** (to the Centre) **+ SGST** (to the State). **Inter-state** supply: **IGST**.
- **Input tax credit (ITC):** GST paid on purchases is recorded as an **asset (a credit)**, not added to the cost of goods. It's then set off against GST charged on sales (a liability).
- This removes the old "tax on tax" (cascading) problem.

**The ₹100 example: a Kolkata stationery trader (18% GST, within West Bengal)**
- Buys stock worth **₹1,00,000 + ₹18,000 GST** (₹9,000 CGST + ₹9,000 SGST). Cost of stock: **₹1,00,000**; the ₹18,000 is input tax credit.
- Sells it for **₹1,50,000 + ₹27,000 GST**.
- Pays the government only **₹27,000 − ₹18,000 = ₹9,000**, which is exactly 18% of the **₹50,000 value added**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2026)
India's **gross GST collections were about ₹2.04 lakh crore in September 2026, up 14.7%** from a year
earlier. GST on imports jumped about 26% to roughly ₹65,500 crore, and refunds (largely unused input tax
credit returned to exporters and others) came to about **₹27,000 crore**, leaving net collections near
₹1.76 lakh crore.
*Sources: CAclubindia and A2Z Taxcorp summaries of official GST data, Oct 2026.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why isn't GST paid on purchases counted as part of the cost of inventory?"*

**Strong answer:** "Because the business gets it back as input tax credit. It's set off against the GST
it collects on sales, so it's really an asset, a claim against the government, not a cost. Adding it to
inventory would overstate costs and double-count the tax. Only when credit isn't available, for example
on blocked items, does GST become part of cost."

**Common trap**
Thinking businesses "bear" GST. They collect it and pass the credit along the chain; the **final consumer** bears the tax.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* A supply from West Bengal to Odisha attracts which GST? **Answer: IGST** (inter-state).
2. *(Application)* Input GST paid is ₹18,000 and output GST charged is ₹27,000. How much GST does the business pay the government? **Answer: ₹9,000.**
3. *(Mini case)* A company gets a GST demand because it claimed input tax credit on invoices its suppliers never reported. What should it fix?
   **Answer:** Its **ITC controls**: claim credit only on valid invoices that suppliers have actually reported, and reconcile the purchases register with supplier filings every month, before filing returns.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 2 Unit 1, para 1.11 (Accounting for GST)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card C18 · Capital vs Revenue: Expenditure and Receipts

**In one line**
Spending that gives benefits beyond this year is capital (it goes to the balance sheet); spending used up within the year is revenue (it goes to the P&L).

**Explain it simply**
- **Capital expenditure:** acquires or improves assets for lasting benefit, or increases capacity or cuts costs (machines, buildings, licence fees, legal fees to buy property). It's shown as an asset and charged to profit gradually through depreciation.
- **Revenue expenditure:** maintains earning capacity within the period (salaries, rent, ordinary repairs, replacing worn-out parts).
- **How to tell them apart:** the nature of the business (furniture is stock for a furniture dealer), whether it recurs, its purpose (maintain vs improve), its effect on earning capacity, and materiality.
- **Receipts:** **revenue receipts** come from normal operations (sales, interest) and go to the P&L. **Capital receipts** (sale of fixed assets, loans, owner's capital, share premium) don't, except for any **profit or loss** on selling an asset.

**The ₹100 example: the college canteen**
- New **₹60,000 oven** → capital.
- **₹1,200** monthly gas-burner servicing → revenue.
- **₹15,000** to rebuild the burners for 30% more output → capital (it increases capacity).
- Sells an old fridge with book value **₹8,000** for **₹10,000** → the ₹10,000 is a capital receipt; only the **₹2,000 profit** goes to the P&L.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Feb 2026)
In the **Union Budget 2026–27**, the Centre budgeted **capital expenditure of about ₹12.22 lakh crore**
(roads, railways, defence assets), about 9% more than the previous year's budget. Including grants to
states for building assets, "effective capex" is about **₹17.15 lakh crore**, out of total spending of
about **₹53.47 lakh crore**. The rest is largely revenue spending: salaries, interest, subsidies.
*Sources: PIB; Axis Bank Budget 2026–27 highlights; ORF, Feb 2026.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why does it matter whether an expense is capital or revenue?"*

**Strong answer:** "Because it changes this year's profit. Revenue expenditure hits the P&L immediately,
while capital expenditure sits on the balance sheet and is depreciated over its life. Wrongly capitalising
routine costs inflates profit and assets. Wrongly expensing an asset understates both. Investors watch
this, and it's a classic way profits get manipulated."

**Common trap**
Treating the whole sale price of an old asset as income. Only the **profit or loss** on the sale goes to the P&L; the rest is a capital receipt.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Ordinary repairs to keep a machine running are… **Answer: Revenue expenditure.**
2. *(Application)* Legal fees paid to buy a plot of land are… **Answer: Capital expenditure** (part of the land's cost).
3. *(Mini case)* A ₹2,00,000 fixed deposit matures and the bank also pays ₹14,000 interest. How is each amount treated?
   **Answer:** The **₹2,00,000 is a capital receipt** (return of the investment). The **₹14,000 interest is a revenue receipt**, credited to the P&L.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 1 Unit 3, paras 3.1–3.4 (`93937bos-aps6170-ch1u3.pdf`)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card C19 · Contingent Liabilities, Provisions and Contingent Assets

**In one line**
A possible obligation that depends on an uncertain future event is disclosed in the notes; a probable, measurable one is provided for; a possible gain isn't recorded at all.

**Explain it simply**
- **Contingent liability:** a *possible* obligation from past events, confirmed only by uncertain future events, **or** a present obligation where an outflow isn't probable or can't be reliably measured. Examples: claims not acknowledged as debts, guarantees for others, disputed statutory dues. It is **disclosed in the notes**, not recorded in the balance sheet (unless the chance of paying is remote, in which case it isn't even disclosed).
- **Provision:** a **present** obligation where an outflow is **probable** and can be **reliably estimated**. It is **recognised** in the accounts.
- **Contingent asset:** a possible inflow, such as a claim you're pursuing in court. It is **not recognised** (prudence). It's recognised only when it becomes virtually certain.

**The ₹100 example: Riya's chai stall**
- A customer sues for **₹50,000**, claiming food poisoning. Her lawyer thinks she will probably win → **contingent liability**, disclosed in a note.
- If the lawyer says she will probably lose, and a ₹20,000 settlement is likely → record a **₹20,000 provision**.
- She is suing a supplier for **₹10,000** over spoilt milk → **contingent asset**: not recorded until it's virtually certain.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2026)
On 10 September 2026, an appeals commissioner upheld a GST demand of about **₹3,365 crore** against
**HDFC Life** for 2017–2022: about ₹942 crore of tax and ₹2,423 crore of penalty, plus interest. The
insurer said it expects **no material adverse impact** and will appeal to the GST Appellate Tribunal.
While a demand is disputed, companies typically show it as a **contingent liability** in the notes rather
than as a definite debt.
*Sources: Angel One and StudyCafe, Sept 2026 (company filing to stock exchanges, 11 Sept 2026).*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the difference between a provision and a contingent liability?"*

**Strong answer:** "Both relate to obligations of uncertain timing or amount. A provision is a present
obligation where payment is probable and can be reliably estimated, so it's recorded and reduces profit.
A contingent liability is only possible, or not probable, or can't be measured, so it's disclosed in the
notes without hitting profit. As a case develops, a contingent liability can become a provision."

**Common trap**
Recording a contingent **asset** as income because you expect to win. Prudence says don't, until it's virtually certain.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* How is a contingent liability shown? **Answer: Disclosed in the notes to accounts**, not recorded in the balance sheet.
2. *(Application)* A company guarantees a bank loan taken by another company. What is the guarantee? **Answer: A contingent liability.**
3. *(Mini case)* A lawsuit that was "unlikely to succeed" now looks likely to cost about ₹10 lakh. What changes in the accounts?
   **Answer:** It becomes a **provision**: record ₹10 lakh as a liability and an expense, because the outflow is now **probable and measurable**.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 1 Unit 4, paras 4.1–4.4 (`93938bos-aps6170-ch1u4.pdf`)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card C20 · Accounting Standards, Policies and Estimates

**In one line**
Standards set the rules everyone follows; policies are the specific methods a company chooses within them; estimates are the judgements inside those methods.

**Explain it simply**
- **Accounting Standards (AS):** issued through ICAI's **Accounting Standards Board** (formed in 1977). Companies not on Ind AS follow AS notified under the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules, 2021.
- **Ind AS:** IFRS-converged standards (converged, not adopted word for word). They apply to **listed companies**, NBFCs, and **unlisted companies with net worth of ₹250 crore or more**.
- **Accounting policies:** the specific principles and methods a company adopts, for example how it values inventories or investments. They're chosen using **prudence, substance over form and materiality**.
- **Change a policy only if** a statute or standard requires it, or the change gives a more appropriate presentation. The effect must be **quantified and disclosed**.
- **Estimates:** judgements such as useful life or doubtful debts. When estimates change, the change applies going forward.

**The ₹100 example: the stationery shop**
- Its policy is weighted-average stock valuation. Switching to FIFO when prices are rising would raise this year's profit from **₹96 to ₹112** (see Card C3). That's a **policy change**, and it's allowed only with a proper reason, quantified and disclosed.
- It decides its ₹8,000 shelves will last **8 years instead of 5** → yearly depreciation falls from ₹1,600 to ₹1,000. That's an **estimate change**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Mar–Apr 2026)
India's insurance regulator **IRDAI** approved a roadmap on 30 March 2026 moving insurers to **Ind AS**,
including **Ind AS 117 (Insurance Contracts)**, from **1 April 2026**. Insurers that weren't ready could
seek a one-year forbearance. It's a textbook case of a **change in accounting policy required by
regulation**, and it changes how insurers report profit from their policies.
*Sources: Grant Thornton India; S.R. Batliboi (EY), 2026.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Can a company change its accounting policy to show better profits?"*

**Strong answer:** "No. A policy can change only if a law or accounting standard requires it, or if the
new policy gives a more appropriate presentation. Even then, the company must quantify and disclose the
effect, so users can compare years. Changing policies to manage profit breaks consistency and misleads
investors."

**Common trap**
Mixing up a **policy** change (changing the method, e.g. FIFO → weighted average) with an **estimate** change (revising a judgement within the method, e.g. useful life).

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which companies must follow Ind AS? **Answer: Listed companies, NBFCs and unlisted companies with net worth of ₹250 crore or more.**
2. *(Application)* Which is a valid reason to change an accounting policy? **Answer:** It's **required by law or an accounting standard**, or it gives a **more appropriate presentation**.
3. *(Mini case)* A CFO wants to switch the inventory method every year, picking whichever gives higher profit. What's wrong?
   **Answer:** It breaks **consistency**: years can't be compared and profits are manipulated. Policy changes need a valid reason and must be quantified and disclosed.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 1 Unit 5, paras 5.1–5.3; Unit 7, paras 7.1–7.5
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card C21 · Measurement Bases: Historical Cost, Current Cost, Realisable Value and Present Value

**In one line**
The same asset can be valued four ways: what you paid, what it would cost now, what you could sell it for, and what its future cash is worth today.

**Explain it simply**
| Basis | Asset is valued at… | Commonly used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical cost** | What was paid to acquire it (including installation) | Long-term assets like machinery |
| **Current cost** | What it would cost to buy the same asset now | Replacement-cost thinking |
| **Realisable value** | What it would fetch in an orderly sale now | Inventories (lower of cost and NRV) |
| **Present value** | Today's value of the future net cash it will generate | Investments, loans |

Liabilities are measured the same way: proceeds received, current settlement amount, settlement value, or discounted future payments.

**The ₹100 example: the canteen's oven**
- Bought in 2022 for ₹40,000 + ₹5,000 installation → **historical cost ₹45,000**.
- The same oven new today costs **₹70,000** → current cost.
- Sold today, it would fetch **₹25,000** → realisable value.
- It will save ₹12,000 a year for 3 more years. At 10%, that's worth **about ₹29,840** today → present value.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept–Oct 2026)
Indian government bond yields rose sharply: the 10-year yield crossed 7% on 11 September and closed
September at **7.17%**, with the RBI then hiking rates in October. When yields rise, **bond prices fall**,
so banks that measure parts of their bond portfolios at **current market value** are expected to book
**mark-to-market treasury losses** for July–September, even though they haven't sold the bonds.
*Sources: Business Standard, 4 Oct 2026; Business Recorder (Reuters), Oct 2026.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why do accountants mostly use historical cost, if market value is more relevant?"*

**Strong answer:** "Historical cost is objective and verifiable: there's an invoice. Market values can
be subjective and volatile, especially for unique assets like a 30-year-old factory. But for items
traded in active markets, like bonds and listed shares, current values are more useful, so standards
use them there. And inventories are capped at realisable value for prudence. It's a trade-off between
reliability and relevance."

**Common trap**
Assuming the balance sheet shows what a company is "worth". Most assets are at historical cost less depreciation, not market value.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Inventories are measured at… **Answer: The lower of cost and net realisable value.**
2. *(Application)* A machine costs ₹7,00,000 plus ₹1,00,000 for installation. What's its historical cost? **Answer: ₹8,00,000.**
3. *(Mini case)* A bank bought a government bond for ₹100. After yields rose, its market price is ₹96. If the bank measures it at market value, what does it report?
   **Answer:** A **mark-to-market loss of ₹4**, even though it hasn't sold the bond. That's the cost of measuring at current value.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 1 Unit 6, paras 6.6–6.8 (`93940bos-aps6170-ch1u6.pdf`)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card C22 · Depreciation: Straight Line vs Written Down Value

**In one line**
Depreciation spreads an asset's cost, minus its residual value, over its useful life, matching the cost to the years that benefit from it.

**Explain it simply**
Under Schedule II of the Companies Act 2013, depreciation is the **systematic allocation of the depreciable amount** (cost − residual value) over the asset's **useful life**.
**Causes:** wear and tear, obsolescence (newer technology) and the passage of time.
- **Straight Line Method (SLM, fixed instalment):** the same amount every year = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. Simple.
- **Written Down Value (WDV, reducing balance):** a fixed **percentage** of the remaining book value each year. Higher depreciation early, lower later, which suits assets that lose value fast or need more repairs as they age.

**The ₹100 example: the canteen's ₹60,000 oven (residual ₹6,000, life 5 years)**
| Year | SLM charge | WDV charge at 20% | WDV book value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹10,800 | ₹12,000 | ₹48,000 |
| 2 | ₹10,800 | ₹9,600 | ₹38,400 |
| 3 | ₹10,800 | ₹7,680 | ₹30,720 |

SLM charges (60,000 − 6,000) ÷ 5 = ₹10,800 every year. WDV starts higher and falls each year.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jul–Aug 2026)
Telecom companies show how today's capex becomes tomorrow's depreciation. In April–June 2026,
**Jio Platforms'** profit of about **₹7,764 crore** was up about 9% on the year but **slipped from the
previous quarter**, reportedly weighed down by **higher depreciation and finance costs from its 5G
rollout**. **Bharti Airtel's** capital spending in the same quarter was reported up about 61%, which
will feed its depreciation for years.
*Sources: Entrepreneur India; Investor Feed, Q1 FY27 results coverage.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Depreciation is a non-cash expense. Why does it matter?"*

**Strong answer:** "Because the cash was spent when the asset was bought, and depreciation spreads that
cost over the years that use the asset. Without it, profit would be overstated in later years. It also
reduces taxable profit, which does save cash. And heavy depreciation signals heavy past investment:
telecom companies show this after 5G rollouts."

**Common trap**
Thinking depreciation is a fund of cash set aside to replace the asset. It's an **allocation of cost**, not a savings account.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Under the straight line method, the depreciation charge is… **Answer: The same amount every year.**
2. *(Application)* Cost ₹1,00,000, residual value ₹10,000, useful life 9 years. What's the annual SLM depreciation? **Answer: ₹10,000.**
3. *(Mini case)* A company's laptops lose most of their value in the first two years. Which method fits better, and why?
   **Answer:** **Written down value.** It charges more depreciation early, when the laptops actually lose most of their value (obsolescence).

**Source:** ICAI Ch 5, paras on meaning of depreciation, SLM and reducing balance methods (`93957bos-aps6170-ch5.pdf`)

## Card C23 · Bills of Exchange and Promissory Notes

**In one line**
A bill of exchange is a creditor's written **order** to a debtor to pay; a promissory note is the debtor's written **promise** to pay. Both turn credit sales into tradeable paper.

**Explain it simply**
| | Bill of exchange | Promissory note |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | An unconditional **order** to pay | An unconditional **promise** to pay |
| Made by | The **creditor** (drawer) | The **debtor** (maker) |
| Parties | 3: drawer, drawee (who accepts and pays), payee | 2: maker and payee |
| Acceptance | Needed from the drawee | Not needed |

- **Maturity:** a time bill gets **3 days of grace** after the due date.
- **Discounting:** the holder can encash a bill at a bank **before maturity**, receiving less than its face value (the bank's discount).
- **Dishonour:** if the drawee doesn't pay at maturity, the entry is reversed and **noting charges** are recovered from the drawee.

**The ₹100 example: a Burrabazar saree wholesaler**
- Sells sarees worth **₹1,00,000** to a Siliguri retailer and draws a **3-month bill dated 1 January**, which the retailer accepts.
- Due date 1 April + 3 days of grace = **maturity on 4 April**.
- Needing cash now, the wholesaler **discounts** it at a bank at 12% a year for 3 months, a discount of about **₹3,000**, and receives about **₹97,000** today.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jun 2026)
Today's version of bill discounting for small firms is **TReDS**, RBI-authorised online platforms where
MSMEs sell their invoices on big buyers to banks for early payment. Invoice discounting on TReDS grew from
about ₹40,000 crore in FY2021-22 to **₹3.47 lakh crore in FY2025-26**. From **30 June 2026**, the
government requires all central public sector enterprises to **settle MSME invoices through TReDS**.
*Sources: PIB press release, June 2026; Data Diaries analysis of TReDS data, 2026.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why would a small supplier accept a lower amount by discounting its bills or invoices?"*

**Strong answer:** "For liquidity. A supplier selling on 90-day credit still has to pay workers and buy
materials now. Discounting turns a receivable into cash today for a small cost, like interest. It also
shifts the collection wait to the bank. Platforms like TReDS make this cheaper for MSMEs, because the bank
is really relying on the big buyer's credit, not the small supplier's."

**Common trap**
Mixing up the parties. In a bill, the **creditor** draws it and the **debtor** accepts it. In a promissory note, the **debtor** makes it.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* How many parties does a bill of exchange generally have? **Answer: Three:** drawer, drawee and payee.
2. *(Application)* A bill dated 1 March is payable 2 months after date. When does it mature? **Answer: 4 May** (due 1 May + 3 days of grace).
3. *(Mini case)* A bill for ₹50,000 is dishonoured at maturity, and the bank charges ₹200 noting charges. What does the drawer do in its books?
   **Answer:** **Debit the drawee ₹50,200** (bill amount + noting charges) and reverse the bills receivable entry. The customer owes the full amount again, plus the charges.

**Source:** ICAI Ch 6, paras 1–8 (`93958bos-aps6170-ch6.pdf`)
