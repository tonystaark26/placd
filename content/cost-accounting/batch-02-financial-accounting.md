## Card C8 · What Accounting Is, Its Branches & Who Uses It

**In one line**
Accounting is the language of business: it records money events and turns them into reports that different people use to make decisions.

**Explain it simply**
**The process:** identify → measure → record → classify → summarise → analyse and interpret → communicate.
- **Book-keeping** is the clerical part: recording every transaction regularly.
- **Accounting** prepares and presents the accounts.
- **Accountancy** is the whole body of knowledge: the rules that explain *why* and *how*.

**Three branches:**
| Branch | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Financial accounting** | The whole organisation's performance and position, for everyone |
| **Cost accounting** | The cost of products, jobs and departments, and how to cut it |
| **Management accounting** | Financial + cost data used for planning, control and decisions |

**Users:** *internal* (managers, employees) and *external* (investors, lenders and creditors, suppliers, customers, tax authorities, regulators).

**The everyday example: your college fest**
The treasurer notes every ₹ spent and received: that's **book-keeping**. After the fest, she prepares a statement showing ₹4,00,000 raised and ₹3,60,000 spent, a ₹40,000 surplus: that's **accounting**. Now see who reads it:
- the **principal** (management) checks it was well run,
- **sponsors** (like investors) want to know their money was well used,
- the **sound vendor** (a creditor) wants to know it will be paid,
- the **college auditor** (like a regulator) checks it's true.

Same numbers, different questions.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Aug 2025)
India's new **Income-tax Act, 2025** received the President's assent on **21 August 2025** and
comes into force on **1 April 2026**, replacing the Income-tax Act of 1961. It cuts the law from
**819 sections to 536** without changing tax rates. Tax authorities are among the biggest
**external users** of accounting: every business's profit for tax starts from its books.
*Sources: All India Radio News, 22 Aug 2025; Taxmann; IBFD.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Who uses a company's financial statements, and what does each user look for?"*

**Strong answer:** "Internally, managers use them to plan and control, and employees look at job
security and bonuses. Externally, investors check profitability and growth, lenders check whether
the company can repay, suppliers check whether they'll be paid, tax authorities check taxable
profit, and regulators like SEBI check compliance. The same statements answer very different
questions, which is why they must be reliable and standardised."

**Common trap**
Using book-keeping and accounting as if they mean the same thing. Book-keeping is just the recording step; accounting goes on to summarise, interpret and communicate.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* The routine, clerical recording of transactions is called… **Answer: Book-keeping.**
2. *(Application)* The canteen wants to know what each plate of biryani really costs. Which branch of accounting helps? **Answer: Cost accounting.**
3. *(Mini case)* A bank is deciding whether to lend ₹50 lakh to a small firm. Which kind of user is the bank, and what will it focus on?
   **Answer:** An **external user (a lender/creditor)**. It will focus on the firm's ability to repay: cash flows, existing debt, assets available as security and steady profits.

## Card C9 · The Accounting Equation

**In one line**
Assets = Liabilities + Capital: everything a business owns is financed either by outsiders or by the owner, and every transaction keeps the two sides equal.

**Explain it simply**
- **Assets:** resources owned and controlled that give future benefits (cash, stock, machines, buildings).
- **Liabilities:** obligations to outsiders (loans, creditors, outstanding expenses).
- **Capital:** the owner's claim, which equals assets minus liabilities.

Every transaction has two sides (the **dual aspect**), so the equation always balances. It is the base of **double-entry** book-keeping, and the balance sheet is simply this equation written out in detail.
**Profit increases capital. Expenses, losses and drawings reduce it.**

**The everyday example: Riya's chai stall**
| Transaction | Assets | = Liabilities | + Capital |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starts with ₹50,000 of her own cash | 50,000 | 0 | 50,000 |
| Buys a stove for ₹8,000 cash (asset swap) | 50,000 | 0 | 50,000 |
| Buys tea leaves worth ₹5,000 on credit | 55,000 | 5,000 | 50,000 |
| Pays ₹2,000 rent (an expense) | 53,000 | 5,000 | 48,000 |
| Sells tea costing ₹3,000 for ₹5,000 cash | 55,000 | 5,000 | 50,000 |

After every step, the left side equals the right side.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr 2024)
In April 2024, **Vodafone Idea** raised about **₹18,000 crore** through a follow-on public
offer (FPO), India's largest ever. In equation terms: **cash (an asset) went up ₹18,000 crore,
and share capital went up by the same amount**. Before this, its net worth had been
**negative** (December 2023): its liabilities were larger than its assets, so the "capital" side
of the equation was below zero.
*Sources: Business Standard, 12 Apr 2024; PTI via The Week, 22 Apr 2024; Share.Market.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"A company buys a machine on credit. What happens to the accounting equation?"*

**Strong answer:** "Assets go up by the machine's cost, and liabilities go up by the same amount,
since we owe the supplier. Capital doesn't change, because no income or expense has happened yet.
The equation stays balanced. Later, depreciation on the machine will reduce both assets and capital."

**Common trap**
Thinking an expense paid in cash only reduces assets. It reduces **capital** too, which keeps the equation balanced.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which equation is the basis of double-entry book-keeping? **Answer: Assets = Liabilities + Capital.**
2. *(Application)* A firm has assets of ₹2,00,000 and liabilities of ₹80,000. What's the capital? **Answer: ₹1,20,000.**
3. *(Mini case)* A firm pays ₹5,000 salary in cash. Which parts of the equation change?
   **Answer:** **Assets fall ₹5,000 (cash), and capital falls ₹5,000** (an expense reduces the owner's claim). Liabilities don't change.

## Card C10 · Reading the Financial Statements

**In one line**
The balance sheet is a **photo** of what a business owns and owes on one day; the profit and loss statement is a **video** of how it performed over the year.

**Explain it simply**
Indian companies follow the **Schedule III** formats (Companies Act, 2013).
- **Balance sheet ("as at" a date):**
  - **Assets:** **non-current** (property, plant and equipment; intangibles; long-term investments) and **current** (inventories, trade receivables, cash).
  - **Equity and liabilities:** **equity** (share capital, retained earnings), then **non-current liabilities** (long-term borrowings) and **current liabilities** (trade payables, short-term borrowings).
- **Statement of profit and loss ("for the year ended"):** revenue from operations + other income − expenses (materials, employee benefits, finance costs, depreciation, others) = profit before tax − tax = **profit for the period**, then **earnings per share (EPS)**.

**Kinds of assets:**
- **Tangible:** you can touch them, like aircraft and machines.
- **Intangible:** software, patents, goodwill, franchises, licences.
- **Financial:** deposits, bonds, money market funds.

**The everyday example: Riya's chai stall after one year**
*Profit and loss for the year:*
- Revenue **₹6,00,000** − tea, milk and sugar **₹3,60,000** − rent and helper's wages **₹1,20,000** − depreciation on the ₹8,000 stove **₹1,600**
- = **profit ₹1,18,400** before tax

*Balance sheet at year end:*
- **Assets:** stove ₹6,400 (non-current: ₹8,000 − ₹1,600), plus stock ₹7,000 and cash ₹1,60,000 (current) = **₹1,73,400**
- **Liabilities and capital:** creditors ₹5,000 + capital ₹1,68,400 (her ₹50,000 + profit ₹1,18,400) = **₹1,73,400**

Both sides match: it's the accounting equation again.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (FY2025)
**Infosys** reported FY2025 revenue of **₹1,62,990 crore** (up 6.1%), an **operating margin of
21.1%** and **earnings per share of ₹64.50**. Each figure comes straight from the statement of
profit and loss: revenue at the top, operating profit after expenses, and EPS at the very bottom.
Analysts compare these lines quarter by quarter.
*Sources: Infosys FY25 results press release (via PR Newswire, 17 Apr 2025); Angel One; Simply Wall St.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the difference between a balance sheet and a profit and loss statement?"*

**Strong answer:** "The balance sheet shows the financial position at one point in time: what the
company owns, what it owes and the owners' equity. The P&L shows performance over a period:
revenue, expenses and profit. They connect: the year's profit flows into retained earnings on the
balance sheet. If I had to pick one for a quick health check, I'd read both, plus the cash flow statement."

**Common trap**
Thinking profit equals cash. A company can be profitable but short of cash, for example when customers pay late (high trade receivables).

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which statement shows the financial position on a particular date? **Answer: The balance sheet.**
2. *(Application)* Classify: a patent, a fixed deposit and a delivery van. **Answer:** Patent: **intangible**. Fixed deposit: **financial**. Van: **tangible**.
3. *(Mini case)* A company reports record profit, but its cash balance fell and trade receivables doubled. What's going on?
   **Answer:** It's **selling on credit and not collecting cash fast enough**. Profit is recorded when sales are made, but the cash is stuck with customers. Check the cash flow statement and customers' credit quality.

## Card C11 · Accounting Concepts I: Entity, Money Measurement, Going Concern, Dual Aspect & Periodicity

**In one line**
Accounting concepts are the basic assumptions every accountant follows, so that everyone's books are built on the same foundation.

**Explain it simply**
- **Business entity:** the business is separate from its owner. The owner's personal spending is **drawings**, not a business expense.
- **Money measurement:** only events measurable in money are recorded. A loyal team or a great reputation isn't recorded until it's bought.
- **Going concern:** the business is assumed to continue for the foreseeable future, so assets are spread over their useful life, not valued at fire-sale prices.
- **Dual aspect:** every transaction has a giving side and a receiving side. This is the backbone of double entry.
- **Periodicity (accounting period):** the business's life is split into periods (usually a year) so results can be measured and compared.

**The everyday example: Riya's chai stall again**
- She pays her **personal ₹500 phone bill** from the stall's cash → **drawings** (entity concept), not an expense.
- Her regulars love her chai, but that loyalty isn't recorded → **money measurement**.
- The ₹8,000 stove is spread over **5 years**, about ₹1,600 a year, because she plans to keep going → **going concern**.
- Buying the stove for cash: stove up, cash down → **dual aspect**.
- She checks her profit every **31 March** → **periodicity**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr 2025)
On **15 April 2025**, SEBI passed an interim order against **Gensol Engineering**. It said loans
of about **₹978 crore**, taken mainly from IREDA and PFC to buy electric vehicles for BluSmart, were
partly **diverted to promoter-linked entities**, including money used towards a luxury flat at DLF's
The Camellias in Gurugram. SEBI barred the promoters from the securities market. These were *prima
facie* findings, not a final ruling. Treating a company's money as the owner's own breaks the most
basic idea in accounting: the **business entity concept**.
*Sources: Outlook Business and Deccan Herald, Apr 2025; The Week (PTI), 12 May 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why can't a sole proprietor pay his family's school fees from the business account and call it an expense?"*

**Strong answer:** "Because of the business entity concept: the business and the owner are separate
in the books. Personal payments are drawings, which reduce the owner's capital. They aren't business
expenses, so they mustn't reduce business profit. Mixing them would overstate expenses, understate
profit and taxes, and mislead lenders. It's exactly the concern SEBI raised in the Gensol case."

**Common trap**
Thinking the entity concept applies only to companies. It applies to a sole proprietor too, even though the law sees owner and business as one.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* "The business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future." Which concept? **Answer: Going concern.**
2. *(Application)* The owner takes ₹10,000 of stock home for personal use. How is it treated? **Answer: Drawings**, which reduce capital (entity concept), not a business expense.
3. *(Mini case)* A start-up's founder wants to record the "value" of a newly hired star manager as a ₹10 lakh asset. Is that allowed?
   **Answer: No.** Under **money measurement** (and the cost concept), only transactions measured in money are recorded. The manager's talent wasn't purchased as an asset; the salary is an expense.

## Card C12 · Accounting Concepts II: Accrual, Cost, Matching & Revenue Recognition

**In one line**
Record income when it's **earned** and expenses when they're **incurred** (not when cash moves), record assets at **cost**, and match each period's expenses with its revenue.

**Explain it simply**
- **Accrual:** transactions are recorded on a "due" basis. Income is recorded when earned, expenses when incurred, whether or not cash has changed hands. This creates outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, accrued income and income received in advance.
- **Cost concept:** assets are recorded at what was paid for them, not at today's market value.
- **Matching:** this period's revenue is matched with the costs incurred to earn it, giving a true profit.
- **Revenue recognition (realisation):** revenue is recognised when the sale is made or the service is delivered, not when an order is placed or an advance arrives.

**The everyday example: the college fest's March accounts**
- A sponsor pays **₹50,000 in February** for the **March** fest → it counts as March revenue (earned in March). In February it's **income received in advance**, a liability.
- The **₹12,000 electricity bill** for the fest arrives in April → it's still a **March** expense (accrual and matching).
- The society bought a **laptop for ₹60,000**. A similar one now sells for ₹70,000, but it stays in the books at **₹60,000** (cost concept).

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2024 – Jan 2025)
Tickets for **Coldplay's** India concerts went on sale in **September 2024**, but the Mumbai and
Ahmedabad shows were held in **January 2025**. For a promoter, the cash came in months early, but
the revenue is **earned only when the concert happens**. Until then, the money is **income received
in advance**, owed to fans if the show is cancelled. That's revenue recognition and accrual in action.
*Sources: Coverage of the BookMyShow ticket sale, Sept 2024; concert reporting, Jan 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"A company receives ₹12 lakh in March for a one-year service contract starting 1 March. How much is revenue for the year ending 31 March?"*

**Strong answer:** "Only ₹1 lakh: one month of service has been delivered. The remaining ₹11 lakh is
income received in advance, a liability, because the company still owes 11 months of service. That's
the accrual and revenue recognition concepts. Cash received isn't the same as revenue earned."

**Common trap**
Treating cash received as revenue. Advances are a **liability** until the goods or service are delivered.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Assets are recorded at their purchase price, not their current market value. Which concept is this? **Answer: The cost concept.**
2. *(Application)* March's rent of ₹20,000 is paid on 5 April. In which year's accounts is it an expense, if the year ends 31 March? **Answer: The year ending 31 March** (accrual). It's shown as an outstanding expense.
3. *(Mini case)* A coaching institute collects ₹1,20,000 in January for a 12-month course starting 1 January, and its year ends 31 March. How much revenue should it record for the year?
   **Answer: ₹30,000** (3 months × ₹10,000). The remaining **₹90,000 is income received in advance**, a liability, because those classes haven't been delivered yet.

## Card C13 · Accounting Conventions: Consistency, Full Disclosure, Materiality & Conservatism

**In one line**
Conventions are the customs accountants follow when preparing statements: stay consistent, disclose what matters, ignore what's trivial, and be cautious about gains.

**Explain it simply**
- **Consistency:** once a method is chosen (say FIFO, or straight-line depreciation), keep using it so years can be compared.
- **Full disclosure:** reveal everything material, including notes on **contingent liabilities** like pending lawsuits.
- **Materiality:** small, insignificant items can be treated simply. A ₹50 stapler is expensed, not recorded as an asset.
- **Conservatism (prudence):** don't record expected gains, but do provide for expected losses. Stock is valued at the **lower of cost or net realisable value**, and doubtful debts get a provision.

**Concepts vs conventions:** concepts are basic assumptions set by accounting bodies and are uniform; conventions grew from practice and involve more judgement.

**The everyday example: the stationery shop's year-end**
- Calculators cost **₹20,000** but can now sell for only **₹15,000** → show **₹15,000** (conservatism).
- If they could sell for ₹25,000 → still show **₹20,000**: no unrealised gain is booked.
- A **₹50 stapler** goes straight to expenses (materiality).
- A customer is suing for **₹1,00,000**, outcome unknown → disclose it in the notes as a contingent liability (full disclosure).
- It has used FIFO every year → keeps using FIFO (consistency).

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Mar–Apr 2025)
In March 2025, **IndusInd Bank** disclosed problems in how it had accounted for internal derivative
trades. An external review in April 2025 found that incorrect accounting had **recorded "notional
profits"** (gains that weren't real), with a negative impact of **₹1,979 crore**, about **2.27% of
its net worth**. It's the opposite of conservatism: recognising gains that hadn't truly been earned.
The bank had to disclose the issue and absorb the loss in FY25.
*Sources: IndusInd Bank filing to NSE, 15 Apr 2025; Outlook Business, Apr 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What is the conservatism convention, and isn't it unfair to recognise losses but not gains?"*

**Strong answer:** "Conservatism means anticipating losses but not gains: for example, valuing
inventory at the lower of cost or net realisable value and providing for doubtful debts. It looks
lopsided, but it protects users from over-optimistic statements. Over-stated profits mislead investors
and lenders far more than cautious ones. The IndusInd case shows the damage when gains are booked
before they're real."

**Common trap**
Taking conservatism too far and creating hidden reserves by under-valuing assets on purpose. Prudence means caution, not distortion.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Valuing stock at the lower of cost or net realisable value follows which convention? **Answer: Conservatism.**
2. *(Application)* A company switches its depreciation method every year to show smoother profits. Which convention does it break? **Answer: Consistency.**
3. *(Mini case)* A company faces a ₹50 crore lawsuit that it may or may not lose. Its CFO wants to leave it out of the annual report. What do you advise?
   **Answer:** **Disclose it as a contingent liability** in the notes to the accounts (full disclosure). If a loss becomes probable and can be estimated, provide for it (conservatism).
