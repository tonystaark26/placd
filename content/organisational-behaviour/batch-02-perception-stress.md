## Card O9 · Perception: How We Make Sense of the World

**In one line**
Perception is how we select, organise and interpret what our senses pick up. We react to *our version* of reality, not reality itself.

**Explain it simply**
**The process:** stimuli → we **select** (pay attention to some things) → **organise** (group them into patterns) → **interpret** (give them meaning) → respond.
Three sets of factors shape what we see:
- **The perceiver:** attitudes, motives, interests, past experience and expectations.
- **The target:** size, intensity, novelty, motion, background, and how close or similar it is to other things.
- **The situation:** time, work setting and social setting.

The ambiguous pictures in the slides, such as the vase that is also two faces, show this. Same picture, different perceptions.

**The ₹100 example**
A new café in Gariahat charges **₹100** for a cutting chai.
- A Salt Lake IT worker thinks: "Fair, it's an *experience*."
- A retired professor thinks: "Daylight robbery, I pay ₹10 at the corner."
- A student on a date thinks: "Worth it to impress."

Same chai, same ₹100. The perceivers' experience, motives and situation create three different realities.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Oct 2024)
In **October 2024**, the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) sent **Ola Electric** a show-cause notice. It was based on **10,644 complaints** to the National Consumer Helpline in a year, mostly about service delays.
Around the same time, comedian Kunal Kamra and founder Bhavish Aggarwal had a public spat online about service quality.
Ola said it had resolved **99.1%** of the complaints. But for many buyers, *perception* had already formed from their own past experience: "service is poor."
Once formed, a perception filters what people notice next.
*Sources: Business Today, The News Minute, MediaNama, Oct 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why do two managers rate the same employee so differently?"*

**Strong answer:** "Because perception is shaped by the perceiver, not just the target.
One manager may value speed and notice the employee's quick turnaround. Another may value polish and notice the typos. Their past experiences and the situations they've seen the employee in also differ.
That's why good appraisal systems use clear criteria, several raters and documented examples. They make the rating about the work, not the rater's lens."

**Common trap**
Thinking perception is just "seeing". It is an *active* process of choosing, organising and interpreting. That's why it can be managed, through framing, first impressions and experience.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What are the three stages in the middle of the perception process? **Answer: Selecting, organising and interpreting.**
2. *(Application)* A bright red "50% OFF" sign stands out in a row of plain shop boards. Which target factors make it noticed? **Answer: Intensity, size and novelty, plus contrast with the background.**
3. *(Mini case)* A new HR policy allowing two work-from-home days is welcomed by young staff, but older managers see it as "slacking". Using perception factors, explain why.
   **Answer: Perceiver factors.** The two groups have different past experiences, attitudes and expectations about work. Managers trained in "presence means productivity" interpret the same policy differently. Sharing data on output would help align their perceptions.

**Source:** `Perception.pptx` (definition, factors, process); Robbins & Judge 15e, ch. 6

## Card O10 · Attribution Theory: Who Gets the Blame?

**In one line**
When we see someone's behaviour, we judge whether it was caused by *them* (internal) or by the *situation* (external), using three clues.

**Explain it simply**
Harold Kelley's three clues:
| Clue | Question | If HIGH, points to |
|---|---|---|
| **Distinctiveness** | Do they behave this way only in this situation? | External |
| **Consensus** | Do others behave the same way in the same situation? | External |
| **Consistency** | Do they behave this way every time? | Internal |

Two common biases:
- **Fundamental attribution error:** we blame *other people's* failures on their personality and ignore the situation.
- **Self-serving bias:** we credit *our own* successes to our talent, and blame our failures on bad luck.

**The ₹100 example**
Sumit is late to class, so the professor fines him **₹100**.
- Is he late only on rainy days (high **distinctiveness**)?
- Is the whole class late, because the metro broke down (high **consensus**)?
- Is he late every Monday (high **consistency**)?

If the whole class was late because the metro broke down, it's external, and the fine is unfair.
If only Sumit is late, every week, it's internal.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov 2024)
On **3 November 2024**, New Zealand beat India by 25 runs in Mumbai to complete a **3-0 Test series sweep**. It was India's first home whitewash in a series of three or more Tests. It also ended a run of **18 home series wins in a row** since 2012.
The debate that followed was pure attribution theory:
- **External:** spin-friendly pitches and a brilliant Ajaz Patel (11 wickets in the match).
- **Internal:** India's batters' technique against spin.

Low **distinctiveness** (struggles against spin had shown up before) pointed many experts towards internal causes.
*Sources: ESPNcricinfo, Al Jazeera, Wisden, Nov 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Your team member missed a deadline. How do you decide what to do?"*

**Strong answer:** "First, I'd avoid the fundamental attribution error of assuming they're careless. I'd check three things:
- Did others on similar tasks also miss? That's consensus.
- Is this unusual for this person? That's distinctiveness.
- Has it happened before? That's consistency.

If it's a one-off and others struggled too, the cause is probably external, like unclear scope or a tool failure, and I fix the system. If it's a repeated pattern only with them, it's a coaching conversation."

**Common trap**
Mixing up the clues. High **consistency** points *internal*, but high **distinctiveness** and high **consensus** point *external*. Students often say all three point the same way.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is the fundamental attribution error? **Answer: Blaming others' behaviour on their personality** while underestimating the effect of the situation.
2. *(Application)* A salesperson says "I hit target because I'm brilliant; I missed last quarter because the market was bad." Which bias is this? **Answer: Self-serving bias.**
3. *(Mini case)* All five branch managers in Bihar missed their loan targets this quarter. Their regional head wants to replace them all. Apply attribution theory.
   **Answer: High consensus points to an external cause.** Everyone in the same situation failed, so check the region's economy, product fit or the targets themselves before blaming individual managers.

**Source:** Robbins & Judge 15e, ch. 6 (attribution theory); builds on `Perception.pptx`

## Card O11 · Perception Shortcuts: Halo, Stereotypes and More

**In one line**
To save effort, we judge people with mental shortcuts. They're fast, but they often lead us wrong at work.

**Explain it simply**
- **Selective perception:** we notice what fits our interests and background, and miss the rest.
- **Halo effect:** one good trait colours our whole view of a person, such as "great speaker, so must be great at everything". A bad trait does the reverse.
- **Contrast effect:** we judge someone against whoever came just before them, not against a fixed standard.
- **Stereotyping:** judging a person by the group they belong to.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy (Pygmalion effect):** expectations change how we treat people, and that makes the expectation come true.

These shortcuts matter most in **interviews, appraisals and investment decisions**.

**The ₹100 example**
At a Kolkata college fest, three students pitch to sell **₹100** tickets.
- The first is so polished that the second looks weak next to him, even though she's good. That's the **contrast effect**.
- The third went to a famous school, so the judges assume he'll sell the most. That's the **halo effect**.

At the end of the day the second student sold the most tickets, and the third sold the fewest.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr 2025)
On **15 April 2025**, SEBI passed an interim order against **Gensol Engineering's** promoters, the Jaggi brothers, and barred them from the securities market.
- Gensol had raised about **₹978 crore** in loans from IREDA and PFC to buy EVs for its sister company **BluSmart**.
- SEBI's forensic analysis found about **₹262 crore** unaccounted for. It alleged that some was diverted for personal uses such as a luxury flat in Gurugram.

BluSmart soon shut down. A "green, fast-growing EV startup" image can create a **halo** that makes outsiders ask fewer hard questions.
*Sources: SEBI order as reported by Value Research, Moneylife, Entrackr, Apr 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"How would you make your hiring decisions fairer?"*

**Strong answer:** "I'd design against the shortcuts:
- **Structured interviews**, with the same questions for everyone and scoring against a rubric, so the halo effect and contrast effects matter less.
- **Several interviewers** who score independently before discussing.
- **Work samples or case tests**, so we judge the work, not the college name or accent, which reduces stereotyping.

It's also important to tell interviewers about these biases, so they catch themselves using them."

**Common trap**
Thinking only "bad" people use stereotypes. Everyone uses shortcuts. The skill is designing processes such as rubrics and blind reviews that limit their damage.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is the halo effect? **Answer: Forming a general impression of someone from a single trait**, such as appearance, a famous college or good speaking.
2. *(Application)* An average candidate seems excellent because the two before her were poor. Which shortcut is this? **Answer: The contrast effect.**
3. *(Mini case)* A manager told that a new trainee is "a topper" gives him the best projects and extra mentoring, and the trainee indeed shines. Another equally capable trainee gets routine work and stays average. Name the effect and its risk.
   **Answer: Self-fulfilling prophecy (Pygmalion effect).** The manager's expectations shaped the opportunities. The risk is that talent is wasted and treatment becomes unfair. The fix is to give everyone similar stretch work and judge by results.

**Source:** Robbins & Judge 15e, ch. 6 (shortcuts in judging others); builds on `Perception.pptx`

## Card O12 · Stress: Eustress vs Distress

**In one line**
Stress is the body's response when demands feel bigger than our resources. A little sharpens performance (eustress); too much breaks it (distress).

**Explain it simply**
NIOSH defines job stress as the harmful physical and emotional reaction that happens when a job's demands don't match a worker's abilities, resources or needs.
Stress isn't all bad. Think of an upside-down **U** curve:
- **Too little stress:** calm turns into boredom, and performance is low.
- **Moderate stress (eustress):** alert, energised, focused. Performance peaks.
- **Too much stress (distress):** anxiety, mistakes, exhaustion. Performance falls.

The skill is staying near the top of the curve: enough challenge to stretch people, with enough support to avoid burnout.

**The ₹100 example**
A ₹100-a-head quiz night at a Park Street café, three teams:
- One team is told it doesn't matter: they're bored and score **4/20**.
- One is told the top team wins dinner: they're excited and score **16/20**. That's **eustress**.
- One is told losers pay for everyone: they panic and score **7/20**. That's **distress**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jan 2025)
The **Economic Survey 2024-25**, tabled on **31 January 2025**, said studies show health harms once working hours go beyond about **55–60 hours a week**.
- Citing Sapien Labs data, it said people sitting at a desk **12 or more hours a day** reported distressed mental well-being.
- It added that **workplace culture** matters as much as hours.

The Survey arrived during the "70-hour" and "90-hour week" debate. Its message was the inverted U: longer isn't always better.
*Sources: Business Today, ETV Bharat, The Federal, Jan 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"How do you handle pressure?"*

**Strong answer:** "I actually work well with *some* pressure. A deadline makes me focused. That's the good kind of stress.
The key for me is noticing when it tips into the bad kind. When I'm making careless errors or losing sleep, I break the work into smaller pieces, cut what isn't essential, and ask for help early.
During our final exams plus a live consulting project, that's how I delivered both without burning out."

**Common trap**
Saying "I never feel stress". It sounds fake, and it misses the point: moderate stress *helps* performance. Show that you recognise both kinds and manage the tipping point.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is eustress? **Answer: Positive, moderate stress** that energises people and improves performance.
2. *(Application)* A team with no deadlines and no targets keeps underperforming. Where are they on the stress curve? **Answer: The low end**: too little challenge, so boredom and low performance.
3. *(Mini case)* A consulting firm asks analysts to work 80-hour weeks for months. Output rose in the first fortnight, but now errors and resignations are climbing. Explain with the stress curve.
   **Answer: They've crossed from eustress into distress.** The short push raised performance, but sustained overload sent them past the peak. Reset hours to a sustainable level, add support, and space out the crunch periods.

**Source:** `Work Stress GD TB.pptx` (NIOSH definition, eustress and distress curve)

## Card O13 · Where Stress Comes From and How It Shows

**In one line**
Stress comes from the environment, the organisation and personal life, and it shows up in the mind, the body and behaviour.

**Explain it simply**
**Sources (stressors):**
- **Environmental:** the economy, politics, technology, security fears.
- **Organisational:**
  - **task demands:** workload, deadlines, little control;
  - **role demands:** role conflict and ambiguity;
  - **interpersonal demands:** poor relationships, harassment, low support.
- **Personal:** family problems, money worries, personality.

**Symptoms:**
- **Psychological:** anxiety, irritability, low job satisfaction.
- **Physiological:** headaches, blood pressure, poor sleep.
- **Behavioural:** absenteeism, quitting, mistakes, eating or smoking more.

Stressors **add up**. A small issue on top of a big load can tip someone over.

**The ₹100 example**
Ankita, a first-year MBA student:
- skips the **₹100** canteen lunch to save money (**personal:** money);
- her group project has no clear leader (**role ambiguity**);
- three submissions fall due on one day (**task demands**).

By week three she has headaches, is snapping at friends and is missing classes: **physiological, psychological and behavioural** symptoms.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2024)
In **September 2024**, the mother of **Anna Sebastian Perayil**, a 26-year-old chartered accountant at EY in Pune, wrote to EY India chairman Rajiv Memani. Anna had died in July 2024, about four months into the job. Her mother's letter linked the death to work pressure, describing a heavy workload and late-night tasks.
- The **Labour Ministry** said it would look into the working environment.
- EY said it was committed to employee well-being.

The case set off a national discussion about workload and role demands in high-pressure professional firms.
*Sources: Business Today, The Week, Tribune, Outlook, Sept 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What signs would tell you a team member is stressed, and what would you do?"*

**Strong answer:** "I'd look for changes, not just levels:
- **behavioural:** someone punctual starts coming late, or makes unusual errors;
- **psychological:** irritability or withdrawal;
- **physical:** fatigue, falling sick often.

Then I'd have a private, non-judgemental conversation and look for the stressor. Is it task load, an unclear role, a conflict, or something personal? Then I'd fix what's in my control, like redistributing work or clarifying roles, and point them to support, like counselling, for the rest."

**Common trap**
Treating stress as only the individual's problem ("they should be tougher"). Many stressors, such as role ambiguity, overload and poor managers, are *organisational*, and only management can fix them.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Name the three categories of stress symptoms. **Answer: Psychological, physiological and behavioural.**
2. *(Application)* An employee reports to two bosses who give opposite instructions. What type of stressor is this? **Answer: Role conflict**, an organisational role demand.
3. *(Mini case)* After a merger, staff don't know their new reporting lines, and absenteeism has doubled. Identify the stressor and the symptom, and suggest one fix.
   **Answer: Role ambiguity (stressor), shown by absenteeism (behavioural symptom).** Fix: quickly publish clear role charts and reporting lines, and hold team meetings to answer questions.

**Source:** `Work Stress GD TB.pptx` (environmental, organisational and personal factors; symptoms)

## Card O14 · Coping with Stress: Individual and Organisational

**In one line**
Individuals manage stress through habits like exercise and time management. Organisations manage it by removing stressors and supporting people.

**Explain it simply**
**Individual strategies:**
- time management (prioritise, plan, say no);
- physical exercise;
- relaxation and meditation;
- **cognitive restructuring:** reframing "I'll fail" into "this is hard but doable";
- building social support.

**Organisational strategies:**
- better selection and placement;
- realistic, clear goals;
- job redesign that gives more control;
- involving employees in decisions;
- wellness programmes;
- counselling services;
- limits on overwork.

The best results come when **both** act together. A yoga class can't fix a toxic boss.

**The ₹100 example**
A hostel spends **₹100 per student per month** on a Sunday morning yoga session. That's an organisational wellness programme.
But it also fixes the real stressor: the Wi-Fi used to crash every night before deadlines.
Students who also use a simple weekly planner (individual time management) report the fewest all-nighters.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jun–Jul 2025)
In mid-**2025**, The Economic Times reported, and others picked up, that **Infosys** had begun sending alerts to employees who worked beyond **9 hours 15 minutes a day**, especially when working remotely.
The emails reportedly showed each person their hours and asked them to rest and recharge.
It drew notice because co-founder Narayana Murthy had earlier called for 70-hour weeks. Here was the company using an organisational coping tool: monitoring overwork and nudging people back to balance.
*Sources: HCAMag, People Matters, MIT Sloan ME, Onmanorama, Jun–Jul 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"If you were HR head, how would you reduce burnout in a fast-growing company?"*

**Strong answer:** "I'd work on two levels.
**Organisationally:**
- fix the biggest stressors first: unclear roles and unrealistic targets;
- set limits on after-hours work;
- train managers to spot early signs;
- offer confidential counselling.

**For individuals:** time-management and resilience workshops, and protected leave.
Then I'd track it, with pulse surveys, attrition and sick days, so we know it's working, not just looking good."

**Common trap**
Offering only individual fixes like meditation apps. Interviewers want to hear that the organisation removes stressors at the source.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is cognitive restructuring? **Answer: Changing negative thought patterns** into more realistic ones, such as "I'll fail" into "this is tough but I've handled tough before."
2. *(Application)* A company redesigns jobs so employees can choose how to complete their tasks. Which stressor does this target? **Answer: Low task control.** More control reduces stress.
3. *(Mini case)* A bank offers free gym memberships, but stress surveys don't improve. Staff say targets change every week. What's missing?
   **Answer: Organisational action on the real stressor.** Wellness perks help individuals, but changing targets create role ambiguity and overload. Set stable, realistic targets and involve staff in setting them.

**Source:** `Work Stress GD TB.pptx` (individual and organisational coping)
