## Card O15 · Groups vs Teams, and the Types of Groups

**In one line**
A group is people who share information. A team is people whose combined effort produces more than they could alone.

**Explain it simply**
| | Work group | Work team |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Share information | Collective performance |
| Synergy | Neutral or negative | Positive (1+1 > 2) |
| Accountability | Individual | Individual *and* mutual |
| Skills | Random, varied | Complementary |

**Types of groups:**
- **Formal:** set up by the organisation.
  - **Command group:** a boss and direct reports.
  - **Task group:** people brought together for a job.
- **Informal:** formed naturally.
  - **Interest group:** joined to pursue a shared cause, such as better pay.
  - **Friendship group:** people who share traits or like each other.

People join groups for **security, status, self-esteem, affiliation, power and goal achievement**. Social identity theory adds that groups also give us a sense of who we are.

**The everyday example**
Six friends each put **₹200** into a shared WhatsApp "food fund". That's an informal **friendship group**: they share information about deals.
For the college fest, five students with different skills form a stall team: a cook, a designer, a cashier, a marketer and a manager. They turn **₹500** of ingredients into **₹3,000** in sales.
That's a **team**: complementary skills, a shared goal and synergy.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept–Oct 2024)
From **9 September 2024**, about **1,450 workers** at **Samsung's plant near Chennai** went on strike for **37 days**. They wanted better pay and recognition of their new union, the Samsung India Workers Union.
An **interest group** of employees had formed outside the company's formal structure, around a shared goal. The Tamil Nadu government mediated, and the strike was called off in mid-October.
Later reports say the union was formally registered in **January 2026**.
*Sources: Deccan Herald, The Quint, Business & Human Rights Resource Centre, Oct 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the difference between a group and a team? Give an example of each from your life."*

**Strong answer:** "A group shares information and each person is judged on their own work, like our class WhatsApp group, which shares notes.
A team has a common goal, complementary skills and mutual accountability, so the result is more than the sum of parts. In our marketing case competition, I did the research, a teammate built the model and another designed the deck. We were judged together, and we won.
That's synergy."

**Common trap**
Calling every group a team. Putting people together doesn't create synergy. That needs a shared goal, complementary skills and mutual accountability.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is the key performance difference between a work group and a work team? **Answer: Synergy.** A team's combined effort is greater than the sum of individual efforts.
2. *(Application)* Employees from different departments join a "Save Our Canteen" campaign. What type of group is it? **Answer: An informal interest group.**
3. *(Mini case)* A company renames every department a "team", but nothing changes and people still work alone. What's missing?
   **Answer: The features of a real team**: a shared goal, complementary roles, mutual accountability and team-based rewards. Without these, it's still a group with a new name.

## Card O16 · How Groups Develop: Tuckman and Punctuated Equilibrium

**In one line**
Groups typically go through forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning. Teams with deadlines often change course sharply at the halfway point.

**Explain it simply**
**Tuckman's five stages:**
1. **Forming:** polite, unsure. "What are we here for?"
2. **Storming:** conflict over roles, leadership and ideas.
3. **Norming:** roles and rules settle, and closeness grows.
4. **Performing:** energy goes into the task.
5. **Adjourning:** the group wraps up; temporary groups end.

**Punctuated equilibrium (Gersick)**, for temporary groups with deadlines:
- the first meeting sets the direction;
- a long phase of inertia follows;
- at about the **midpoint**, there's a sudden burst of change;
- then a second phase, and a rush at the end.

Groups don't always move neatly. They can slide back to storming when something changes.

**The everyday example**
Five MBA students get a 4-week marketing project with a **₹500** prize voucher each.
- Week 1: polite small talk (**forming**).
- Week 1 ends with a fight over who presents (**storming**).
- Week 2: they agree roles (**norming**).
- They do little until the midpoint, when they panic and change their whole approach (**punctuated equilibrium**).
- They do their best work in the last two weeks (**performing**), then celebrate and disband (**adjourning**).

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov 2024)
On **12 November 2024**, **Vistara merged into Air India**, creating a group with about **300 aircraft** and over **30,000 staff**.
- Around **7,000 Vistara employees** were assessed and fitted into Air India roles.
- For a time, cabin-crew rosters stayed separate.
- Reports flagged differences in work culture and pilot retirement ages.

Merging two crews is Tuckman in real life. A new combined group must form and work through storming over roles, rules and culture before it can perform as one airline.
*Sources: Tata Group release; Business Standard, People Matters, Outlook Business, Nov 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Tell me about a time your team had conflict. How did it get resolved?"*

**Strong answer:** "In our consulting project, two of us disagreed sharply on the recommendation in the first week. That was the storming stage.
Instead of avoiding it, we set a rule: decisions backed by data, and each side gets 10 minutes to present. That norm settled the roles. By week three we were performing, and the client adopted our plan.
I learned that early conflict is normal. The job is to turn it into clear norms quickly."

**Common trap**
Thinking storming is a failure. It's a normal stage. Teams that skip open disagreement often hide problems that come back later.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* In which of Tuckman's stages do roles and rules become settled? **Answer: Norming.**
2. *(Application)* A 6-week task force does little for 3 weeks, then suddenly rethinks its whole approach. Which model explains this? **Answer: The punctuated-equilibrium model**: a midpoint transition after a phase of inertia.
3. *(Mini case)* A high-performing team gets a new manager and three new members, and arguments start again. What's happening, and what should the manager do?
   **Answer: The team has slipped back to forming and storming.** Membership changed, so it must re-form. Hold a reset meeting to clarify the goal, roles and norms, and let disagreements surface openly so the team can re-norm quickly.

## Card O17 · Group Properties: Roles, Norms, Status, Size and Cohesiveness

**In one line**
Five properties shape how any group behaves: roles, norms, status, size and cohesiveness.

**Explain it simply**
- **Roles:** the behaviour expected from a position. **Role conflict** happens when expectations clash.
- **Norms:** the group's unwritten rules, about performance, appearance, social life and resources. Breaking them can lead to **deviant behaviour** or punishment.
- **Status:** social rank. It can be **ascribed** (given, such as age), **achieved** (earned) or a **master** status that overrides the rest.
- **Size:** big groups bring more ideas, but also **social loafing**: individuals put in less effort when their contribution can't be seen.
- **Cohesiveness:** how strongly members are attracted to the group.

**The key insight:**
| | High performance norms | Low performance norms |
|---|---|---|
| **High cohesion** | Very high productivity | Very *low* productivity |
| **Low cohesion** | Moderate productivity | Low productivity |

A close-knit group that values slacking is the worst combination.

**The everyday example**
Eight hostel friends get a **₹200** pizza treat for every room that passes the hostel inspection.
- When all eight clean together, only three actually work: **social loafing**.
- When they split into pairs, each with its own room, every pair cleans. Each person's effort is now visible.
- When they make "nobody sleeps till rooms pass" a group rule, with **high cohesion** and a **high norm**, all four rooms pass every week.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov 2025)
On **2 November 2025**, the Indian women's cricket team beat South Africa by **52 runs** in Navi Mumbai to win their **first ODI World Cup**.
- Shafali Verma (87) and Deepti Sharma (fifty runs and five wickets) led the final.
- Captain **Harmanpreet Kaur** and coach **Amol Muzumdar** were credited with building the side.

India became only the fourth team to win the title. A tight, cohesive group with demanding performance norms is exactly the top-left box of the grid.
*Sources: DTNext, The Hitavada, Cricket.com, IANS, Nov 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"How would you prevent social loafing in a group project?"*

**Strong answer:** "Social loafing happens when individual effort is invisible, so I'd make it visible:
- break the work into clear individual pieces with named owners;
- keep the team small enough that everyone matters;
- do quick peer check-ins;
- assess both individual and team results.

I'd also build a norm early, like 'we don't present anything we haven't all reviewed', so the group itself keeps standards high."

**Common trap**
Assuming high cohesion is always good. Cohesion amplifies whatever the norms are. A close group with low standards will defend its low output together.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is social loafing? **Answer: The tendency to put in less effort when working in a group than alone**, especially when individual contributions can't be identified.
2. *(Application)* A senior employee who is a long-time favourite gets every comfortable assignment. Which status type is at work? **Answer: Ascribed status** (seniority) or master status, not achieved status.
3. *(Mini case)* A sales team is very close: they lunch together and defend each other. But they collectively agree to "never exceed target, or management will raise it." Diagnose it and suggest a fix.
   **Answer: High cohesion with low performance norms gives very low productivity.** Fix: change the norms by linking team rewards to beating target, ensuring targets aren't simply raised as punishment, and involving the team in setting goals.

## Card O18 · Group Decisions: Groupthink, Groupshift and Better Techniques

**In one line**
Groups can decide better than individuals, but they can also fall into groupthink (no one disagrees) or groupshift (they become more extreme together).

**Explain it simply**
**Groups vs individuals:**
- **Groups** bring more information, more ideas and more acceptance of the decision.
- **Individuals** are faster and have clearer accountability.

**Two group traps:**
- **Groupthink:** the pressure to agree silences doubts. Warning signs are rationalising warnings away, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship and an illusion that everyone agrees.
- **Groupshift (group polarisation):** after discussion, the group's view becomes more extreme than the members' starting views, either riskier or more cautious.

**Better techniques:**
- **Brainstorming:** generate ideas freely, with no criticism.
- **Nominal group technique:** members write ideas silently, present them one by one, discuss, then vote privately. It is great for avoiding domination by one loud voice.

**The everyday example**
A class committee has to decide how to spend the **₹300 per student** class fund.
The class rep says "Let's do an expensive trip!" and nobody wants to look cheap, so everyone agrees. That's **groupthink**. In discussion, the trip grows from Digha to Darjeeling to Goa. That's **groupshift** towards risk.
With the **nominal group technique**, each student writes an idea silently first, then everyone votes privately. The class picks a farewell dinner, which most actually wanted.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Feb 2024)
In **February 2024**, after a door plug blew off a Boeing 737 MAX 9 in flight in January, an **FAA-appointed expert panel** reported on Boeing's safety culture.
- It found employees **hesitant to raise safety concerns for fear of retaliation**.
- It found a **"disconnect"** between what senior management said about safety and what front-line staff experienced.
- It made **27 findings and 53 recommendations**.

When people stay silent to avoid conflict, an organisation loses the dissent that catches errors. That is the core danger of groupthink.
*Sources: FAA expert panel report as reported by ABC/6abc, AirlineGeeks, Feb–Apr 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"How would you avoid groupthink in your team?"*

**Strong answer:** "Groupthink grows when disagreeing feels unsafe, so I'd make dissent part of the process:
- the leader shares their view *last*;
- one person is assigned to be the devil's advocate;
- for big decisions, everyone writes their view before discussion, as in the nominal group technique;
- outside experts review the plan.

And I'd thank people publicly when they raise concerns, so speaking up becomes a norm."

**Common trap**
Thinking groupthink means a group that's "too friendly". It's about the *suppression of dissent*. It happens in tense, hierarchical groups too, often more so.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is groupshift? **Answer: The tendency for a group's position to become more extreme than members' initial positions** after discussion, either riskier or more cautious.
2. *(Application)* A team wants many ideas fast, with no criticism. Which technique fits? **Answer: Brainstorming.**
3. *(Mini case)* In board meetings, the founder speaks first and directors always agree. Two failed acquisitions later, nobody had raised the risks. Diagnose it and suggest two fixes.
   **Answer: Groupthink**: self-censorship and pressure to conform. Fixes: the founder speaks last; appoint a devil's advocate; collect written anonymous views before discussion (nominal group technique); bring in independent reviewers.

## Card O19 · Four Types of Teams

**In one line**
Teams come in four main types: problem-solving, self-managed, cross-functional and virtual. Each suits a different job.

**Explain it simply**
| Type | Who | Does what |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem-solving** | 5–12 people from the *same* department | Meet a few hours a week to suggest improvements (but can't act alone) |
| **Self-managed** | 10–15 people | Plan, schedule and control their own work; take on supervisor duties |
| **Cross-functional** | Same level, *different* departments | Combine expertise for complex projects |
| **Virtual** | Any of the above, spread out | Work mainly through technology across places and time zones |

Each type has a typical risk:
- **Cross-functional** teams take time to build trust across specialties.
- **Virtual** teams can lack social bonds and clear communication.
- **Self-managed** teams need mature members.

**The everyday example**
A Kolkata biryani chain:
- **Problem-solving:** six kitchen staff meet weekly to cut waste and save **₹250** per batch.
- **Self-managed:** a night-shift crew sets its own schedule.
- **Cross-functional:** chef, marketer and finance head design a new **₹149** combo.
- **Virtual:** the online-delivery team works with an app partner's Bengaluru staff over video calls.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jan 2025)
On **16 January 2025**, ISRO docked two satellites in orbit in its **SpaDeX** mission. India became the **fourth country** to do this, after the US, Russia and China.
- ISRO says the spacecraft were designed by the **U R Rao Satellite Centre** with support from **VSSC, LPSC, SAC, IISU and LEOS**: centres with different specialties.
- Earlier attempts were postponed to validate the system on the ground.

A mission like this needs a **cross-functional team**: specialists from several centres working as one.
*Sources: ISRO SpaDeX mission page; Gulf News, Deccan Herald, Jan 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What challenges would you expect leading a cross-functional team?"*

**Strong answer:** "Three challenges:
1. **Different languages:** finance talks margins, design talks experience.
2. **Divided loyalty:** members still report to their own department heads.
3. **Slow trust-building**, because they haven't worked together.

I'd handle these with a clear shared goal and success metric from day one, a short kick-off where each function explains its constraints, and agreed decision rules. I'd also get department heads to protect members' time for the project."

**Common trap**
Mixing up problem-solving and self-managed teams. Problem-solving teams only *suggest*; management decides. Self-managed teams actually *run* their own work.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which team type takes over many of a supervisor's duties? **Answer: Self-managed teams.**
2. *(Application)* A bank brings together people from IT, compliance, marketing and branches to launch a UPI feature. Which team type? **Answer: Cross-functional.**
3. *(Mini case)* A remote team spread across Kolkata, Pune and Singapore keeps missing handovers, and members feel like strangers. What type is it, and what would you fix?
   **Answer: A virtual team lacking social bonds and clear processes.** Fixes: regular video check-ins, clear handover templates and owners, overlapping working hours for key tasks, and an occasional in-person meet-up to build trust.

**Source:** `OB Group Dynamics Team Building.pptx` (four types of teams); `Work Stress GD TB.pptx`

## Card O20 · What Makes Teams Effective

**In one line**
Effective teams need the right context (resources, leadership, trust, rewards), the right composition (skills, roles, size), and the right processes (shared purpose, specific goals, belief in success).

**Explain it simply**
Robbins's **team effectiveness model** has three groups of factors.

**1. Context:**
- adequate resources;
- good leadership and structure;
- a climate of trust;
- evaluation and rewards that recognise *team* contribution.

**2. Composition:**
- abilities and personalities;
- clear role allocation;
- diversity;
- the right **size**. Smaller is usually better, often fewer than 10.
- members who actually *want* teamwork.

**3. Process:**
- a common purpose;
- **specific goals**;
- **team efficacy**: belief that the team can succeed;
- shared mental models;
- moderate *task* conflict, but little *relationship* conflict;
- low social loafing.

The class notes add **psychological safety**: people can speak up without fear. They also suggest celebrating wins.

**The everyday example**
Two college fest teams each get **₹300 per member** to run a stall.
- **Team A:** six friends with no roles and no target. They argue, and sell **₹1,500**.
- **Team B:** five members with clear roles (cook, cashier, two sellers, one social-media lead), a target of "₹5,000 by 8 pm", a captain who listens and a pizza promised if they hit it. They sell **₹6,200**.

Same money. Different context, composition and process.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jun 2024)
On **29 June 2024**, India beat South Africa by **7 runs** in Barbados to win the **T20 World Cup**, their first ICC trophy in 11 years. Both teams were unbeaten going into the final.
- Captain **Rohit Sharma** said the team had worked on giving players **role clarity**.
- Coach **Rahul Dravid**, in his last match, praised the team's fight.
- R. Ashwin praised Dravid for turning down a "#DoItForDravid" campaign, saying a team sport shouldn't build narratives around one person.

That covers role allocation (composition), leadership and trust (context) and common purpose (process).
*Sources: Al Jazeera, ESPNcricinfo, Gulf News, Jun–Jul 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What makes a team effective? Use an example."*

**Strong answer:** "I think of it in three layers.
- **Context:** does the team have resources, a leader people trust, and rewards for team results?
- **Composition:** the right skills, clear roles and a small enough size.
- **Process:** a shared purpose, specific goals and a belief that we can win.

India's 2024 T20 World Cup win shows it: clear roles for players, a captain and coach the team trusted, and a common purpose bigger than any individual. In my own case-competition team, setting a specific target and splitting roles early is what got us to the national finals."

**Common trap**
Saying "conflict is bad for teams". Moderate *task* conflict (debating ideas) improves decisions. It's *relationship* conflict (personal clashes) that hurts.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What are the three broad groups of factors in the team effectiveness model? **Answer: Context, composition and process.**
2. *(Application)* A talented team fails because members are rewarded only for individual targets. Which factor is broken? **Answer: Context**: the performance evaluation and reward system doesn't recognise team contribution.
3. *(Mini case)* A 14-person product team has bright members, but meetings drag, two people do most of the work, and no one is sure what "success" means. Diagnose it using the model.
   **Answer: Composition and process problems.** The team is too big (fewer than 10 is better), which invites social loafing. There is no specific goal or common purpose. Fix: split into smaller sub-teams with clear roles, set a measurable target, and track individual contributions.
