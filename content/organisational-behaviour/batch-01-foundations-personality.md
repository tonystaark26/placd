## Card O1 · What Organisational Behaviour Is

**In one line**
OB studies how people think, feel and act at work, so managers can understand, predict and shape that behaviour.

**Explain it simply**
Fred Luthans put it in three verbs: OB is about **understanding, predicting and controlling** human behaviour in organisations.
It works at **three levels**:
- **Individual:** personality, perception, motivation, stress.
- **Group:** teams, roles, norms, conflict, leadership.
- **Organisation:** structure, culture, change.

OB borrows from other subjects:
- **psychology** for the individual;
- **sociology** and **social psychology** for groups;
- **anthropology** for culture;
- **political science** for power.

It is **applied**: the aim is better performance and more satisfied people.

**The ₹100 example**
Two tea stalls stand side by side near College Street. Both charge **₹10** a cup and have the same kettle. One sells **200** cups a day, the other **80**.
The difference isn't the tea. In one stall, the helper is greeted, trusted and shares tips. In the other, he is shouted at and keeps leaving.
That gap of **120 cups (₹1,200 a day)** is OB.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2024)
On **16 September 2024**, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told corporate staff to return to the office **five days a week from 2 January 2025**. Since May 2023 they had come in three days a week.
He said he wanted Amazon to run like "the world's largest startup", and planned fewer layers of managers.
One decision touched all three OB levels:
- **individual:** commute, stress, motivation;
- **team:** how people collaborate;
- **organisation:** culture and structure.

*Sources: Amazon memo as reported by Sinclair/ABC News affiliates, Sept 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What is organisational behaviour, and why should a finance or marketing manager care?"*

**Strong answer:** "OB is the study of how individuals, groups and structures shape behaviour at work, used to improve performance and satisfaction. Every manager gets results *through* people.
A great pricing strategy fails if the sales team is demotivated. A budget fails if teams hide bad news.
OB gives me tools to read people, build teams and manage change. Those are the things that decide whether a plan actually gets done."

**Common trap**
Calling OB "common sense". Research often contradicts common sense: for example, a happy worker is not automatically a productive one. OB tests these beliefs systematically.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Name the three levels at which OB studies behaviour. **Answer: Individual, group and organisation.**
2. *(Application)* A company studies how its office culture differs between its Kolkata and Tokyo branches. Which discipline is OB borrowing from most? **Answer: Anthropology**, the study of cultures and how they shape behaviour.
3. *(Mini case)* A startup has good funding and a strong product, but 40% of new hires quit within six months. The founder says "it's a salary problem". What would an OB view add?
   **Answer: Look beyond pay to all three levels.** Individual: role fit and stress. Group: how managers and teams treat newcomers. Organisation: culture and onboarding. Exit interviews would show which level is driving the quits.

**Source:** `OB_Introduction_2026.pptx` slides 11–15; Robbins & Judge 15e, ch. 1

## Card O2 · Lewin's Equation: B = f(P, E)

**In one line**
Behaviour is a function of the Person and the Environment. To change what people do, change one or both.

**Explain it simply**
Kurt Lewin (1936) gave OB its simplest model:
- **B** is behaviour: what we can see someone do.
- **P** is the person: personality, skills, values, mood.
- **E** is the environment: the boss, team, rules, rewards, the physical setting.

The same person behaves differently in different settings. Different people behave differently in the same setting.
For a manager, **E** is often easier to change than **P**. You can redesign rewards, rules and teams far faster than you can change someone's personality.

**The ₹100 example**
Riya is quiet in a 60-student lecture at her Park Street college. She never speaks.
In a 5-person case-study group with a friendly senior, she speaks up and wins a **₹100** prize for the best idea.
Her **P** didn't change; the **E** did, from a big hall to a small, safe group. So did her **B**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr 2024)
In **April 2024**, TCS tied employees' **quarterly variable pay to office attendance**, according to an HR note reported by NDTV Profit and Business Today.
Below 60% attendance meant no variable pay. Full pay needed 85% or more.
TCS's HR head later said about **70%** of staff were back in office.
That is Lewin in action: TCS didn't try to change people's personalities (**P**). It changed the reward environment (**E**), and behaviour (**B**) followed.
*Sources: Business Today, NDTV Profit, Apr 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"If an employee is underperforming, is it the person or the situation?"*

**Strong answer:** "Usually both. Lewin said behaviour is a function of the person and the environment.
Before blaming the person, I'd check the environment:
- Are the targets clear?
- Do they have the tools?
- Is the manager supportive?
- Do the rewards match what we want?

If others in the same setting do well, the issue may be skills or fit. Then I'd coach or move them to a better-fitting role.
Fixing the environment is often faster and helps the whole team."

**Common trap**
Treating it as maths. You can't plug in numbers. It's a way of thinking that says don't explain behaviour by personality *alone*.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* In B = f(P, E), what does E stand for? **Answer: Environment**: the situation around the person, such as the boss, team, rules and rewards.
2. *(Application)* A bank cuts long queues by adding a token machine and seating, and staff complaints drop. Which part of Lewin's equation did it change? **Answer: E, the environment**, which changed staff behaviour without changing the staff.
3. *(Mini case)* A star salesperson becomes average after moving from Kolkata to the Delhi branch. Use Lewin's equation to suggest two causes.
   **Answer: Look at E and P together.** E: a different manager, territory or team support. P: stress from relocating, or a mismatch between their skills and the new market.

**Source:** `OB_Introduction_2026.pptx` slide 22

## Card O3 · The Five OB Models: Autocratic to System

**In one line**
Organisations run on different beliefs about people, from "obey because I'm the boss" to "we're partners who trust each other".

**Explain it simply**
| Model | Rests on | Employee feels | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autocratic** | Power | Obedience, fear | Minimum effort |
| **Custodial** | Money and benefits | Security, dependence | Passive cooperation |
| **Supportive** | Leadership | Recognition | Motivated effort |
| **Collegial** | Partnership | Responsibility | Enthusiasm, teamwork |
| **System** | Trust and community | Meaning, belonging | Passion, commitment |

Most firms mix models. The trend over time has been from top to bottom: from power towards trust.

**The ₹100 example**
Five sweet shops in Kolkata pay their helpers the same **₹100** for an evening shift:
- **Autocratic:** "Do it or you're out."
- **Custodial:** the owner adds free dinner.
- **Supportive:** the owner coaches the helper and thanks him.
- **Collegial:** the helper suggests a new sandesh flavour and the owner tries it.
- **System:** the helper feels the shop is *his* too, and stays late in the Durga Puja rush without being asked.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jan 2025)
In **January 2025**, a video of L&T chairman **S.N. Subrahmanyan** went viral. He said he wished he could make staff work on Sundays, and asked "how long can you stare at your wife?" He was talking about a **90-hour week**.
The backlash was swift:
- Bajaj Auto's **Rajiv Bajaj** said the quality of work matters, not the hours.
- **Harsh Goenka** joked about renaming Sunday "Sun-duty".

L&T said the remarks were about "nation-building", not a mandate. The debate was really autocratic thinking (output through pressure) against supportive and collegial thinking (output through engagement).
*Sources: People Matters, Gulf News, Storyboard18, Jan 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Which OB model would you use to manage a team, and why?"*

**Strong answer:** "It depends on the team. A new team in a crisis might need clear, firm direction for a short time. But for knowledge work I'd aim for the **supportive and collegial** models.
People who feel trusted and responsible bring ideas, not just hours. Fear-based control gets minimum effort and high attrition.
Custodial benefits help with security, but they don't create drive."

**Common trap**
Thinking custodial means caring. Benefits create *dependence and security*, not motivation. People stay but don't push themselves.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which OB model is based on economic resources such as pensions and benefits? **Answer: The custodial model.**
2. *(Application)* A startup gives every employee a say in product decisions and treats them as partners. Which model is this? **Answer: Collegial**, built on partnership and shared responsibility.
3. *(Mini case)* A factory pays well and offers housing, but workers do the bare minimum and never suggest improvements. Diagnose it.
   **Answer: A custodial model with no leadership support.** Benefits buy loyalty and security, but not effort. Moving towards supportive (recognition, coaching) and collegial (involving workers in decisions) would raise motivation.

**Source:** `OB_Introduction_2026.pptx` slide 21; OB models after Keith Davis

## Card O4 · Schools of Management Thought

**In one line**
Management thinking moved from "people are parts of a machine" (classical), to "people have feelings and needs" (neoclassical), to "it depends on the situation" (modern).

**Explain it simply**
**1. Classical (about 1880–1920): efficiency first.**
- **Scientific management (Taylor):** study each task, find the "one best way", pay by output.
- **Administrative (Fayol):** principles for managing the whole organisation, such as planning, organising and command.
- **Bureaucracy (Weber):** a clear hierarchy and written rules, not personal favours.

**2. Neoclassical (about 1920–1950): people first.**
- **Human relations:** satisfied workers work better, so communication, leadership and group behaviour matter.
- **Behavioural:** study what actually drives behaviour at work.

**3. Modern: it depends.**
- **Systems:** the organisation is a set of connected parts.
- **Contingency:** there is no single best way; fit the style to the situation.
- **Organisational humanism:** let people use all their abilities.
- **Management science:** use numbers and models.

**The ₹100 example**
A momo stall near New Market sells plates at **₹100**:
- **Taylor** times every step and finds rolling takes 40 seconds, so he redesigns the counter and output doubles.
- **Fayol** writes clear roles: who cooks, who serves, who handles cash.
- **Weber** fixes rules: no free plates for friends.
- **Human relations:** the owner notices staff work faster when he eats with them.
- **Contingency:** on Puja nights he hires two more people and simplifies the menu. On a rainy Tuesday he doesn't.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Dec 2024)
In **December 2024**, a US Senate committee led by **Bernie Sanders** released a report on Amazon's warehouses, "The Injury-Productivity Trade-off".
It said Amazon's internal studies linked **speed quotas** to injuries. It also said executives rejected safety staff's advice to ease the quotas.
Amazon called the report "fundamentally flawed". It said injury rates had fallen while speed rose.
This is the oldest debate in management: Taylor's focus on measured efficiency against the human relations view that worker wellbeing drives long-term performance.
*Sources: US Senate HELP Committee report; CNN, Boston Globe, Dec 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is Taylor's scientific management still relevant today?"*

**Strong answer:** "Very much. Delivery apps timing every order, call centres tracking handling time, and warehouse pick rates are all Taylor's ideas with software added.
But the classical schools ignored people's feelings and needs. That's why human relations followed.
Today, contingency thinking makes most sense to me. Measure efficiency where tasks are routine, but give autonomy where work needs judgement and creativity. And watch the human cost of pure speed targets."

**Common trap**
Thinking the newer schools replaced the old ones. They didn't. Modern firms use all of them together, in different parts of the business.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Who is linked with bureaucracy, the ideal of a hierarchy governed by written rules? **Answer: Max Weber.**
2. *(Application)* A manager says "there's no one best way; it depends on our market and technology". Which school is this? **Answer: Contingency theory.**
3. *(Mini case)* A call centre doubles productivity by scripting and timing every call, but attrition hits 60% a year. Which school drove the gain, and which school explains the problem?
   **Answer: Scientific management drove the gain; human relations explains the attrition.** Ignoring workers' needs for recognition and autonomy made them leave. The fix is to keep the efficient processes and add people-focused practices.

**Source:** `Schools of Thought.pdf` pages 3–19

## Card O5 · OB's Big Challenges Today

**In one line**
Managers today must handle diverse, global, often temporary workforces, while pushing quality, empowerment and constant innovation.

**Explain it simply**
The challenges (and opportunities) that OB helps with:
- **People skills:** managers succeed through others, so they need soft skills.
- **Quality and productivity:** for example, Total Quality Management, where everyone improves the process.
- **Workforce diversity:** different genders, ages, regions, languages and abilities.
- **Globalisation:** working across cultures and time zones.
- **Empowerment:** pushing decisions down to the people closest to the work.
- **Temporariness:** contracts, gig work and constant reorganisation.
- **Innovation and change:** adapt fast or fall behind.

**The ₹100 example**
A Salt Lake café's evening team:
- a Bengali manager;
- a Nepali chef;
- two part-time students from Odisha;
- a delivery rider from an app, paid about **₹100** per order run.

In one shift the manager handles diversity (three languages), temporariness (the rider changes every week) and empowerment (letting the chef change the menu).
That is today's OB in one small kitchen.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Feb 2025)
In the **Union Budget on 1 February 2025**, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced that gig and platform workers would get:
- **identity cards**;
- registration on the **e-Shram** portal;
- health cover under **PM-JAY**.

That covers workers who deliver or drive for platforms such as Zomato, Swiggy, Blinkit, Ola and Uber. The plan was expected to help about **1 crore** gig workers.
It is a policy answer to OB's "temporariness" challenge: people who work for a company without being its employees.
*Sources: Union Budget 2025-26 coverage, Outlook Money, Exchange4media, YourStory, Feb 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the biggest people challenge Indian companies face today?"*

**Strong answer:** "I'd say the shift to a temporary, hybrid and diverse workforce.
- A growing share of work is done by gig workers, contractors and remote teams who may never meet the manager.
- At the same time, firms need innovation, which needs trust and commitment.

So the challenge is building engagement with people who aren't permanent or present. Clear goals, fair treatment, recognition and inclusion become more important, not less."

**Common trap**
Listing challenges without the "opportunity" side. For example, diversity is a challenge to manage, but it is also a source of better ideas and wider markets.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What does "coping with temporariness" mean in OB? **Answer: Managing in constant change**: contract and gig workers, frequent reorganisation, and skills that go out of date fast.
2. *(Application)* A bank lets branch staff approve small loans without head-office sign-off. Which challenge is it responding to? **Answer: Empowering people.** Decisions are pushed to those closest to the customer.
3. *(Mini case)* A Kolkata IT firm wins its first Japanese client, and its project team now spans three cities and two cultures. Name two OB challenges and one response to each.
   **Answer: Globalisation and workforce diversity.** For globalisation: cross-cultural training on Japanese business norms. For diversity: clear communication norms and inclusive meetings, so no one city dominates.

**Source:** `OB_Introduction_2026.pptx` slide 9; Robbins & Judge 15e, ch. 1

## Card O6 · Personality: The Big Five (and the MBTI)

**In one line**
Personality is a person's stable pattern of thinking, feeling and acting. The Big Five is the best-supported way to describe it.

**Explain it simply**
The **Big Five** traits (remember **OCEAN**):
- **O: Openness.** Curious and creative, or conventional.
- **C: Conscientiousness.** Organised and dependable, or careless.
- **E: Extraversion.** Sociable and energetic, or reserved.
- **A: Agreeableness.** Warm and cooperative, or tough and critical.
- **N: Neuroticism.** Anxious, or emotionally stable.

**Conscientiousness** predicts job performance best, across almost all jobs.
Earlier models in the notes:
- **Jung:** introvert or extrovert.
- **Eysenck:** three factors, including introversion–extraversion and stability–neuroticism.
- **Cattell:** 16 traits.

The popular **MBTI** sorts people into 16 types using four pairs (E/I, S/N, T/F, J/P). It's useful for self-awareness, but research says it predicts job performance poorly.

**The ₹100 example**
Five friends each get **₹100** at a Gariahat fair:
- **High C:** writes a budget and keeps ₹30 back.
- **High O:** tries the strangest street food.
- **High E:** spends it treating everyone.
- **High A:** lends half to a friend who forgot his wallet.
- **High N:** worries the whole time about losing it.

Same money, five different stable patterns.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Oct 2024)
**Ratan Tata** died on **9 October 2024**, aged 86. The tributes kept naming the same traits:
- the Tata Group's statement and PM Modi stressed his **humility** and kindness: high **agreeableness**;
- Tata Sons chairman N. Chandrasekaran also praised his humility;
- others pointed to his ethical consistency, a sign of high **conscientiousness**, and to his bold bets, which show high **openness**.

Whatever one thinks of individual deals, the public remembered the *pattern*. That is what personality means.
*Sources: Deccan Herald, ETV Bharat, Asia News Network, Oct 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Describe your personality using the Big Five."*

**Strong answer:** "I'd say I'm high on **conscientiousness**. I plan my week and I meet deadlines, like running our fest budget of ₹2 lakh without overspending.
I'm moderately **extraverted**: comfortable presenting, but I recharge alone.
I'm high on **openness**, which is why I enjoy case competitions.
I'm working on **agreeableness** in tough negotiations, where I sometimes give in too fast."

**Common trap**
Treating MBTI types as scientific fact. Interviewers trained in OB know the Big Five has much stronger evidence. Use it, and back each trait with an example.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which Big Five trait best predicts job performance across jobs? **Answer: Conscientiousness.**
2. *(Application)* A role needs calm decisions under pressure, such as an emergency room or a trading desk. Which trait should be *low*? **Answer: Neuroticism.** You want high emotional stability.
3. *(Mini case)* A company hires sales staff purely by MBTI type ("only ENTJs") and finds results no better than before. Why, and what's better?
   **Answer: MBTI predicts performance poorly.** It puts people in boxes and is not strongly linked to results. Better: assess Big Five traits such as conscientiousness and extraversion, together with structured interviews and work samples.

**Source:** `Theories of Personality.pdf`; Robbins & Judge 15e, ch. 5

## Card O7 · Freud: Id, Ego, Superego and Defence Mechanisms

**In one line**
Freud saw personality as a tug-of-war. The id wants pleasure now, the superego says "be good", and the ego referees with reality.

**Explain it simply**
Freud's **iceberg**: most of the mind sits below the surface, in the unconscious.
- **Id:** instinct and impulse. "I want it now."
- **Superego:** conscience and rules learned from parents and society. "That's wrong."
- **Ego:** the realistic manager that balances the two.

When anxiety rises, the ego uses **defence mechanisms**, often without us knowing:
- **denial:** "it's not a problem";
- **rationalisation:** a good-sounding excuse;
- **projection:** blaming others for our own feelings;
- **displacement:** taking anger out on a safer target.

At work, these explain why people make excuses, blame others or ignore bad news.

**The ₹100 example**
Arjun has **₹100** left for the week.
- His **id** says "biryani from Arsalan, now!"
- His **superego** says "save it, your parents work hard for this."
- His **ego** decides: a ₹40 roll today and ₹60 saved.

He fails a test and says "the questions were out of syllabus anyway." That is **rationalisation**: an excuse that protects his self-image.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2024)
On **23 September 2024**, SEBI published a study of equity futures and options (F&O) trading. It found that **93% of over 1 crore individual traders lost money** between FY22 and FY24.
- The average loss was about **₹2 lakh** each, including costs.
- Total losses were above **₹1.8 lakh crore**.
- Yet more than **75% of loss-makers kept trading**.

Freud's ideas help explain why: the id's thrill of a quick win, and defences like "I was just unlucky" (rationalisation) or "next time will be different" (denial).
*Sources: SEBI press release, Sept 2024; Business Standard, Outlook Business.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Tell me about a failure. What did you learn?"*
> (This question quietly tests whether you use defence mechanisms.)

**Strong answer:** "In my second semester I lost a case competition because I hadn't checked our market-size numbers. My first instinct was to blame the judges, and I caught myself doing it.
I realised that was an excuse. So I went back, found my error, and now I build a quick sanity check into every analysis. In the next competition our numbers were the thing judges praised."

**Common trap**
Saying Freud is "outdated" and skipping him. Much of his theory isn't testable. But defence mechanisms are a very practical lens for feedback, failure and blame at work.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which part of Freud's model acts as our conscience? **Answer: The superego.**
2. *(Application)* A manager scolded by the boss goes back and snaps at a junior. Which defence mechanism is this? **Answer: Displacement.** Anger is moved to a safer target.
3. *(Mini case)* A project lead keeps saying "the delay is fine, clients always wait" while the deadline slips week after week. Which defence mechanisms are at work, and how should a manager respond?
   **Answer: Denial and rationalisation.** Respond with facts in a calm, safe conversation: show the timeline and the client impact. Focus on fixing the problem, not blaming the person, so their ego doesn't need to defend itself.

**Source:** `Theories of Personality.pdf` (Freud, iceberg model, defence mechanisms)

## Card O8 · Locus of Control and Self-Efficacy

**In one line**
Locus of control is whether you believe *you* or *outside forces* control your results. Self-efficacy is your belief that you can do a specific task.

**Explain it simply**
**Locus of control (Rotter):**
- **Internals** believe "my effort decides my results." They tend to perform better, take initiative and handle setbacks.
- **Externals** believe "luck, fate or other people decide." They're more likely to give up, which can slide into **learned helplessness**.

**Self-efficacy (Bandura):** confidence for a *particular* task: "I can crack this case interview." It grows from four sources:
1. **past success** (the strongest);
2. **watching similar people succeed**;
3. **encouragement**;
4. **managing stress and emotions**.

Robbins adds **core self-evaluation**: an overall belief in your own worth and ability.

**The ₹100 example**
Two students each lose **₹100** on a bad online order.
- **Priya (internal):** "I didn't check the seller rating. Next time I will." She checks, and never loses money that way again.
- **Rahul (external):** "These apps are all scams, nothing I can do." He keeps losing.

Same loss. The belief about *control* changed what happened next.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Dec 2024)
On **12 December 2024**, **D. Gukesh** beat Ding Liren in Singapore to become the **youngest world chess champion, at 18**.
After winning the Candidates, he asked for a mental trainer and brought in **Paddy Upton**. Upton had worked with India's 2011 World Cup cricket team.
Gukesh credited the work with helping him handle the "emotional pressure". That is self-efficacy built through two of Bandura's sources: mastery from past wins and managing emotional states.
*Sources: The Bridge, Onmanorama, DTNext, Dec 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What do you do when things go wrong despite your best efforts?"*

**Strong answer:** "I separate what I control from what I don't.
When our team's internship project lost its data source a week before the deadline, I didn't blame the vendor. I listed what we could still do: we found a public dataset, cut the scope and told the client early.
Focusing on what I can control keeps me moving. And every time it works, my confidence for the next problem grows."

**Common trap**
Confusing self-efficacy with self-esteem. Self-esteem is how much you like yourself overall. Self-efficacy is task-specific: you can be confident in Excel and not at all in public speaking.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* According to Bandura, what is the strongest source of self-efficacy? **Answer: Mastery experiences**, meaning your own past successes at the task.
2. *(Application)* An employee says "promotions here are just politics, so why try?" Internal or external locus? **Answer: External**, and it puts them at risk of learned helplessness.
3. *(Mini case)* A new joinee is scared of client presentations. As her manager, how would you build her self-efficacy?
   **Answer: Use Bandura's sources.** Start her with small internal presentations so she gets early wins (mastery). Let her watch a peer present (modelling). Give specific encouragement (persuasion). Help her rehearse to manage nerves (emotional state).

**Source:** `Theories of Personality.pdf` (Rotter, Bandura); Robbins & Judge 15e, ch. 5
**Status:** DRAFT
