## Card 1 · The Law of Demand (and when it breaks)

**In one line**
When a price goes up, people buy less; when it goes down, they buy more, as long as nothing else changes.

**Explain it simply**
Economists add "ceteris paribus", which just means *other things staying the same*
(your income, your tastes, the prices of other goods). Why do we buy less when price
rises? Three reasons: we switch to cheaper alternatives (*substitution effect*), our
money now buys less (*income effect*), and each extra unit gives us less satisfaction
than the last (*diminishing marginal utility*), so we only buy more if it's cheaper.

**The ₹100 example**
You have ₹100 for snacks. Samosas cost ₹10, so you buy 10. The price rises to ₹20.
Now ₹100 buys only 5, and you might switch to vada pav instead. Same money, same
hunger, but the higher price means fewer samosas.

**But it breaks sometimes (interviewers love this part):**
| Exception | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Veblen goods** | Higher price makes it *more* desirable, because it signals status | Luxury watches, diamonds |
| **Giffen goods** | A very poor household buys *more* of a cheap staple when its price rises, because it can no longer afford better food | Coarse grains like bajra |
| **Expectations / speculation** | People buy *more* while prices rise, expecting further rises | Shares, gold, property |

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2025)
On 23 July 2025, gold in India hit a record ₹1,00,130 per 10 grams. Two things happened at once:
- **Jewellery buying slowed.** That's the normal law of demand: price up, quantity down.
- **Gold ETF investment boomed.** By September 2025, Indian gold ETFs had drawn
  $2.18 billion for the year, already more than all of 2024 ($1.29 billion).

Same product, opposite reactions. People who buy gold to *wear* bought less. People who
buy it to *invest*, expecting prices to rise further, bought more.
*Sources: World Gold Council; 5paisa / Outlook Money reports on AMFI data.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is the law of demand always true? Give me a case where a price rise increases demand."*

**Strong answer:** "Mostly yes, but not always. With Veblen goods like luxury watches,
a higher price adds status value, so demand can rise. With Giffen goods, very poor
households buy more of a staple when its price rises because they can't afford
substitutes. And when people expect prices to keep rising, as with gold or shares, they
buy more now. Strictly speaking that last case doesn't break the law; it shifts demand
through expectations."

**Common trap**
Mixing up Giffen and Veblen. **Giffen = poor buyers + cheap staple. Veblen = rich buyers + status item.** They are opposite ends of the income scale.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which is a Veblen good? (a) Rice (b) A Rolex watch (c) Salt (d) Bus tickets
   **Answer: (b).** Its appeal comes from its high price and status.
2. *(Application)* All Giffen goods are inferior goods, but are all inferior goods Giffen goods?
   **Answer: No.** A Giffen good is an inferior good with no close substitutes that takes up a large share of a poor household's budget. Most inferior goods don't meet all three conditions.
3. *(Mini case)* A jewellery brand raises prices on its premium "heritage" line by 15%, and sales *rise*. The CFO calls it a pricing mistake that got lucky. Is she right?
   **Answer:** Probably not. The heritage line is likely a Veblen good, where the higher price strengthens the status signal. The brand should test whether *discounting* would actually hurt that line.

## Card 2 · Change in Demand vs Change in Quantity Demanded

**In one line**
If the product's *own price* changes, we move *along* the demand curve. If *anything else* changes, the whole curve *shifts*.

**Explain it simply**
Picture a demand curve as a menu: "at this price, I'll buy this much."
- **Movement along the curve** (called *expansion/contraction of demand*): only the
  product's own price changed. You simply pick a different line from the same menu.
- **Shift of the curve** (called *increase/decrease in demand*): something else changed,
  such as income, tastes, a substitute's price, expectations, population or government
  policy. You now have a whole new menu.

**The ₹100 example**
Ice cream costs ₹50 and you buy 2 a week. The price drops to ₹40, so you buy 4. That's a
**movement** (only the price changed). Now the price is back at ₹50, but your pocket
money doubles and you buy 3 at the *same* ₹50. That's a **shift** (your income changed).

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2025)
From 22 September 2025 ("GST 2.0"), GST on air conditioners and small cars was cut from
28% to 18%, which lowered their prices. Higher sales because of the lower price are a **movement
along** the curve. If AC sales jump because of a heatwave while prices stay the same,
that's a **shift**, since the weather is a non-price factor.
*Source: GST Council, 56th meeting (3 Sept 2025); notifications dated 17 Sept 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"AC sales jumped 40% this summer. Is that a change in demand or quantity demanded?"*

**Strong answer:** "It depends on the cause. If brands cut prices and people bought more,
that's a change in quantity demanded, a movement along the curve. If prices stayed the
same and a heatwave or rising incomes drove sales, that's a change in demand, a rightward
shift. As a manager I'd want to know which, because one is bought with discounts and the
other comes free."

**Common trap**
Saying "demand increased" when only the price fell. Economists keep the terms strictly separate: **price change = quantity demanded; everything else = demand.**

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which causes a **shift** in the demand curve for tea? (a) Tea becomes cheaper (b) Coffee becomes more expensive (c) Tea becomes costlier (d) None
   **Answer: (b).** Coffee's price is a non-price factor for tea, so tea's whole curve shifts right.
2. *(Application)* An advertising campaign makes people want more of a soap at every price. Movement or shift?
   **Answer: Shift (right).** Advertising changes tastes, a non-price factor. The notes point out this is exactly what advertising aims to do.
3. *(Mini case)* A Kolkata sweet shop sells more rosogolla in October than in July at the same price. The owner credits his new signboard. What's a more likely explanation?
   **Answer:** Durga Puja season. Festive demand (tastes, customs, gifting) shifts the demand curve right. The signboard may help, but the season is the bigger non-price factor.

**Source:** `Demand_Notes.PDF` pp. 2.13–2.17 · `Demand_in_Economics.pptx` slide 6

## Card 3 · Price Elasticity of Demand

**In one line**
How *strongly* buyers react to a price change, measured as the % change in quantity ÷ % change in price.

**Explain it simply**
The law of demand tells us the *direction* (price up, quantity down). Elasticity tells us
the *size* of the reaction.
- **Elastic (more than 1):** buyers react a lot (restaurant meals, air travel, one brand of soap)
- **Inelastic (less than 1):** buyers barely react (salt, medicines, petrol overall)

What makes demand elastic? Many substitutes, being a luxury, taking a big share of your
budget, and having time to adjust. Habit and necessity make it inelastic.

**The ₹100 example**
A café sells 100 coffees a day at ₹100, which is **₹10,000** in revenue. It raises the price 10% to ₹110.
- If sales fall to **80** (−20%): elasticity = 20 ÷ 10 = **2, elastic**. Revenue = ₹8,800. **Lost money.**
- If sales fall to **95** (−5%): elasticity = 5 ÷ 10 = **0.5, inelastic**. Revenue = ₹10,450. **Gained money.**

Same price hike, opposite results. **That is why managers care about elasticity.**

| | Elastic | Unit elastic | Inelastic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price ↑ | Revenue ↓ | Same | Revenue ↑ |
| Price ↓ | Revenue ↑ | Same | Revenue ↓ |

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2025)
GST 2.0 (September 2025) created a special **40% rate** for "sin and luxury" goods. Aerated
and sugary drinks moved from 28% to 40%. Tobacco and pan masala stayed at 28% plus a cess,
and will move to 40% once the states' compensation loans are repaid.
Why tax these so heavily? Demand for habit-forming goods is **inelastic**: people keep
buying even at higher prices, so tax revenue stays reliable, and the high price also
discourages some consumption.
*Source: GST Council, 56th meeting (3 Sept 2025); Vision IAS summary of The Hindu, 4 Sept 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"You're a brand manager and need more revenue. Do you raise or cut the price?"*

**Strong answer:** "It depends on price elasticity. If demand is elastic, because there
are many substitutes, as in FMCG brands, a price cut brings in proportionally more buyers
and revenue rises. If it's inelastic, as with a strong brand or a necessity, a price
increase raises revenue. So first I'd estimate elasticity from past price changes."

**Common trap**
Thinking *petrol* and *Indian Oil's petrol* have the same elasticity. Petrol as a whole is inelastic, but **one brand is elastic** because you can simply go to the next pump.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Price falls 5%, quantity demanded rises 15%. Elasticity? **Answer: 3, elastic.**
2. *(Application)* Price drops from ₹6 to ₹4 and quantity rises from 10 to 15 units. Find elasticity.
   **Answer:** (Δq ÷ Δp) × (p ÷ q) = (5 ÷ 2) × (6 ÷ 10) = **1.5, elastic.**
3. *(Mini case)* An airline and a salt company both plan a 10% price hike. Which should worry more about revenue, and why?
   **Answer: The airline.** Air travel is elastic (substitutes like trains, and trips can be postponed), so revenue likely falls. Salt is highly inelastic (no substitute, tiny budget share), so revenue likely rises.

## Card 4 · Income Elasticity of Demand

**In one line**
How demand changes when people's *income* changes, which tells you whether a product is a necessity, a luxury or an inferior good.

**Explain it simply**
Income elasticity = % change in demand ÷ % change in income.
| Value | Type of good | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Less than 0** | Inferior | Richer people buy *less* of it (they upgrade) |
| **0 to 1** | Necessity | Demand rises, but slower than income |
| **More than 1** | Luxury | Demand rises *faster* than income |

Note: "inferior" and "luxury" are economic labels, not judgements about quality.

**The ₹100 example**
Your monthly pocket money rises from ₹1,000 to ₹1,100 (+10%).
- Canteen thali spending rises 5% → elasticity **0.5, necessity**
- Café visits rise 30% → elasticity **3, luxury**
- Instant noodles fall 10% → elasticity **−1, inferior** (you've upgraded to proper meals)

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2024–25)
Indian consumer companies keep talking about **"premiumisation"**. NielsenIQ found that
premium FMCG brands are growing nearly **twice as fast** as mass brands: they make up 27%
of FMCG sales but drive 42% of the sector's value growth. Hindustan Unilever says its
premium segments grow about 30% faster than regular ones, even in rural India.
As incomes rise, spending shifts towards premium versions. That's income elasticity above 1 in action.
*Sources: NielsenIQ via Storyboard18; ICRA, Dec 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why do luxury car sales fall harder in a slowdown than a salt company's sales?"*

**Strong answer:** "Income elasticity. Luxury cars have income elasticity above 1, so
when incomes fall, demand falls more than proportionally, and sales are highly cyclical.
Salt is a necessity with elasticity near zero, so its sales barely move. Some goods, like
used cars, can even sell *more* in a slowdown because they're inferior goods."

**Common trap**
Assuming "inferior good" means bad quality. It only means people buy less of it as they get richer, like **used cars** in the notes' example, which have an elasticity of −0.375.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Income rises 10%, TV demand rises 20%. What type of good? **Answer: Luxury (elasticity 2).**
2. *(Application)* Income rises 5%, bajra demand falls 2%. Elasticity and type?
   **Answer: −0.4, an inferior good.**
3. *(Mini case)* A car dealer sees new-car sales rise from 400 to 500 and used-car sales fall from 4,000 to 3,850 after incomes rose 10%. What should they stock more of if incomes keep rising?
   **Answer: New cars.** New cars: +25% ÷ 10% = **2.5 (luxury)**. Used cars: −3.75% ÷ 10% = **−0.375 (inferior)**. Rising incomes favour new cars.

**Source:** `Demand_Notes.PDF` pp. 2.30–2.32 · `Elasticity_in_Economics.pdf` slide 7

## Card 5 · Cross Elasticity of Demand

**In one line**
How demand for one product reacts when the price of *another* product changes, which tells you if they're substitutes or complements.

**Explain it simply**
Cross elasticity = % change in demand for X ÷ % change in price of Y.
- **Positive → substitutes.** Y gets costlier, so people switch to X (tea ↔ coffee).
- **Negative → complements.** Y gets costlier, so people buy less X too (cars ↔ petrol, printers ↔ ink).
- **Zero → unrelated** (salt ↔ sneakers).

The bigger the number, the closer the relationship.

**The ₹100 example**
At the college canteen, coffee goes from ₹20 to ₹22 (+10%). Tea sales go from 100 to 115
cups (+15%). Cross elasticity = 15 ÷ 10 = **+1.5**. Tea and coffee are **strong substitutes**,
and the tea stall just got customers for free.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2024)
In early July 2024, Jio, Airtel and Vodafone Idea raised mobile tariffs by **10–27%**.
State-run BSNL did not raise prices, and its cheapest monthly plan was around ₹108 against
₹189–199 at the private telcos. In July, BSNL was the **only** telecom company to gain
subscribers, adding about 29 lakh, while Airtel, Vi and Jio all lost users.
Private telco prices went up, and demand for BSNL went up: **positive cross elasticity,
so they are substitutes.**
*Caution: the shift was partly temporary. BSNL still ended July 2024 with fewer users than in January 2024.*
*Source: TRAI subscriber data for July 2024, via Light Reading and Moneylife.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Petrol prices rise sharply. Which companies win and which lose?"*

**Strong answer:** "Use cross elasticity. Petrol and petrol cars are complements, so
costlier petrol hurts demand for petrol cars. Electric vehicles and public transport
are substitutes for petrol, so EV makers and metro or bus services may gain. That's why
fuel price news moves auto stocks differently."

**Common trap**
Assuming a negative cross elasticity *always* means complements. The notes warn that a strong **income effect** can also cause it. Positive reliably means substitutes; negative needs a closer look.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Cross elasticity between two brands of notebooks is +1.5. What are they? **Answer: Close substitutes.**
2. *(Application)* Printers are sold cheap but ink cartridges are expensive. Which concept explains this strategy?
   **Answer: Complementary goods (tied demand).** Once you own the printer, your demand for that brand's ink is inelastic, so the company profits on the ink.
3. *(Mini case)* A Kolkata metro fare cut is announced. Which local businesses should be worried: app-based cab services, or the tea stalls outside metro stations?
   **Answer: Cab services.** Metro rides are a *substitute* for cabs (positive cross elasticity), so cheaper metro fares reduce cab demand. Tea stalls near stations are closer to *complements*, since more metro riders means more footfall, so they may gain.

**Source:** `Demand_Notes.PDF` pp. 2.29, 2.33–2.35 · `Elasticity_in_Economics.pdf` slide 8
