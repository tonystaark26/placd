## Card 6 · Scarcity & Opportunity Cost

**In one line**
Resources are limited but wants are not, so every choice costs you the best thing you gave up.

**Explain it simply**
Economics starts with **scarcity**: we never have enough money, time or resources for
everything we want, so we must choose. Every choice is a **trade-off**. The
**opportunity cost** is the value of the *next-best* option you gave up, not every option
and not just the money. Smart decisions are also made **at the margin**: not "study or
sleep?" but "is *one more* hour of study worth *one less* hour of sleep?"

**The ₹100 example**
You have ₹100 and one evening. You could go for a pizza, buy a used exam guide, or
take an auto home instead of the bus. You pick pizza. If the exam guide was your second
choice, **the exam guide is the opportunity cost of the pizza.** The bus fare saving
doesn't count, because it wasn't your next-best option.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Feb 2025)
In Union Budget 2025, the government made income up to **₹12 lakh tax-free** under the
new regime. Reports estimated the government would give up about **₹1 lakh crore** in
direct tax revenue. That money could have built roads or hospitals: that is the
**opportunity cost**. The government judged that leaving the money with about 1 crore
middle-class taxpayers, to spend and boost demand, was the better use.
*Sources: Business Today, 1 Feb 2025; Union Budget 2025–26.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the opportunity cost of your MBA?"*

**Strong answer:** "Not just the fees. The bigger cost is the salary and work experience I
gave up for two years. I chose the MBA because I expect the long-term return, through
better roles and faster growth, to be higher than what I gave up."

**Common trap**
Adding up *all* the alternatives, or counting only money. Opportunity cost is **only the single next-best option**, and it includes time and experience, not just rupees.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Opportunity cost is… (a) the money you spend (b) the value of the next-best option you give up (c) the total of all options (d) a sunk cost
   **Answer: (b).**
2. *(Application)* Rahul leaves a ₹6 lakh-a-year job for a 2-year MBA with ₹10 lakh in fees. What's the full economic cost?
   **Answer: ₹22 lakh** = ₹10 lakh fees (money actually paid) + ₹12 lakh salary given up (opportunity cost).
3. *(Mini case)* A company can spend ₹50 crore on a new factory or on a marketing campaign. It picks the factory. What should the CFO call the marketing campaign's expected return?
   **Answer:** The **opportunity cost** of building the factory. The factory only makes sense if its expected return beats the campaign's.

## Card 7 · Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)

**In one line**
A curve showing the most an economy (or firm) can produce of two things with the resources and technology it has.

**Explain it simply**
- **On the curve:** resources are fully used, so production is efficient.
- **Inside the curve:** some resources sit idle (unemployment, unused machines), so production is inefficient.
- **Outside the curve:** impossible *today*, but reachable later with more resources or better technology, which **shifts the curve outward**.

Why is it bowed outward? Because of **increasing opportunity cost**: resources are
specialised. To make more guns, you must turn dairy farms into factories, and each extra
gun costs more butter than the last.

**The ₹100 example**
The college canteen has 2 cooks and 1 hour. They can make **40 dosas, or 60 sandwiches**,
or a mix like 20 dosas + 35 sandwiches. If one cook calls in sick and they make only 15 of
each, they're **inside** the curve. If the canteen buys a dosa machine, the whole curve
**shifts out**: more of both becomes possible.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2025–26)
The RBI's survey of factories found Indian manufacturing was using about **74–77% of its
capacity** through 2025–26 (77.4% in Jan–Mar 2026). So roughly a quarter of factory
capacity sat unused: the economy was working **inside** its PPF. Economists watch this
number closely, because when utilisation stays high, firms start building new capacity,
which shifts the curve out.
*Source: RBI, Order Books, Inventories and Capacity Utilisation Survey (OBICUS), Q2–Q4 2025–26.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What does it mean if an economy is producing inside its PPF?"*

**Strong answer:** "It's wasting resources: there's unemployment or idle capacity. It could
produce more of *both* goods without giving anything up. The fix is better use of existing
resources, not new ones. Moving outward from the curve itself needs new resources or
better technology."

**Common trap**
Calling points *outside* the curve "inefficient". They are **unattainable** for now. Points *inside* are the inefficient ones.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* A point inside the PPF shows… (a) efficiency (b) unused resources (c) an impossible output (d) growth
   **Answer: (b).**
2. *(Application)* Which shifts India's PPF outward? (a) A strike in factories (b) Better use of idle machines (c) New technology in manufacturing (d) Moving along the curve
   **Answer: (c).** (a) moves production inside the curve; (b) moves it *towards* the curve, not beyond it.
3. *(Mini case)* A textile firm's machines run at 60% capacity, yet the CEO wants to build a new plant. What would you ask first?
   **Answer:** "Why not use the 40% idle capacity first?" The firm is inside its own PPF. A new plant makes sense only if demand will outgrow even full capacity.

## Card 8 · Law of Supply & What Shifts It

**In one line**
When price rises, sellers offer more. When costs, technology, taxes or weather change, the whole supply curve shifts.

**Explain it simply**
Supply is the mirror image of demand: price and quantity supplied move in the **same**
direction, because a higher price makes producing more profitable.
- **Movement along the curve:** only the product's own price changed.
- **Shift of the curve:** something else changed, such as cost of production, technology,
  taxes or subsidies, the number of sellers, expectations, or weather for farm goods.

**Exceptions:** perishable or seasonal goods (supply is nearly fixed in the short run), rare
items (only one painting exists) and labour (past a point, some people work *fewer* hours
as wages rise, because they value leisure more).

**The ₹100 example**
A juice stall sells 20 glasses a day at ₹20. A college fest raises the going price to ₹30, so
the owner stays open longer and makes 30 glasses. That's a **movement** along the curve.
Then oranges double in price: even at ₹30 a glass, he now makes only 15. That's a
**leftward shift**, because his costs rose.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (July 2024)
Extreme heat followed by heavy rain, and landslides that cut roads from Himachal
Pradesh, choked tomato supply in July 2024. By 20 July, government data showed tomatoes
averaging about **₹74/kg across India and ₹93/kg in Delhi** (₹100 at some outlets),
up from about ₹35 in early June. People didn't suddenly want more tomatoes: the
**supply curve shifted left**, and the price jumped.
*Source: Department of Consumer Affairs price data, via PTI / Deccan Herald, 21 July 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Tomato prices doubled this month. Is that a demand-side or supply-side story?"*

**Strong answer:** "Mostly supply-side. Weather and transport problems shifted the supply
curve left. Demand for a daily staple barely changes, so the price jumped. And because
tomatoes are perishable, supply can't respond quickly, which makes the spike sharper."

**Common trap**
Saying "supply increased" when sellers sold more *because the price rose*. That's an increase in **quantity supplied** (a movement). **Supply** only increases when the curve shifts.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which causes a **shift** in supply? (a) The product's price rises (b) A new technology cuts production costs (c) Buyers' incomes rise (d) None
   **Answer: (b).** (c) shifts *demand*, not supply.
2. *(Application)* The government gives a subsidy to solar panel makers. What happens to the supply curve?
   **Answer: It shifts right.** Lower costs mean more is supplied at every price.
3. *(Mini case)* A Kolkata flower seller can't increase supply during Durga Puja even though prices triple. Which exception is this?
   **Answer: Perishable / seasonal goods.** Supply is nearly fixed in the short run, so a demand spike becomes a price spike.

## Card 9 · Market Equilibrium, Shortage & Surplus

**In one line**
The price where the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. Above it there's a surplus; below it, a shortage.

**Explain it simply**
- **Price too high → surplus:** unsold stock piles up, so sellers cut prices.
- **Price too low → shortage:** queues and "sold out", so prices get pushed up.
- The market keeps nudging back to **equilibrium**, where nothing is left over and nobody is left wanting.

| What changes | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand ↑ (shifts right) | ↑ | ↑ |
| Demand ↓ | ↓ | ↓ |
| Supply ↑ (shifts right) | ↓ | ↑ |
| Supply ↓ | ↑ | ↓ |

**The ₹100 example**
The canteen makes 100 samosas. At **₹10**, exactly 100 students want one, which is equilibrium.
At **₹15**, only 60 want one, so 40 are left over (a **surplus**) and the price will come down.
At **₹5**, 150 want one, so 50 students leave hungry (a **shortage**) and the price will go up.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2024 – Jan 2025)
When tickets for Coldplay's Mumbai shows (held in January 2025) went on sale on 22 September 2024,
about **1.3 crore people** logged in for roughly **1.5 lakh tickets**, priced at about ₹2,500–₹35,000.
They sold out in minutes, and resale listings appeared at **₹2–3 lakh and more**. The official
price was far **below equilibrium**, which created a huge shortage. The organisers' response
was to add more shows, increasing supply.
*Note: ticket scalping is illegal in India, and BookMyShow filed a police complaint.*
*Sources: Business Today, 24–25 Sept 2024; Outlook Business; The Week, 13 Nov 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Coldplay tickets sold out in minutes and resold for lakhs. Should they have charged more?"*

**Strong answer:** "Economically, the price was below equilibrium, so there was a shortage,
and scalpers captured the difference. They could raise prices, but that risks fan anger and
looks unfair. The better options are what they did: add shows to increase supply, and fight
resale with ID-linked tickets. It's a trade-off between revenue and brand goodwill."

**Common trap**
Thinking a shortage just means "not enough was made". A shortage only exists **at a given price**: at a high enough price, the shortage disappears.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* If the price is set above equilibrium, there will be… (a) a shortage (b) a surplus (c) equilibrium (d) no effect
   **Answer: (b).**
2. *(Application)* A heatwave raises demand for ACs while a GST cut makes them cheaper to supply. What happens to AC sales quantity?
   **Answer: It rises.** Both demand ↑ and supply ↑ push quantity up. The price effect is unclear, since the two forces pull it in opposite directions.
3. *(Mini case)* A new café's ₹49 cold coffee has a 30-minute queue every evening. What's the economic signal?
   **Answer:** The price is below equilibrium, so there's a shortage. The café can raise the price, add capacity (supply), or accept the queue as free marketing.

**Source:** `Demand_vs_Supply_Comparative_Analysis.pptx` slides 4–8

## Card 10 · Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility

**In one line**
Each extra unit of the same thing gives you less extra satisfaction than the one before.

**Explain it simply**
**Utility** = satisfaction. **Total utility (TU)** is all the satisfaction you get; **marginal
utility (MU)** is the *extra* satisfaction from one more unit:
**MU = TUₙ − TUₙ₋₁** (correcting a typo in the source notes, which show a + sign).

MU keeps falling, and can even hit zero or go negative (one samosa too many). This is
why demand curves slope down: you'll only buy more if it's cheaper, because each extra
unit is worth less to you.

**The ₹100 example**
From the notes' tea table: the 1st cup of tea gives 30 units of satisfaction, the 2nd gives 20,
the 3rd 15, the 4th 10… the 10th gives just 1, and the **11th gives −4**: total satisfaction
actually *drops* from 99 to 95. In rupees: if your first chai is worth ₹30 to you, the second
₹20 and the third ₹15, and chai costs ₹15, you stop at three cups.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2025)
In July 2025, gold hit a record **₹1,00,130 per 10 grams**, while water is almost free.
Water is far more essential, so its **total** utility is enormous. But it's abundant, so the
**marginal** utility of one more litre is tiny. Gold is scarce, so each extra gram has high
marginal utility, and price follows **marginal**, not total, utility. This is the classic
**diamond–water paradox**.
*Source: World Gold Council / Outlook Money, July 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Water is essential and diamonds aren't. So why is water cheap and diamonds expensive?"*

**Strong answer:** "Because price depends on marginal utility, not total utility. Water is
plentiful, so the value of one extra litre is low. Diamonds are scarce, so one more carat is
valued highly. Total usefulness and price per unit are different things."

**Common trap**
Mixing up total and marginal utility. Total utility can still be *rising* while marginal utility *falls*, as long as MU is positive.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* TU after 3 cups = 65, after 4 cups = 75. What is the MU of the 4th cup? **Answer: 10** (75 − 65).
2. *(Application)* When MU becomes negative, what happens to TU? **Answer: TU starts falling.**
3. *(Mini case)* A pizza chain offers "second pizza at 50% off". Which concept explains the discount?
   **Answer: Diminishing marginal utility.** The second pizza is worth less to you, so the chain lowers its price to match what you'd pay for it.

## Card 11 · Indifference Curves & Consumer Equilibrium

**In one line**
You're happiest where your budget line just touches the highest indifference curve you can reach, where MRS = Pₓ ÷ Pᵧ.

**Explain it simply**
- An **indifference curve (IC)** shows combinations of two goods that make you *equally*
  happy. This is the *ordinal* approach of Hicks & Allen: we only rank choices, we don't
  measure utility in numbers (unlike Marshall's *cardinal* approach).
- ICs slope down, bow towards the origin, **never cross**, and a higher IC means more satisfaction.
- The **marginal rate of substitution (MRS)** is how much of one good you'd give up for one
  more of the other, and it keeps falling.
- The **budget line** shows everything you can afford. **Equilibrium** is where the two
  touch: the best combination you can actually afford.

**The ₹100 example**
You have ₹100. Chai costs ₹10 and a samosa ₹20. Your budget line: 10 chai, *or* 5 samosas,
*or* mixes like 4 chai + 3 samosas (₹40 + ₹60). Among all the mixes costing exactly ₹100,
the one you enjoy most is your equilibrium.
From the notes' table: going from (1 apple, 10 oranges) to (2, 7), you give up 3 oranges for
1 apple. Next you give up 2, then 1. That's **diminishing MRS**, which is why ICs bow inward.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2025)
GST 2.0 (22 September 2025) cut GST on air conditioners from **28% to 18%**. For a
household choosing between an AC and other spending, the AC's *relative price* fell, so the
budget line pivots outward on the AC side. The household can now reach a **higher
indifference curve**, and many will rearrange their spending towards the cheaper AC.
*Source: GST Council, 56th meeting, 3 Sept 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Explain consumer equilibrium in one line."*

**Strong answer:** "It's the point where the consumer's budget line is tangent to the highest
indifference curve they can reach. There, the rate at which they're willing to swap two
goods (MRS) equals the rate at which the market lets them swap (the price ratio)."

**Common trap**
Drawing two indifference curves that cross. They **never** intersect: that would mean one combination gives two different levels of satisfaction.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Why are indifference curves convex to the origin? **Answer: Diminishing MRS.** You give up less and less of one good for each extra unit of the other.
2. *(Application)* Which approach does the indifference curve use: cardinal or ordinal? **Answer: Ordinal** (Hicks & Allen). It ranks preferences without measuring utility.
3. *(Mini case)* A student spends all her pocket money on chai and samosas. Chai's price doubles. What happens to her budget line, and can she stay on the same indifference curve?
   **Answer:** The budget line pivots inward on the chai side. She can no longer afford her old combination, so she moves to a **lower** indifference curve and, in all likelihood, buys fewer chais.
