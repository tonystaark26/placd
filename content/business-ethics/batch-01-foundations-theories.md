## Card E1 · What Business Ethics Is

**In one line**
Business ethics applies ideas of right and wrong to business decisions: how a firm treats customers, staff, investors and society.

**Explain it simply**
**Ethics** is our sense of right and wrong behaviour, our moral values.
**Business ethics** applies those values to business situations: pricing, selling, hiring, reporting, sourcing and the environment.

In the slides, Andrew Crane calls it the study of business situations where right and wrong are at stake. Kirk Hanson calls it the study of standards of business behaviour that promote human welfare.

**Its key features:**
- It works as a **code of conduct**, built on moral values like honesty, fairness and respect.
- It applies at **every level**, from the CEO to the newest trainee.
- It protects **all stakeholders**, not just owners.
- It builds **trust**, and guides decisions in grey areas.
- It's partly **voluntary** and goes **beyond the law** (Card E2).
- It takes a **long-term** view: no short-term gains through unfair means.

**The everyday example**
A Kolkata sweet shop sells **₹40** rosogollas.
- **Ethical choices:** using fresh chhena, honest weights, paying the karigar fairly, not reusing yesterday's syrup.
- **Unethical shortcuts:** using cheaper stale chhena "because no one will know" saves maybe **₹3** a piece.

Customers who find out stop coming, and the shortcut costs far more than it saves.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Mar 2024)
On **28 March 2024**, a US judge sentenced **Sam Bankman-Fried**, founder of the crypto exchange **FTX**, to **25 years** in prison for fraud and ordered him to forfeit over **$11 billion**.
Customers lost about **$8 billion** when FTX collapsed in 2022, after their deposits had been secretly used elsewhere.
A business that looked brilliant failed on the most basic ethical duty: being honest with the people who trusted it with their money.
*Sources: AP via ADN, Investment Executive, Mar 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What does business ethics mean to you?"*

**Strong answer:** "It's applying right and wrong to business choices, beyond just what's legal. To me it comes down to three questions:
- Am I being honest with everyone affected?
- Am I being fair to customers, colleagues and investors?
- Could I defend this decision publicly?

FTX shows the opposite: a company that grew fast but broke customers' trust, and collapsed with them. Ethics isn't a cost centre; it's what makes trust, and so the business, last."

**Common trap**
Treating business ethics as just "following the law" or "doing charity". It's about **everyday decisions**, like pricing, selling, reporting and treating staff, not just big donations.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* How is business ethics different from ethics in general? **Answer: It is the application of general ethical principles to business situations and decisions.**
2. *(Application)* A shop owner uses honest weights even though customers can't check. Which feature of business ethics does this show? **Answer: Honesty and integrity, as a voluntary code of conduct**: doing right even when no one is watching.
3. *(Mini case)* A startup inflates its user numbers to raise funds faster, saying "everyone does it". Why is this an ethical problem, not just a strategy choice?
   **Answer: It deceives investors who rely on honest information.** It trades long-term trust for short-term gain. When discovered, it can destroy the company, as the FTX and Satyam cases show.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slides 15–26

## Card E2 · Ethics vs Law

**In one line**
Law sets the minimum that society enforces. Ethics asks what's right, even where the law is silent or allows something unfair.

**Explain it simply**
| | Law | Ethics |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Parliament, courts, regulators | Moral values, conscience, society |
| Nature | Compulsory, with penalties | Largely voluntary |
| Scope | Written and specific | Wider, covers grey areas |
| Question | "Is it allowed?" | "Is it right?" |

They overlap but aren't the same, which gives four combinations:
- **Legal and ethical:** paying fair wages on time.
- **Legal but unethical:** exploiting a legal loophole that harms customers.
- **Illegal but arguably ethical:** a whistleblower leaking confidential proof of fraud.
- **Illegal and unethical:** bribery, accounting fraud.

Good companies aim for **legal *and* ethical**, and treat the law as the floor, not the ceiling.

**The everyday example**
A PG owner can legally charge any rent the tenant agrees to.
- Raising the rent from **₹6,000 to ₹9,000** mid-term for a student who can't move during exams may be **legal** if the contract allows it.
- But using the student's exam-time helplessness that way is **unethical**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr 2024)
In **April 2024**, a report by the Swiss NGO **Public Eye** and IBFAN said **Nestlé** added sugar to **Cerelac** baby cereal in India and other lower-income markets: about **3 g per serving** in India, while similar products in the UK and Germany had **none**.
- Nestlé India said it had cut added sugar by up to 30% over five years, and that the sugar was declared on the pack.
- The FSSAI and the Consumer Affairs Ministry began examining it.

Even if a product meets local rules, a **double standard** across countries raises an ethical question that the law alone doesn't answer.
*Sources: Deccan Herald, DTNext (Reuters), Onmanorama, Apr 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"If something is legal, is it automatically ethical?"*

**Strong answer:** "No. Law is the minimum society enforces; ethics asks what's right. Something can be legal but unethical, like selling a less healthy version of a product in one country just because local rules allow it.
I see law as the floor. In a business decision, I'd first check it's legal, then ask whether it's fair to everyone affected and whether I'd be comfortable if it were public. If it fails those, legality alone isn't enough."

**Common trap**
Saying ethics and law are opposites. They mostly **overlap**: many laws started as ethical expectations. The interesting cases are the gaps between them.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is the key difference between law and ethics? **Answer: Law is compulsory and enforced with penalties. Ethics is largely voluntary and covers what's right beyond legal rules.**
2. *(Application)* A company legally avoids tax through complex structures in a way that harms its home country's revenue. Which quadrant is this? **Answer: Legal but (arguably) unethical.**
3. *(Mini case)* A firm's product meets Indian food rules but is less healthy than its European version. The marketing head says "we're compliant, case closed." What would you add?
   **Answer: Compliance isn't the same as ethics.** I'd ask whether the double standard is fair to Indian consumers, how it would look if reported, and whether we can apply the higher standard everywhere, since trust is at stake.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slide 26 (ethics vs business law)

## Card E3 · Why Ethics Pays

**In one line**
Ethical firms earn trust, talent and goodwill. Unethical shortcuts bring short-term gains and long-term collapse.

**Explain it simply**
The slides list why business needs ethics:
- **Survival and growth:** unethical gains don't last.
- **Consumer rights and the welfare of society.**
- **Mutual benefit:** goodwill, reputation and repeat customers.
- **Fewer penalties:** fewer fines and less regulatory trouble.
- **Better employees:** people want to work for honest firms, and productivity rises.
- **Protection** against corrupt employees and competitors.
- **A stronger economy:** higher productivity and national income.

The cost of getting it wrong is visible in famous collapses: **Enron** (hidden debt), **Satyam** (over ₹7,000 crore of fake profits), **Volkswagen** (emissions cheating), **Kingfisher** and **Nirav Modi–PNB** (bank fraud of over ₹13,000 crore).

**The everyday example**
Two mobile-repair shops in Chandni Chowk:
- **Shop A** charges an honest **₹1,200** for a screen and tells you when a cheap fix will do.
- **Shop B** charges **₹2,000**, swaps in a used part and pockets the gain.

Shop B makes **₹800** more on the first job. But Shop A gets your repeat visits, your friends' business and good reviews, so over a year it earns far more.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jan–Mar 2024)
On **31 January 2024**, the **RBI** barred **Paytm Payments Bank** from taking new deposits, wallet top-ups or FASTag credits, at first from 29 February and later from **15 March 2024**. It cited audit reports showing **persistent non-compliance** and material supervisory concerns.
A business used by millions lost its core banking functions almost overnight. It shows how ignoring compliance and governance can cost far more than following the rules ever would.
*Sources: RBI press release; Outlook Business, Business Standard, Jan–Mar 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Doesn't being ethical cost a company money?"*

**Strong answer:** "Sometimes in the short run. But unethical shortcuts usually cost far more later, in fines, lost licences, lost customers and lost talent.
Paytm Payments Bank lost its core functions after repeated compliance failures. Satyam and Nirav Modi destroyed thousands of crores of value.
Ethics works like insurance and a brand asset together: trust brings repeat customers, better staff and cheaper capital."

**Common trap**
Arguing ethics **only** because it pays. If honesty is just a profit tactic, it disappears when cheating seems profitable. The strongest answer gives both the business case and the moral case.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Name two business reasons for being ethical. **Answer: Any two of:** long-term survival, customer trust and goodwill, fewer legal penalties, attracting and retaining talent, protection against fraud.
2. *(Application)* An employee says "a small lie to close this ₹5 lakh deal won't hurt anyone." What long-term costs might it bring? **Answer: Lost client trust, cancelled contracts, legal claims and reputational damage**, far more than ₹5 lakh.
3. *(Mini case)* Satyam's founder admitted to faking over ₹7,000 crore of profits. Using "why ethics pays", explain what the company lost.
   **Answer: Everything trust supported.** Investor confidence collapsed, clients and staff were shaken, leaders were arrested, and the company had to be rescued by new owners. The fake profits created no real value and destroyed real value.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slides 1–6, 19, 27–29

## Card E4 · Ethical Dilemmas and How to Decide

**In one line**
An ethical dilemma is a choice where values clash, often doing right versus protecting yourself. Simple tests help you decide.

**Explain it simply**
A **dilemma** is not a choice between right and obviously wrong. Real options conflict: honesty vs loyalty, safety vs speed, your job vs the public.

Situations from the slides:
- pushing an inferior product to keep your job;
- approving a slightly contaminated food batch;
- staying quiet about illegal dumping;
- promising features the software doesn't have;
- hiring the CEO's under-qualified nephew.

**Tests to decide:**
1. **Legality test:** is it legal?
2. **Fairness test:** is it fair to everyone affected?
3. **Newspaper test:** would I be comfortable if it were front-page news?
4. **Mirror test:** how will I feel about myself?

These echo Blanchard and Peale's three questions: Is it legal? Is it balanced? How will it make me feel?

**The everyday example**
An elderly widow pays **₹500** for a service at your office. You notice a **second ₹500 note** stuck behind the first. She hasn't noticed.
- **Legal?** Keeping it is effectively taking her money.
- **Fair?** No.
- **Newspaper?** "Office keeps widow's extra ₹500" isn't a story you'd want.
- **Mirror?** You'd know.

You return the **₹500**. That's the slide's classic dilemma, solved with four quick tests.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Oct 2025)
In **October 2025**, Madhya Pradesh police arrested the owner of Tamil Nadu-based **Sresan Pharmaceuticals** after children in the state died after taking its **Coldrif** cough syrup. Reports put the toll at about 20 or more.
- Tests found one batch had about **48.6% diethylene glycol**, a toxic chemical, hundreds of times the permitted limit.
- States banned the syrup and seized stock.

It's the slides' quality-control dilemma ("approve the slightly contaminated batch?") at its gravest. Skipping safety checks to save time or money can cost lives.
*Sources: Business Today, The Week, Gulf News, Oct 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Your manager asks you to approve a batch you think fails quality checks. What do you do?"*

**Strong answer:** "I'd run the tests:
- Is it legal? Probably not, if it fails the standards.
- Is it fair to customers? No.
- Would I be fine if it were in the news? Definitely not.

So I wouldn't sign off. I'd raise it with facts: test results, the risk, and options like re-testing or a short delay. If my manager insisted, I'd escalate through the company's ethics or vigil channel. Speed matters, but never more than customer safety."

**Common trap**
Treating every hard choice as a dilemma to "balance". When one option seriously harms others or breaks the law, like approving an unsafe batch, it isn't a balance. The right answer is clear, even if it's hard.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is the "newspaper test"? **Answer: Asking whether you'd be comfortable if your decision appeared on tomorrow's front page.**
2. *(Application)* Your boss tells you to ensure the CEO's under-qualified nephew gets a job. Which values clash? **Answer: Fairness to other candidates and merit, versus loyalty and self-protection.**
3. *(Mini case)* A sales executive is told to promise a client software features that don't exist, or lose his bonus. Apply the tests.
   **Answer: It fails the fairness test** (it misleads the client) **and the newspaper test** (public deception), and may breach contract and consumer law. He should refuse, explain the risk to his manager, and offer an honest alternative such as a feature roadmap.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slides 7–12, 16; Fernando (ethical decision making)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card E5 · Utilitarianism: The Greatest Good for the Greatest Number

**In one line**
Utilitarianism judges an action by its consequences: the right choice produces the most total good for everyone affected.

**Explain it simply**
Associated with **Jeremy Bentham** and **John Stuart Mill**, it's an ethics of **welfare** and **outcomes**.
1. List the options.
2. Estimate the benefits and harms of each, for everyone affected.
3. Choose the one with the **greatest net good**.

**Strengths:** practical, impartial (everyone counts equally) and familiar to managers, since it resembles cost–benefit analysis.

**Weaknesses:**
- Benefits and harms are hard to measure.
- It can **justify harming a minority** if the majority gains.
- It can ignore rights and justice (Card E6).

**The everyday example**
A college canteen can spend **₹50,000**:
- **(a)** an AC that only the 20 staff enjoy; or
- **(b)** water purifiers used by all 2,000 students.

A utilitarian picks **(b)**: far more people benefit for the same money.
The harder case: closing the canteen's only vegetarian counter, used by 150 students, to add a cheaper counter for 1,850 others. The numbers favour it, but it ignores the minority's needs.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jul 2025)
In **July 2025**, **TCS** said it would cut about **12,000 jobs**, roughly 2% of its workforce, mostly at mid and senior levels, over the year to March 2026.
- CEO K. Krithivasan said it was driven by **skill mismatch** and the inability to redeploy people, not AI.
- He called it the toughest decision of his career.

Layoffs are often defended in utilitarian terms: keeping the company competitive protects many jobs. Critics ask about the harm to those let go, which is exactly the minority problem.
*Sources: Moneylife, Outlook Business, Reuters via Business Recorder, Jul 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is it ethical to lay off 5% of staff to save the company?"*

**Strong answer:** "From a utilitarian view it can be. If it saves the other 95% of jobs and the business, the total good is greater.
But I'd also apply duty and justice. Those let go deserve fair treatment: generous notice, severance, help finding work and transparent criteria. And I'd ask whether we tried alternatives first, like redeployment, reskilling or pay cuts at the top. The right answer combines good outcomes with fair process."

**Common trap**
Using utilitarianism as "the majority always wins". Its real weakness is that it can trample a minority's rights, so mature decisions check it against rights and justice.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* On what basis does utilitarianism judge an action? **Answer: Its consequences: whether it produces the greatest good for the greatest number.**
2. *(Application)* A city must choose between spending ₹10 crore on a flyover for 1 lakh commuters or a hospital wing for 2,000 patients. What would a utilitarian ask? **Answer: Which option produces more total well-being**, weighing the size *and* the seriousness of each benefit, not just the headcount.
3. *(Mini case)* A pharma firm could save ₹50 crore by skipping one safety test, with a tiny chance of harming a few patients. A strict cost–benefit sheet says save the money. What's wrong?
   **Answer: It ignores rights and duties.** Patients have a right to safety, and some harms (deaths) can't be offset by savings. Utilitarianism must be checked against duty and justice.

**Source:** Fernando, *Business Ethics: An Indian Perspective* (utilitarianism: the ethics of welfare)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card E6 · Duties, Rights and Justice

**In one line**
Some things are right or wrong regardless of results. Duty ethics, rights and justice focus on principles and fairness, not just outcomes.

**Explain it simply**
- **Duty ethics (deontology), Immanuel Kant:** act only in ways you'd accept as a **universal rule** (the categorical imperative). Treat people as **ends**, never merely as means. Lying is wrong even if it helps, because you couldn't want everyone to lie.
- **Rights:** people have moral rights, to life, safety, privacy, fair pay and free speech, that others must respect, even if violating them would benefit many.
- **Justice (John Rawls):** a fair system is one you'd choose without knowing your own place in it, behind a "veil of ignorance". It covers:
  - **distributive justice:** fair shares;
  - **procedural justice:** fair processes;
  - **compensatory justice:** making good a harm.

These balance utilitarianism (Card E5): good outcomes can't be bought by trampling rights or fairness.

**The everyday example**
A delivery app could save **₹2 per order** by not paying riders for waiting time at restaurants.
- A utilitarian spreadsheet might say customers benefit.
- **Kant** asks: are we treating riders merely as a means?
- **Rights** asks: do they have a right to fair pay for time worked?
- **Rawls** asks: would you accept this rule if you didn't know whether you'd be the customer or the rider?

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sep 2025)
In **September 2025**, the **Karnataka Platform-Based Gig Workers (Social Security and Welfare) Act, 2025** became law.
- It sets up a welfare board and fund, financed by a **1–5% welfare fee** on payouts to gig workers, paid by aggregators.
- Reports say aggregators can't end a worker's engagement without **14 days' notice and written reasons**.

It reflects rights and justice thinking: delivery and ride workers deserve social security and fair process, not just low prices for customers.
*Sources: PRS India; Outlook Business, Entrackr, Aug–Sep 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is it ethical for an app to keep gig workers' pay low so prices stay low?"*

**Strong answer:** "Outcomes matter, since cheap prices help many customers. But duty and justice ethics say people mustn't be used merely as means, and they have a right to fair pay and safety.
Under Rawls's test, would I accept this pay rule if I might be the rider? If not, it's unfair. Good platforms find a balance: transparent pay, insurance and fair dispute processes. That's where laws like Karnataka's are pushing the industry."

**Common trap**
Treating rights and justice as "anti-business". Fair processes, like transparent pay and appeal rights, reduce disputes, attrition and regulation, which is why many firms adopt them before laws require it.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What does Kant's categorical imperative say? **Answer: Act only on rules you would want to become universal laws, and treat people as ends, never merely as means.**
2. *(Application)* A manager promotes his friend over a more qualified colleague, using a secret process. Which kind of justice is violated? **Answer: Procedural justice**, the fairness of the process, and also distributive justice.
3. *(Mini case)* Collecting and selling users' location data would earn a company ₹100 crore and make its app free for millions. Users didn't clearly consent. Is it ethical?
   **Answer: Not under rights or duty ethics.** It violates users' right to privacy and treats them as means. Large benefits don't justify using people's data without informed consent.

**Source:** Fernando (theories of ethics); Francis & Mishra (ethical theories)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card E7 · Virtue Ethics and the Indian View: Trusteeship and the Gita

**In one line**
Virtue ethics asks "what would a person of good character do?" Indian thought adds trusteeship: wealth is held in trust for society.

**Explain it simply**
- **Virtue ethics (Aristotle):** focus on **character**, not rules or results. Be honest, fair, courageous, compassionate and prudent, and good actions follow. Ask: *what kind of person, or company, do I want to be?*
- **Gandhian trusteeship:** a businessperson is a **trustee**, not the absolute owner, of their wealth. It should be used for the good of all, with harmony between capital and labour. **Sarvodaya** means welfare for all.
- **The Bhagavad Gita:** do your duty (*dharma*) well, without being driven by greed for the fruits (*nishkama karma*). Right means matter, not just results.

Fernando links trusteeship to Indian business philanthropy: the Birla Temple in Kolkata, the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research and the Lifeline Express hospital train.

**The everyday example**
A Gariahat shopkeeper earns well in the Puja season and keeps **₹20,000** aside each year to pay school fees for his two helpers' children.
He doesn't do it because a law demands it or to get publicity. It's his sense of being a **trustee**: "the business grew with them; it should grow them too."

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov 2024)
In the **EdelGive Hurun India Philanthropy List 2024** (for FY24), **Shiv Nadar** and family topped the list, giving **₹2,153 crore**, mainly to education. That's roughly **₹5.9 crore a day**, and the third time in five years they'd been first.
Mukesh Ambani's family was second, with ₹407 crore.
Large-scale giving by founders is the modern face of the trusteeship idea: wealth created by business returned to society.
*Sources: Outlook Money, Adda247, Madhyamam, Nov 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What is Gandhi's idea of trusteeship, and is it relevant today?"*

**Strong answer:** "Gandhi said business owners should see themselves as trustees of their wealth, using it for society's good, with fairness between owners and workers.
It's very relevant. You see it in Indian founder philanthropy like Shiv Nadar's ₹2,000-crore-plus giving and the Tata Trusts. And today it shows up beyond charity, as fair wages, community investment and long-term thinking.
For me it's virtue ethics at the company level: what kind of organisation do we want to be?"

**Common trap**
Reducing trusteeship to charity. Gandhi meant **how the business is run day to day**, fair to labour and community, not just donating at the end.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What does Gandhian trusteeship say about wealth? **Answer: The owner is a trustee of wealth, which should be used for the welfare of society, not merely personal gain.**
2. *(Application)* A manager asks "what would an honest, fair leader do here?" rather than checking rules or numbers. Which approach is this? **Answer: Virtue ethics.**
3. *(Mini case)* A promoter donates ₹50 crore to a hospital but pays his factory workers below minimum wage. Is he practising trusteeship?
   **Answer: No.** Trusteeship covers how the business treats labour and society every day, not just donations. Paying unfair wages contradicts the idea of capital and labour in harmony.

**Source:** Fernando (Gandhian trusteeship, Gita); Francis & Mishra (virtue ethics)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card E8 · Kohlberg's Stages of Moral Development

**In one line**
People reason about right and wrong at different levels, from "will I be punished?" to "what is universally right?". This is why people behave differently in the same situation.

**Explain it simply**
Lawrence Kohlberg's model has **three levels**, with two stages each.

**Pre-conventional** (it's about me):
1. **Punishment and obedience:** "I follow rules so I don't get caught."
2. **Exchange:** "I'll do it if I get something back."

**Conventional** (it's about others and the system):
3. **Interpersonal conformity:** "I'll do what people close to me expect."
4. **Social system and law:** "Rules and duty keep society working."

**Post-conventional** (it's about principles):
5. **Social contract and rights:** "Laws should serve people's rights; some can be changed."
6. **Universal ethical principles:** "I'll do what's just, even against the rules or my own interest."

Fernando notes that many adults stay at the middle stages. For managers, this explains why codes, training and culture matter.

**The everyday example**
Your team finds the canteen billing machine undercharging every lunch by **₹30**. Four colleagues react differently:
- **Stage 1:** "Don't tell, but don't get caught either."
- **Stage 2:** "Tell them only if they give us a discount."
- **Stage 4:** "Rules are rules. Report it."
- **Stage 6:** "It's the canteen worker who'll be blamed for the shortfall. Telling is simply right."

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (May 2024)
**Deloitte's 2024 Gen Z and Millennial Survey** of about 22,800 people in 44 countries found that **50% of Gen Zs** and **43% of millennials** had **turned down assignments** because of their personal ethics or beliefs. About **44% of Gen Zs** had rejected **employers** on the same grounds.
That's reasoning at the higher Kohlberg stages: choosing on principle even when it costs you.
*Sources: Deloitte Global press release, May 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why do some employees cheat while others in the same situation don't?"*

**Strong answer:** "Partly because they reason at different moral levels. Kohlberg showed some people avoid wrongdoing only out of fear of punishment, some to fit in, some because rules matter, and some on principle.
For a company, that means rules and audits catch the first group, culture and role models shape the middle, and the best people need room to raise concerns.
That's why ethics needs all three: clear rules, a strong culture and safe whistleblowing."

**Common trap**
Treating Kohlberg's stages as fixed personality types. People can move up with experience, education and good role models, and even mature people can slip under pressure.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* At Kohlberg's first stage, why do people follow rules? **Answer: To avoid punishment.**
2. *(Application)* An employee reports a colleague's fraud only because "the company rules say I must." Which stage? **Answer: Stage 4, social system and law and order** (conventional level).
3. *(Mini case)* A manager refuses a lucrative but unfair deal even though it's legal and his boss wants it, saying "it would cheat the farmers." Which level of reasoning, and why does it matter to firms?
   **Answer: Post-conventional (stage 5/6)**, principled reasoning beyond rules and self-interest. Firms benefit from people who stop harm the rules didn't anticipate, so they should protect and reward such judgement.

**Source:** Fernando (Kohlberg's model of cognitive moral development); Francis & Mishra
**Status:** DRAFT
