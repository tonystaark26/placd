## Card E9 · Unethical Behaviour at Work

**In one line**
Most workplace ethics problems are everyday ones: misusing company time, lying, theft, abusive behaviour, taking credit, and misleading customers to hit targets.

**Explain it simply**
Common forms, from the slides:
- **Misuse of company time:** personal work during office hours, faked activity.
- **Deliberate deception:** taking credit for others' work, fake sick leave, sabotaging colleagues, misrepresenting products to make a sale.
- **Employee theft:** stationery, cash, data, inflated expense claims.
- **Abusive behaviour:** bullying, humiliation, harassment.
- **Lying to employees:** broken promises, like "an extra day off if you finish early".
- **Violating internet and IT policies.**

**Why it happens:** pressure to meet targets, a poor example from the top, unclear rules, "everyone does it" culture, and weak checks.

**The everyday example**
A management trainee claims **₹1,200** for "client lunch" receipts that were actually a weekend dinner with friends. He also leaves a mouse-jiggler app running so he looks "active" for **2 hours** a day while doing other things.
Each act seems small. Together they're **theft and misuse of company time**, and they break trust when found out.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jun 2024)
In **June 2024**, **Wells Fargo** disclosed in a regulatory filing that it had **fired more than a dozen employees** in its wealth-management unit after reviewing allegations of **"simulation of keyboard activity creating impression of active work"**.
The bank said it **does not tolerate unethical behaviour**.
Faking activity looks harmless, but it's deliberate deception about company time, one of the slides' everyday ethics breaches.
*Sources: Bloomberg via Bloomberg Law; ABC News; HCAMag, Jun 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"You see a colleague padding expense claims. What do you do?"*

**Strong answer:** "I'd first make sure I'm not misreading it, maybe by having a quiet word with them. If it's clear and continuing, I'd report it through the proper channel, my manager or the ethics line, sticking to facts, not gossip.
Small dishonesty spreads if it's tolerated, and it puts everyone's trust, including mine, at risk. I'd rather raise it early than be part of a team where cheating becomes normal."

**Common trap**
Thinking only big frauds count as "unethical". Most ethics damage comes from small, repeated acts that become normal, which is why companies police the small things.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Give two examples of deliberate deception at work. **Answer: Any two of:** taking credit for others' work, fake sick leave, sabotaging a colleague, misrepresenting a product to close a sale.
2. *(Application)* A boss promises an extra day off for finishing early, then refuses. Which behaviour from the slides is this? **Answer: Lying to employees**, a broken promise that erodes trust.
3. *(Mini case)* A sales team is pushed so hard on targets that several reps start selling unsuitable insurance policies. Whose ethics problem is it?
   **Answer: Both the reps' and the organisation's.** The reps are mis-selling, but the target design and the culture are driving it. The fix needs realistic targets, rewards for quality and checks on mis-selling, not only punishing individuals.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slides 7–13

## Card E10 · Conflict of Interest

**In one line**
A conflict of interest arises when personal gain could influence a decision you're supposed to make in someone else's interest.

**Explain it simply**
Managers are trusted to act for the **company and its stakeholders**. A conflict arises when their **personal interest**, like family, friends, side businesses or gifts, could pull them another way.
Typical cases:
- awarding contracts or loans to relatives' firms;
- accepting gifts from vendors;
- trading shares on inside information;
- secretly running a competing side business.

**How to handle it:**
1. **Disclose** it early.
2. **Recuse** yourself from the decision.
3. Let **independent** people decide.
4. **Record** it.

Having a conflict isn't always wrong; **hiding** it and acting on it is. Codes of conduct also ask people to avoid even *apparent* conflicts.

**The everyday example**
The college fest committee needs a **₹45,000** sound system. The treasurer's cousin quotes ₹45,000; another vendor quotes ₹38,000.
If the treasurer quietly picks the cousin, that's a conflict acted on, and costs the fest **₹7,000**.
The ethical route: the treasurer discloses the relationship, steps out, and the committee decides on quality and price.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jul 2025)
In **July 2025**, an **appellate tribunal** in a money-laundering case upheld the Enforcement Directorate's attachment of assets of former **ICICI Bank** CEO **Chanda Kochhar**.
- It found **substance in the ED's allegation of a "quid pro quo"**: about **₹64 crore** reached a company linked to her husband after a ₹300 crore ICICI loan to Videocon.
- She argued a committee, not she alone, sanctioned the loan; the tribunal pointed to a "complete chain of events".
- The findings are prima facie, in a property-attachment proceeding; the separate criminal case continues.

It's the module's conflict-of-interest story: personal interest colliding with a decision made for the bank.
*Sources: SCC Online, Moneylife, Business Today, Jul 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"A vendor who is your close friend bids for a contract you're evaluating. What do you do?"*

**Strong answer:** "I'd disclose the relationship to my manager straight away and step out of the evaluation. Someone independent should score the bids on clear criteria.
Even if my friend's bid is the best, the process has to be clean and seen to be clean. Hiding the link is where a conflict turns into a breach of trust, as the ICICI–Videocon case shows."

**Common trap**
Thinking a conflict is fine "as long as the decision was good". Even a good decision is tainted if the conflict was hidden. Disclosure and recusal protect both you and the company.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is a conflict of interest? **Answer: A situation where personal interest could improperly influence a decision someone is supposed to make for another party**, such as an employer or client.
2. *(Application)* A procurement manager accepts a free foreign holiday from a supplier, then renews its contract. What's the issue? **Answer: Conflict of interest, and possibly bribery.** The gift could influence, or appear to influence, the decision.
3. *(Mini case)* A board member's family owns 30% of a company that wants to merge with yours. What should the board do?
   **Answer: The member must disclose the interest and recuse themselves from discussions and the vote.** The decision should go to independent directors, with the disclosure recorded in the minutes.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slide 4 (ICICI Bank case); Fernando (codes on conflict of interest)

## Card E11 · Whistleblowing

**In one line**
Whistleblowing is an insider exposing wrongdoing, inside the company or to outside authorities. Good firms protect whistleblowers instead of punishing them.

**Explain it simply**
- **Internal whistleblowing:** reporting to management, an ethics officer or an audit committee.
- **External whistleblowing:** going to regulators, police or the media, usually when internal routes fail or are compromised.

**In India:**
- **Sec 177 of the Companies Act, 2013** requires listed companies and certain others to set up a **vigil mechanism** for directors and employees, with direct access to the audit committee chair in appropriate cases.
- **SEBI's listing rules** require a whistleblower policy.
- SEBI runs an **informant reward** scheme for insider-trading tips.

**The ethical tension:** loyalty to the employer versus duty to the public. Whistleblowing is usually justified when the harm is serious, the evidence is solid, internal routes have been tried or are unsafe, and disclosure can actually stop the harm.

In the Nirav Modi–PNB case in the slides, employees who raised concerns were pushed aside. That's what happens when the whistle isn't heard.

**The everyday example**
You're in charge of quality at a food unit and see a supervisor relabelling expired **₹60** juice packs with new dates.
- **Step 1:** report it through the company's vigil or ethics channel, with photos and batch numbers.
- **Step 2:** if nothing happens, and people may get sick, report it to the **FSSAI**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr 2024)
In **April 2024**, the US Senate held a hearing with **Boeing quality engineer Sam Salehpour**. He said shortcuts in assembling the **787 Dreamliner** could create serious safety risks, and that he faced **retaliation** after raising concerns.
The FAA had been examining his claims since February, and Boeing said it was cooperating.
His experience, raising concerns inside, being sidelined, then going outside, is the classic whistleblowing path.
*Sources: AP via Sinclair stations; New York Times reporting, Apr 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Would you blow the whistle on your own company?"*

**Strong answer:** "If I found serious wrongdoing that could harm people or investors, yes, but in the right order. First I'd gather facts and use internal channels: my manager, the ethics line or the vigil mechanism under the Companies Act.
If those failed, or the people involved were senior, I'd go to the regulator. Loyalty matters, but not more than public safety. And a company that punishes whistleblowers has a bigger problem than the one being reported."

**Common trap**
Calling every whistleblower "disloyal". Ethically, loyalty is owed to the company's legitimate purpose, not to wrongdoing. Firms with strong vigil mechanisms catch problems early and cheaply.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is the difference between internal and external whistleblowing? **Answer: Internal reports go to people inside the company, such as management or the audit committee. External reports go outside, to regulators, police or media.**
2. *(Application)* Which section of the Companies Act, 2013 requires listed companies to have a vigil mechanism? **Answer: Section 177.**
3. *(Mini case)* An employee reports suspected loan fraud to her bank manager, who tells her to "stay in your lane." The fraud continues. What should she do next?
   **Answer: Escalate through the formal vigil mechanism**, to the ethics officer or the audit committee chair, with documented evidence. If that fails or is compromised, report to the regulator (RBI) or investigating agencies. Retaliation against her would itself be a breach.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slides 6, 10; Fernando (whistle-blowing); Companies Act, 2013 Sec 177

## Card E12 · Corporate Governance

**In one line**
Corporate governance is the system of rules and oversight that makes a company's leaders accountable to shareholders and stakeholders.

**Explain it simply**
**Core principles:** accountability, transparency, fairness to all shareholders, and responsibility.

**Key mechanisms in India** (Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI's LODR Regulations, which replaced the old **Clause 49**):
- **Independent directors:** at least **one-third** of a listed company's board.
- **An audit committee**, mostly independent, overseeing accounts and auditors.
- **At least one woman director** on specified boards.
- **Statutory auditors**, with rotation rules.
- **Related-party transaction** approvals.
- A **vigil mechanism** (Card E11).

**Why it got stricter:** **Satyam (2009)**. Its founder confessed to over ₹7,000 crore of fake profits and cash, and the board and auditors had missed it. The scandal pushed India's governance reforms.

**The everyday example**
A college club's treasurer controls a **₹2 lakh** fest budget alone, with no receipts and no review. That's weak governance.
Good governance looks like this:
- two signatories for payments above **₹5,000**;
- monthly accounts shared with members;
- a faculty adviser reviewing the books;
- an elected committee that can question the treasurer.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Mar–May 2025)
In **March 2025**, **IndusInd Bank** disclosed **accounting discrepancies** in its derivatives portfolio. Reported estimates of the hit were around **₹1,600–2,100 crore**, and the issue had built up over years.
- The board hired **Grant Thornton** for a forensic review.
- In **April 2025**, the **CEO and deputy CEO resigned**.
- Moody's cited internal-control weaknesses.

Governance exists to catch exactly this kind of problem early: strong audit committees, independent checks and clear controls.
*Sources: Moneylife, Business Standard, The Week (PTI), Outlook Business, Mar–May 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the role of independent directors?"*

**Strong answer:** "They're the board's internal check. Because they're not part of management or the promoter family, they can question decisions, chair the audit committee and protect minority shareholders.
Indian law requires at least a third of a listed company's board to be independent. Satyam showed what happens when independent oversight is weak; IndusInd shows why audit committees must dig into complex areas like derivatives, not just sign off."

**Common trap**
Treating governance as just "having the right committees". Satyam had a board and auditors. What matters is whether they actually **question, verify and act**.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Name the four core principles of corporate governance. **Answer: Accountability, transparency, fairness and responsibility.**
2. *(Application)* What minimum share of a listed company's board must be independent directors in India? **Answer: At least one-third.**
3. *(Mini case)* A promoter-led company's CFO reports directly to the promoter, and the audit committee meets once a year for 20 minutes. What governance risks do you see?
   **Answer: Weak oversight.** The finance function isn't independently checked, and the audit committee isn't really reviewing. Fixes: regular audit-committee meetings, direct access to auditors, internal audit reporting to the committee, and independent directors who probe.

**Source:** `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slide 3 (Satyam); Fernando (corporate governance, Clause 49); Companies Act, 2013
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card E13 · Profit Maximisation vs the Stakeholder View

**In one line**
Friedman said a firm's only social responsibility is to increase profits within the rules. Stakeholder theory says it must also serve employees, customers, suppliers and society.

**Explain it simply**
**Profit maximisation (Module 2):**
- *Strengths:* profit is vital for survival, measures efficiency and funds growth.
- *Weaknesses:* "profit" is vague (gross? net?), it ignores the time value of money and risk, and it can neglect quality, image and people.

Fernando describes three views:
- **Stockholder (shareholder) theory:** managers work for shareholders. **Milton Friedman** said business has one social responsibility, to increase profits, while staying within the rules of the game.
- **Stakeholder theory (Freeman):** firms must balance the interests of everyone affected: employees, customers, suppliers, communities and the environment.
- **Social contract theory:** business exists with society's permission, and must give back value in return.

**The everyday example**
A Kolkata biscuit unit can raise profit by **₹4 lakh** a year by switching to cheaper, lower-quality oil.
- **Shareholder view:** do it, if it's legal and sales hold.
- **Stakeholder view:** consider customers' health, workers' pride, distributors' reputation and the brand's long-term value. The ₹4 lakh may cost far more in lost trust.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sep 2025)
In **September 2025**, **Jerry Greenfield**, co-founder of **Ben & Jerry's**, **resigned** from the company. He cited years of disputes with its owner (Unilever, and later the Magnum ice-cream group) over the brand's freedom to speak on social issues.
Ben & Jerry's independent board, created in the 2000 sale to protect its social mission, had sued Unilever earlier in 2025.
It's the shareholder-versus-stakeholder debate in one company: whose interests should guide the brand?
*Sources: NPR, Sep 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Should a company's only goal be maximising shareholder profit?"*

**Strong answer:** "Profit is essential; no profit, no business. But I'd frame it as long-term value, not short-term profit.
Friedman's view works only if the 'rules of the game' capture every harm, and they often don't. Stakeholder thinking recognises that loyal employees, satisfied customers and trusting communities *create* shareholder value over time. So my answer is profit, earned in a way that keeps all stakeholders on side."

**Common trap**
Misquoting Friedman as "anything for profit". He said profits should be made **within the rules of the game**: open, free competition without deception or fraud.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* According to Milton Friedman, what is the social responsibility of business? **Answer: To increase its profits, while staying within the rules of the game**: open competition, without deception or fraud.
2. *(Application)* A firm sets targets for employee well-being, supplier fairness and emissions alongside profit. Which theory is it following? **Answer: Stakeholder theory.**
3. *(Mini case)* A company could raise next quarter's profit by cutting all training and delaying safety upgrades. Name two weaknesses of profit maximisation this shows.
   **Answer: It ignores risk** (accidents, attrition) **and long-term, intangible value** (skills, quality, reputation). It boosts a narrow short-term number at the cost of future value.

**Source:** `Module 2,LMS.pptx` slides 2–4, 7; Fernando (stockholder, stakeholder and social contract theories)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card E14 · CSR and Carroll's Pyramid

**In one line**
Corporate social responsibility is a firm's effort to benefit society beyond profit. Carroll's pyramid ranks it: economic, legal, ethical, then philanthropic.

**Explain it simply**
**CSR** (Module 2): giving back to the community and reducing harm, through donations, renewable energy, sustainable materials, employee volunteering and scholarships.

**Carroll's pyramid**, from the base up:
1. **Economic:** be profitable. It's the foundation.
2. **Legal:** obey the law.
3. **Ethical:** do what's right, fair and just, even beyond the law.
4. **Philanthropic:** be a good corporate citizen; give to the community.

**Why CSR matters** (Module 2): brand reputation, attracting talent, stakeholder relationships, sustainable development, lower legal risk and innovation.

**How to do it well:** link CSR to the company's core values and business, set measurable goals (for example "cut the carbon footprint 10% in three years"), and **measure and report** progress.

**The PR question** (the Madrakuta case in the slides): critics say corporate giving can be "buying good publicity". The test is whether CSR is **integrated and measured**, or just advertised.

**The everyday example**
A **₹2 crore**-revenue Kolkata garment unit:
- pays all workers on time (**economic**);
- follows labour and fire-safety rules (**legal**);
- pays above minimum wage and offers **₹5,000** festival advances interest-free (**ethical**);
- sponsors a local school's uniforms each year (**philanthropic**).

Skipping the bottom layers but publicising only the school uniforms would be CSR as PR.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jul 2024)
In its **2024 Environmental Report** (July 2024), **Google** said its greenhouse-gas emissions had risen about **48% since 2019**, including **13% in 2023 alone**, mainly from **data-centre energy** for AI and from its supply chain.
It said its goal of **net-zero by 2030** was "extremely ambitious" and would be hard to meet.
It's a test of CSR credibility: big public pledges must be matched by measured, reported progress, even when the numbers go the wrong way.
*Sources: Google 2024 Environmental Report; NBC/CNBC, Fast Company, Jul 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is CSR just marketing?"*

**Strong answer:** "It can be, if it's only donations publicised for image. But real CSR sits on Carroll's whole pyramid: profitable, law-abiding, ethical in daily operations, and then philanthropic.
The signs of real CSR are that it's linked to the core business, has measurable goals, and is reported honestly, including setbacks, as when Google disclosed its emissions rising 48%. I'd judge a company's CSR by its operations, not its advertisements."

**Common trap**
Thinking CSR **is** philanthropy. In Carroll's model, philanthropy is just the **top** layer. A firm that donates generously but cheats customers or workers isn't socially responsible.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What are the four levels of Carroll's CSR pyramid, from the bottom? **Answer: Economic, legal, ethical, philanthropic.**
2. *(Application)* A company sets a goal to "cut water use per unit by 15% in two years" and reports progress annually. Which good CSR practice is this? **Answer: Setting measurable goals and measuring and reporting progress.**
3. *(Mini case)* A firm donates ₹10 crore to charity and advertises it widely, while facing complaints of unpaid overtime in its factories. Evaluate its CSR.
   **Answer: Weak.** It skips the legal and ethical layers (fair pay, labour law) and leans on the philanthropic layer for image. Real CSR would fix how workers are treated first.

**Source:** `Module 2,LMS.pptx` slides 5–10; `concept of buisness ethics.(Module-1).pptx` slide 22 (Madrakuta case); Fernando (Carroll's model)

## Card E15 · India's CSR Law: Section 135

**In one line**
India is one of the few countries that legally requires large companies to spend on CSR: at least 2% of average net profits under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Explain it simply**
**Who it applies to:** a company that, in the preceding financial year, had:
- net worth of **₹500 crore** or more; or
- turnover of **₹1,000 crore** or more; or
- net profit of **₹5 crore** or more.

**What it must do:**
- Spend at least **2% of its average net profit of the three immediately preceding financial years** on CSR.
- Use activities listed in **Schedule VII**: hunger, health, education, gender equality, environment, sports, rural development, PM's relief funds and so on.
- Form a **CSR committee** of the board (it can be skipped if the obligation is ₹50 lakh or less) and disclose a CSR policy.

**Unspent money:**
- If it relates to an **ongoing project**, it moves to an **Unspent CSR Account** within 30 days and must be spent within three years.
- Otherwise it goes to a Schedule VII fund within six months.
- Since the 2021 amendments, failing to comply attracts **penalties**.

Spending on the company's **own employees** or on political parties **doesn't count**.

**The everyday example**
A company's net profits for the last three years were **₹40 crore, ₹50 crore and ₹60 crore**. The average is **₹50 crore**, so its minimum CSR spend this year is **2% × ₹50 crore = ₹1 crore**.
If it runs a 3-year school project and spends only ₹70 lakh this year, the unspent **₹30 lakh** goes to an Unspent CSR Account, to be used on that project within three years.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (FY24 data, reported 2024–25)
Reports on **FY 2023–24** data showed **NSE-listed companies** spent about **₹17,967 crore** on CSR, up **16%** from ₹15,524 crore the year before.
- **HDFC Bank** was the top spender at about **₹945 crore**, followed by Reliance Industries (about ₹900 crore) and TCS (about ₹827 crore).
- Wider estimates covering more companies put India Inc.'s total near **₹30,000–35,000 crore**.

This is mandated CSR at scale: the 2% rule turns social spending into a measurable, reported obligation.
*Sources: Vajiram & Ravi / Adda247 summaries of NSE data; RED Lab via Exchange4media; CareEdge via Business Today.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is mandatory CSR a good idea?"*

**Strong answer:** "It has clear benefits. India's 2% rule has channelled tens of thousands of crores a year into education, health and rural development, and made CSR measurable and reported.
But mandating it can turn CSR into compliance: spending to tick the 2% box rather than for impact, or treating it as separate from how the business runs. The best companies use Section 135 as a floor and link CSR to their core strengths, like a bank funding financial literacy."

**Common trap**
Calculating 2% of **this year's** profit. It's 2% of the **average net profit of the preceding three years**.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What minimum CSR spend does Section 135 require? **Answer: 2% of the average net profit of the three immediately preceding financial years.**
2. *(Application)* A company's net worth is ₹300 crore, its turnover ₹800 crore and its net profit ₹6 crore. Does Section 135 apply? **Answer: Yes.** Net profit of ₹5 crore or more alone triggers it.
3. *(Mini case)* A company spends its CSR money on a gym for its own staff and on sponsoring its own product launch event. Does this count?
   **Answer: No.** Activities that benefit only the company's employees, or that are really marketing, don't qualify as CSR under the rules. The money must go to Schedule VII activities for the public.

**Source:** Companies Act, 2013 Sec 135 and Schedule VII; Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, as amended in 2021; `Module 2,LMS.pptx` (CSR)
**Status:** DRAFT
