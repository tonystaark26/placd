## Card M1 · Needs, Wants & Demands

**In one line**
A need is a basic requirement, a want is the specific form it takes, and demand is a want you can actually pay for.

**Explain it simply**
- **Need:** a basic human requirement, such as food, transport or belonging.
- **Want:** that need shaped by culture, society and personality. Everyone needs transport; *you* want an SUV.
- **Demand:** a want backed by **purchasing power and willingness to buy**.

Marketers don't create needs; they influence how needs get expressed as wants, and then
make those wants affordable enough to become demand.

**The everyday example**
It's 1 pm and you're hungry: that's the **need**. You'd love the ₹250 burger at the café:
that's the **want**. You have ₹120 in your pocket, so you buy the ₹80 canteen thali:
that's the **demand**. The café's real market is people who want the burger *and* can pay ₹250.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (FY2025)
Utility vehicles, mostly SUVs plus some MPVs, made up about **65% of India's passenger
vehicle sales in FY2025**, up from around 60% a year earlier (SIAM data).
The **need** (getting around) hasn't changed. The **want** has shifted towards
SUVs, and rising incomes plus easy car loans turned that want into **demand**.
*Source: SIAM data via DTNext, April 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Does marketing create needs?"*

**Strong answer:** "No. Needs like hunger or safety already exist. Marketing shapes the
**want**, for example which brand or what kind of product, and helps turn it into **demand**
through pricing, financing and availability. India's shift to SUVs is a good example:
people always needed transport, but marketing and rising incomes shaped *how* they
want to meet that need."

**Common trap**
Treating "want" and "demand" as the same thing. A want without purchasing power is not demand, so it doesn't count when estimating market size.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* A student wants an iPhone but can't afford one. Is this demand? **Answer: No.** It's a want without purchasing power.
2. *(Application)* A bank launches easy EMIs for two-wheelers. Which stage is it targeting?
   **Answer: Turning wants into demand** by increasing purchasing power.
3. *(Mini case)* A premium gym in Kolkata gets thousands of Instagram likes but few sign-ups. What's the likely gap?
   **Answer:** High **want** but low **demand**. People like the idea but either can't afford or aren't willing to pay the price. Fixes: trial passes, monthly plans, student pricing.

## Card M2 · Customer Value & Value Proposition

**In one line**
Customer value = perceived benefits − perceived costs, and the value proposition says why a customer should choose you over the alternatives.

**Explain it simply**
- **Benefits** can be functional (it works), emotional (it feels good), social (it says
  something about me) or experiential (it's an experience).
- **Costs** are not just money. They include **time, effort, psychological cost and risk**.
- Value is **relative**: customers compare you with the alternatives, so a "better"
  product isn't automatically better value.
- A **value proposition** answers one question: *why should this customer choose us
  rather than an alternative?* For example: IKEA = design + affordability + self-service;
  Volvo = safety.

**The everyday example**
Chai at the gate stall costs **₹10** and takes 2 minutes. The café's cappuccino costs **₹180**,
with AC, Wi-Fi and a 15-minute wait. For a quick break between classes, the stall gives more
value, because benefits are similar and costs are lower. For a 2-hour group study session,
the café wins, because seating and Wi-Fi are worth the extra ₹170. **Same customer,
different situation, different value.**

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov–Dec 2025)
In November 2025, **Zepto removed handling and surge fees** and made delivery free on
orders above ₹99, while a rival still charged around ₹54 in combined fees on small orders.
Cutting the *cost* side of the equation raises customer value without changing the product.
In December 2025, the CCPA (consumer regulator) ordered Zepto to stop **adding charges only
at checkout**. Hidden costs raise the *psychological* cost and damage trust.
*Sources: Business Today, 3 Nov 2025; Inventiva, Dec 2025, on the CCPA order.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Can a technically superior product create less customer value?"*

**Strong answer:** "Yes. Value is benefits minus costs, judged against the alternatives.
A phone with a 200 MP camera may be technically superior, but if the customer cares more
about battery life and price, a cheaper phone gives *more* value. That's why marketers
start with what the customer values, not with what the engineers can build."

**Common trap**
Counting only price as cost. Time, effort and risk are costs too. That's why "10-minute delivery" sells even when prices are higher.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Customer value is… (a) price paid (b) perceived benefits − perceived costs (c) product quality (d) brand awareness
   **Answer: (b).**
2. *(Application)* Name two non-money costs a customer pays when shopping at a crowded mall on a Sunday.
   **Answer:** For example, **time** (travel and queues) and **effort** (parking, crowds).
3. *(Mini case)* A café raises coffee prices by ₹20 but adds free Wi-Fi and charging points, and its student footfall *rises*. Explain using customer value.
   **Answer:** Perceived benefits (work-friendly space) rose more than perceived costs (₹20), so net value went *up* for students who study there.

## Card M3 · Customer Satisfaction & Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)

**In one line**
Satisfaction = performance compared with expectations, and CLV is what a loyal customer is worth over the whole relationship, not just one sale.

**Explain it simply**
- **Performance < expectations → dissatisfaction.** Equal → satisfaction. Better → **delight**.
- Expectations come from **ads, reviews, word of mouth, past experience, price and brand
  promises**. So marketing creates the promise, and the product and service must keep it.
- **The chain:** value → satisfaction → repeat purchase → loyalty → **customer lifetime
  value** → customer equity (the total value of all customers).
- The manager's question shifts from *"did we make this sale?"* to *"what is this
  relationship worth over time?"*

**The everyday example**
A regular at the canteen spends ₹80 a day, and the canteen makes **₹20 profit** on it.
Over 22 college days a month for 2 years: ₹20 × 22 × 24 = **₹10,560**.
Serve that student a stale samosa and lose them, and the canteen hasn't lost ₹20; it has
lost **₹10,560**, plus everyone they tell. (The notes use the same logic: ₹300 × 4
purchases a month × 36 months = ₹43,200.)

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Oct 2024)
Ola Electric led India's electric scooter market, but service complaints piled up. On
**7 October 2024**, the CCPA issued it a show-cause notice citing over **10,000 consumer
complaints**. Reports showed its monthly market share falling from about **39% in July
to about 27% in September 2024**. Performance fell below expectations, dissatisfaction
spread publicly, and future customers were lost along with existing ones.
*Sources: Entrackr, Oct 2024; YourStory, Dec 2024; company statement on CCPA complaints.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Which customer is more valuable: a one-time big spender or a loyal small spender?"*

**Strong answer:** "Usually the loyal one. A one-time ₹10,000 purchase is worth ₹10,000.
A loyal customer spending ₹1,000 a month for three years is worth ₹36,000 in revenue, costs
less to serve because there's no acquisition cost again, and brings in referrals. That's
customer lifetime value. The exception is when the big spender also has high repeat
potential."

**Common trap**
Thinking "delight" just means over-promising. If ads promise too much, expectations rise and the *same* performance now produces dissatisfaction. Under-promise and over-deliver.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Performance exceeds expectations. The customer feels… **Answer: Delight.**
2. *(Application)* A customer contributes ₹200 profit per purchase, buys twice a month and stays 2 years. CLV before adjustments?
   **Answer:** ₹200 × 2 × 24 = **₹9,600.**
3. *(Mini case)* A new app's ads promise "delivery in 10 minutes, every time", but deliveries average 18 minutes. Customers are angry, even though 18 minutes is faster than most rivals. Why?
   **Answer:** Satisfaction compares performance with **expectations**, not with competitors. The ad set expectations at 10 minutes, so 18 feels like failure.

**Source:** `Introduction to Marketing Management.pdf` slides 12–14

## Card M4 · Marketing Orientations & Marketing Myopia

**In one line**
There are five philosophies of where a business should start: production, product, selling, marketing and societal. Starting with the customer's need, not the product, avoids myopia.

**Explain it simply**
| Orientation | Starts with… | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Production** | Make it cheap and widely available | Ignores what customers want |
| **Product** | Make the best product | Features nobody asked for |
| **Selling** | Persuade people to buy | Short-term sales, no loyalty |
| **Marketing** | Find the customer's need, then build value | — |
| **Societal marketing** | Customer + company + long-term welfare of society | — |

**Marketing myopia** (Theodore Levitt): defining your business by the *product* instead of
the *customer need*. Railways that saw themselves as "train companies" rather than
"transport companies" lost out to cars and planes.

**The everyday example: the canteen test**
- *Production:* "How do we make a samosa for ₹5?"
- *Product:* "How do we make the best samosa in Kolkata?"
- *Selling:* "How do we push samosa + chai combos?"
- *Marketing:* "What do students actually want at 1 pm?" → *A quick, filling lunch before the 1:30 class.*
- *Societal:* "How do we do that without plastic plates and food waste?"

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Feb–Mar 2025)
In February 2025, **Zomato Ltd renamed itself "Eternal Ltd"**, and shareholders approved it in
March 2025. Its quick-commerce arm **Blinkit** had become a bigger growth driver than food
delivery, alongside District (going-out) and Hyperpure (restaurant supplies). The Zomato
app kept its name, but the company stopped defining itself by one product. That's the
opposite of myopia: it defines its business by broader customer needs, not "food delivery".
*Sources: TechCrunch, 6 Feb 2025; Angel One; People Matters.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What is marketing myopia? Give a recent example."*

**Strong answer:** "It's defining your business too narrowly, by your product rather than
by the customer need you serve, so you miss new competitors and opportunities. The fix
is outside-in thinking: *what problem do we solve, for whom, better than whom?* Zomato
becoming Eternal is a recent example of avoiding it: it saw itself as serving everyday
convenience, not just food delivery, and Blinkit became its biggest growth engine."

**Common trap**
Thinking a *selling* orientation is the same as *marketing*. Selling starts with the product and pushes it out; marketing starts with the customer and pulls the product in.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* "Consumers won't buy enough unless persuaded" describes which orientation? **Answer: Selling.**
2. *(Application)* A phone brand adds a 200 MP camera, but its target buyers care more about battery life. Which trap is this?
   **Answer: Product orientation, leading to marketing myopia.** A superior feature isn't a superior value proposition.
3. *(Mini case)* A paint company sees itself as being in "the paint business". What would a non-myopic definition be, and what new opportunities does it open?
   **Answer:** "Helping people create beautiful, lasting homes." That opens up waterproofing, home décor, painting services and design consultation.

**Source:** `Introduction to Marketing Management.pdf` slides 16–23

## Card M5 · The Marketing Environment: Micro & Macro (PESTLE)

**In one line**
Marketing decisions are shaped by nearby actors (the microenvironment) and by big societal forces (the macroenvironment) that no company controls.

**Explain it simply**
- **Microenvironment (close to the firm):** the company itself, suppliers, intermediaries
  (distributors, retailers, quick-commerce apps), customers, competitors and publics
  (media, government, local communities, social media).
- **Macroenvironment (PESTLE):** **P**olitical, **E**conomic, **S**ocial, **T**echnological,
  **L**egal, **E**nvironmental.
- A marketer can't control these forces, only **scan → analyse → identify → respond**.
- Key idea: **the same change is an opportunity for one firm and a threat to another.**
  AI threatens a traditional ad agency but helps an AI-first one.

**The everyday example**
Your favourite Kolkata café:
- **Micro:** its milk supplier, Swiggy and Zomato, *you*, the café next door, and the local residents' association.
- **Macro:** milk prices rising with inflation (Economic), GST rates (Political), the
  plastic-straw ban (Environmental/Legal), UPI payments (Technological), students
  working from cafés (Social).

If your pocket money stays at ₹5,000 but prices rise 6%, your *real* spending power has
fallen, so the café's ₹150 coffee feels pricier even though nothing at the café changed.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov 2025)
In November 2025, the government notified the **Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP)
Rules**. The main obligations, including clear notice and consent before collecting
personal data and reporting data breaches, apply after **18 months**, around May 2027.
That's a **Legal** force reshaping marketing: every app, loyalty program and personalised ad
must now be built around consent.
*Sources: The Week / PTI, 14 Nov 2025; AZB & Partners and Mondaq legal summaries.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Do a quick PESTLE analysis of electric vehicles in India."*

**Strong answer:** "**Political:** government incentives and industrial policy. **Economic:**
fuel prices, financing, total cost of ownership. **Social:** urban buyers changing how
they travel. **Technological:** batteries, charging infrastructure, software. **Legal:**
safety and emission rules. **Environmental:** climate concerns. The point is that these
forces act *together*, so carmakers must change product, pricing, communication and
charging partnerships at the same time."

**Common trap**
Listing PESTLE factors as a checklist. Interviewers want the **"so what"**: what each force means for the company's decisions.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Are competitors part of the micro- or macroenvironment? **Answer: Microenvironment.**
2. *(Application)* Rising edible-oil prices hit an FMCG company. Name three marketing responses from the notes.
   **Answer:** For example, **raise prices, shrink pack sizes, reformulate, or revise promotions.**
3. *(Mini case)* A fashion app collects users' location, browsing and purchase history to personalise ads. Under DPDP, what must it change?
   **Answer:** Get **clear, informed consent** for what it collects and why, give proper notice, protect the data and report breaches. Personalisation is still possible, but only built on trust and consent.

**Source:** `Marketing Environment.pdf` slides 02–18, 22

## Card M6 · Porter's Five Forces & Competitive Advantage

**In one line**
Five forces decide how attractive an industry's profits are: rivalry, new entrants, supplier power, buyer power and substitutes.

**Explain it simply**
1. **Rivalry** is high when there are many similar competitors, slow growth and low switching costs.
2. **Threat of new entrants** is low when barriers are high: capital, scale, brand, distribution, network effects.
3. **Supplier power** is high when there are few suppliers and their input is critical and hard to replace.
4. **Buyer power** is high when customers have many alternatives, easy information and low switching costs.
5. **Substitutes** are *different products solving the same need*. Tea is a substitute for coffee, and so is a nap.

**Competitive advantage:** a feature is only an advantage if customers value it **and**
competitors can't easily copy it. Apple's advantage is its whole *system* (brand,
ecosystem, services), not the phone's specs.

**The everyday example: the photocopy shop at the college gate**
- **Rivalry:** three shops side by side, all charging ₹1–2 a page.
- **New entrants:** anyone with a printer can start one, so barriers are low.
- **Suppliers:** paper and toner are available everywhere, so supplier power is low.
- **Buyers:** students compare ₹1 vs ₹2 instantly and switch, so buyer power is high.
- **Substitutes:** PDFs on phones, the biggest threat of all.

Verdict: an **unattractive** industry, unless a shop builds an advantage such as binding,
late-night hours or WhatsApp ordering.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2024–26)
India's quick-commerce market shows all five forces at once. **Blinkit** leads (about 3.4–3.6
million daily orders), followed by **Zepto** and **Swiggy Instamart**. Then big players
entered: **Flipkart Minutes** launched in August 2024 and passed 1,000 dark stores in under
two years, and **Amazon** joined with **Amazon Now**. Rivalry is intense, buyers can switch
apps in seconds, and the main entry barrier is the capital needed for dark stores.
*Sources: Business Today, 8 Aug 2024; TechCrunch (Datum Intelligence estimates); Inc42.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is quick commerce an attractive industry? Use Five Forces."*

**Strong answer:** "Rivalry is very high: Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart and now Flipkart and
Amazon compete on speed and discounts. Buyer power is high, because switching apps costs
nothing. The threat of new entrants is moderate: dark stores need heavy capital, but deep-pocketed
players like Flipkart and Amazon can afford it. Substitutes include kirana stores and
regular e-commerce. So it's a tough industry for profits, and winners will need real
advantages such as density of dark stores, private labels and loyalty programs."

**Common trap**
Defining substitutes as "similar products". A substitute solves the **same need** in a different way. Netflix competes with Instagram and sleep for your time, not just with Prime Video.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which force is high when switching costs are low and information is easily available? **Answer: Bargaining power of buyers.**
2. *(Application)* Only two companies in the world make an advanced chip that phone makers need. Which force is strong? **Answer: Supplier power.**
3. *(Mini case)* A coffee chain only tracks other coffee chains as competitors. What is it missing?
   **Answer:** **Substitutes defined by need**: tea, energy drinks, soft drinks, even "a place to sit with friends". Its real competitive set is much broader.
