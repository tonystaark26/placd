## Card M7 · Four Types of Buying Behaviour

**In one line**
How people buy depends on two things: how involved they are, and how different the brands look to them.

**Explain it simply**
| | Brands look **different** | Brands look **similar** |
|---|---|---|
| **High involvement** | **Complex buying**: lots of research and comparison | **Dissonance-reducing**: decide fairly quickly, worry afterwards |
| **Low involvement** | **Variety-seeking**: switch just for novelty | **Habitual**: buy the familiar one without thinking |

**Involvement** isn't just about price. It's about **risk, personal relevance and consequences**.
What the marketer should do differs for each:
- **Complex:** explain the differences.
- **Dissonance-reducing:** reassure the customer *after* purchase.
- **Habitual:** be available everywhere.
- **Variety-seeking:** give people reasons to try.

**The everyday example**
In one month, a hostel student buys:
- a **₹10** Parle-G: *habitual*, the same pack every time.
- a **₹20** chips packet in a new flavour: *variety-seeking*, just to try it.
- a **₹15,000** mattress: *dissonance-reducing*. They all look the same, so the student picks one quickly, then wonders "did I choose right?"
- a **₹70,000** laptop: *complex*. Weeks of reviews, specs and asking seniors.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sept 2025)
On **2 September 2025**, Apple opened **Apple Hebbal** in Bengaluru, its third store in India
and its first in South India. An iPhone or MacBook is a classic **complex purchase**: high price,
high personal relevance, real brand differences. A store where buyers can handle devices,
compare models and ask questions lowers the **perceived risk** that makes complex buyers hesitate.
*Sources: Apple Newsroom via Business Wire; Storyboard18, Sept 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"How would your marketing differ for a mattress versus a laptop?"*

**Strong answer:** "Both are high-involvement, but buyers see laptop brands as clearly different,
so that's complex buying: I'd help them compare, with spec guides, demos and reviews. Mattresses
look similar, so buyers decide fast and then doubt themselves. That's dissonance-reducing, so I'd
focus on *after* the sale: a 100-night trial, easy returns, follow-up calls, and reviews from
happy customers."

**Common trap**
Assuming involvement just means "expensive". A ₹500 baby food can be very high-involvement because the risk to the baby feels high.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Low involvement + significant brand differences = ? **Answer: Variety-seeking buying.**
2. *(Application)* For habitual products like salt, what matters most to the marketer? **Answer: Availability and easy recognition**: distribution, distinctive packaging, repetition.
3. *(Mini case)* An insurance company finds that many customers cancel within 15 days of buying a policy. Which buying type is this, and what's the fix?
   **Answer: Dissonance-reducing.** Policies look similar, so buyers doubt their choice afterwards. The fix is post-purchase reassurance: a welcome call, a clear benefits summary, and testimonials.

## Card M8 · The Consumer Decision Process (and Why It's No Longer a Straight Line)

**In one line**
Need recognition → information search → evaluation → purchase → post-purchase. Today, buyers loop back and forth between the stages.

**Explain it simply**
1. **Need recognition:** a gap between where you are and where you want to be ("my laptop is too slow").
2. **Information search:** personal (friends), commercial (ads, sales staff), public (reviews,
   comparisons) and experiential (trying it in a store).
3. **Evaluation:** buyers weigh attributes differently. Some *trade off* (a weaker battery is OK
   if the camera is great); others use *cut-offs* (no 5G, no deal).
4. **Purchase decision:** **intention isn't purchase**. Stock-outs, EMI offers or family opinion can change it at the last minute.
5. **Post-purchase:** satisfaction, reviews, repeat purchase, or doubt ("dissonance").

Digital journeys loop: **showrooming** (look in the store, buy online) and **webrooming** (research online, buy in the store).

**The everyday example: a ₹15,000 phone**
You see an Instagram reel (need) → watch 3 YouTube reviews (search) → compare 2 models on
Flipkart (evaluation) → visit the store to hold both (webrooming) → buy the *other* model because
it has a ₹1,000 exchange offer (intention changed) → post a 4-star review (post-purchase), which
becomes the next buyer's information search.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (FY2025)
**Nykaa** started online, but ended FY2025 with **237 physical beauty stores in 79 cities**,
having added about 50 stores that year. Beauty buyers discover products on Instagram and in
reviews, but many still want to **test the shade or smell** before buying. Stores serve
the evaluation stage that screens can't.
*Sources: Nykaa Q4 FY25 results coverage (Business Standard, May 2025); Nykaa AGM presentation 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why would an online-first brand open physical stores?"*

**Strong answer:** "Because buyers don't move in a straight line from search to purchase. In
categories like beauty, discovery happens online but evaluation needs touch and trial, so
stores reduce risk and lift conversion, and they make the online channel stronger too. Nykaa
reaching 237 stores is a good example. The brand has to feel like one experience across
every touchpoint."

**Common trap**
Treating purchase *intention* as a sale. Plenty of things happen between "I'll buy this" and paying: stock, price, EMI, delivery time, family.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Researching online, then buying in a store, is called… **Answer: Webrooming.**
2. *(Application)* A buyer rejects every laptop under 16 GB RAM, however good the other features are. Which evaluation rule is this?
   **Answer: A cut-off (non-compensatory) rule.**
3. *(Mini case)* An electronics brand sees high traffic on its product page but low sales. Using the decision process, where would you look first?
   **Answer:** At **evaluation and purchase**: price compared with rivals, reviews, stock availability, EMI or exchange offers, delivery time. Traffic shows interest; something is blocking the final step.

**Source:** `Understanding Consumer and Business Markets.pdf` slides 12–28

## Card M9 · B2B Buying: The Buying Centre & Buying Situations

**In one line**
When businesses buy, many people decide together, and the effort depends on whether it's a routine reorder or a first-time purchase.

**Explain it simply**
**Buying centre:** everyone who influences an organisation's purchase.
| Role | Who, typically |
|---|---|
| **Initiator** | Spots the need |
| **User** | Will actually use it |
| **Influencer** | Shapes the specifications (IT, technical experts) |
| **Decider** | Makes the final choice |
| **Approver** | Authorises the spend |
| **Buyer** | Negotiates and places the order (procurement) |
| **Gatekeeper** | Controls which vendors and information get through |

**Buying situations:** a **straight rebuy** is a routine reorder; a **modified rebuy** changes
the specs, price or supplier; a **new task** is a first-time purchase with the most research and risk.
**Derived demand:** business demand depends on *end-consumer* demand. Car-part makers sell more only when people buy more cars.

**The everyday example: total cost, not price**
A college is choosing between two projectors.
- **A** costs **₹40,000** and needs an **₹8,000** lamp every year for 5 years: **₹80,000** in total.
- **B** costs **₹55,000** but has an LED with no lamp to replace: **₹55,000** in total.

The cheaper invoice is the *costlier* choice. B2B buyers judge **total cost of ownership**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (FY2024-25)
India's **Government e-Marketplace (GeM)**, the government's online procurement platform,
recorded about **₹5.4 lakh crore** in purchases in FY2024-25, a record. Micro and small
enterprises (MSEs) won about **37%** of the platform's cumulative order value, well above the
mandated 25%. This is B2B buying at scale: formal specifications, registered suppliers and
transparent comparison.
*Sources: AIR News / PIB, 8–9 Aug 2025; Outlook Business, Aug 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"How is selling to a company different from selling to a consumer?"*

**Strong answer:** "Three big differences. First, many people decide, not one: users care about
ease of use, IT about compatibility, finance about total cost, and procurement about terms, so
you must create value for each. Second, it's more formal, with specifications, proposals and
negotiation. Third, it's relationship-driven: after-sales service and reliability decide whether
you get the repeat order. And demand is *derived*, so I'd watch the customer's own market."

**Common trap**
Pitching only to the person who signs the order. In B2B, the user or the IT gatekeeper can quietly kill a deal the decider liked.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* A hospital reorders the same gloves from the same supplier every month. Which buying situation? **Answer: Straight rebuy.**
2. *(Application)* Demand for steel rises because car sales are booming. What concept is this? **Answer: Derived demand.**
3. *(Mini case)* You sell CRM software to a large company. The sales head loves your demo, but the deal stalls for months. Who in the buying centre should you engage?
   **Answer:** The **IT team** (influencer and gatekeeper: security, integration), **finance** (total cost and approval) and **procurement** (buyer: terms). Winning the user isn't enough.

**Source:** `Understanding Consumer and Business Markets.pdf` slides 45–63

## Card M10 · Market Measurement & Demand Estimation

**In one line**
Before deciding anything, size the market, and always define exactly *which* market: product × customer × place × time.

**Explain it simply**
- **Market demand:** what a defined group of customers would buy, in a given place and period.
- **Market potential:** the upper limit if the whole industry marketed as hard as possible.
- **Company demand / sales forecast:** *your* realistic share of that.
- A useful definition is specific: "premium electric SUVs in Bengaluru in FY2027", not "the EV market".
- **Chain-ratio method:** start with a base number and multiply through the filters.
  From the notes: 1.2 crore urban households × 28% buy premium coffee × 35% would try specialty
  coffee × 8 packs a year × ₹450 ≈ **₹423 crore**.

**The everyday example: cold coffee at your college**
2,000 students × 60% drink coffee × 40% would buy cold coffee × 3 cups a week × ₹50
= **₹72,000 a week**. Over 30 college weeks, that's **₹21.6 lakh a year** of market
potential. If your stall can realistically win 25%, your sales forecast is about **₹5.4 lakh**.
Change any one assumption and the answer moves, so always test optimistic, base and pessimistic cases.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (2025)
How big is India's digital advertising market? An **ET BrandEquity–Ipsos** report says about
**₹49,000 crore (44% of all ad spend)** for FY2025, but a **dentsu–e4m** report says about
**₹71,621 crore (59%)** for calendar 2025. Both are respected reports. The gap comes from
**definitions**: financial year versus calendar year, and what counts as "advertising". The
lesson for every market-sizing question: **define before you measure.**
*Sources: Ipsos India, "State of Digital Marketing in India 2025-26"; dentsu–e4m Digital Report via exchange4media.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Estimate the annual market for cold coffee in Kolkata."* (a guesstimate)

**Strong answer:** "First I'd define it: say, ready-to-drink and café cold coffee, urban Kolkata,
per year. Then chain ratios: about 1.5 crore people → share in the 15–40 age group → share who
drink coffee → share who drink it cold → cups a month × months of warm weather × average price.
I'd state each assumption out loud and give a range, not one number. The structure matters
more than the exact answer."

**Common trap**
Quoting one market-size number without saying *which* market and *which* period, or confusing **market potential** (the ceiling) with **your sales forecast** (your realistic share).

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* The upper limit of industry sales as marketing effort becomes very large is called… **Answer: Market potential.**
2. *(Application)* 10,000 households × 20% buy × 4 packs a year × ₹100. What's the market? **Answer: ₹8,00,000 (₹8 lakh).**
3. *(Mini case)* Two consultants give a client very different market sizes for the same product. What's the first thing you check?
   **Answer:** Their **definitions**: product scope, customer group, geography, time period (FY vs calendar year) and assumptions. Most gaps come from defining different markets.

**Source:** `Market Measurement Research.pdf` slides 05–12

## Card M11 · Marketing Research: From Data to Insight

**In one line**
Research starts with the decision you need to make, not with a questionnaire, and its goal is insight, not just data.

**Explain it simply**
**Process:** define the problem and the decision → plan the research → collect → analyse → present → decide.
- **Secondary data** already exists (sales records, Census, industry reports, Google Trends). It's
  fast and cheap, but may be outdated or a poor fit. **Primary data** is collected fresh
  (interviews, surveys, experiments). It fits your question exactly, but costs time and money.
- **Qualitative** research answers *why* and *how*, with small samples (interviews, focus groups).
  **Quantitative** research answers *how many* and *how much*, with large samples (surveys, A/B tests).
- **Data → information → insight:** "repeat orders are 18% lower when the first delivery was late"
  is *information*. "First-delivery reliability builds trust, so fix fulfilment before spending on
  discounts" is an **insight**, because it changes a decision.

**The everyday example**
The canteen's samosa sales fell 30%. A weak response is "let's run a survey." A strong response:
1. **Check existing data first:** did the price go up from ₹10 to ₹15? Did a new café open nearby?
2. **Talk to 10 students** (qualitative) to learn *why*.
3. **Survey 200** (quantitative) to learn *how many* feel that way.
4. **Test a fix:** ₹12 samosas in one counter for a week, compared with the other counter.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (June 2025)
On **16 June 2025**, the government notified India's next **Census**, with a reference date of
**1 March 2027** (October 2026 for snow-bound areas). It will include caste enumeration. The last
Census was in **2011**, so for over a decade marketers have sized markets from 15-year-old
**secondary data** plus estimates. Census 2027 will reset many market-size calculations.
*Sources: Registrar General notification, 16 June 2025; WION and Deccan Herald reports.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Our sales are falling. What research would you do?"*

**Strong answer:** "I wouldn't jump to a survey. First I'd define the decision: are we deciding
on price, product, channel or communication? Then I'd check internal data to see which segments,
regions and channels are falling. Next, qualitative interviews to understand why, then a survey
to size the main reasons, and finally a small experiment to test the fix. Secondary data first,
primary data only for what's still uncertain."

**Common trap**
Treating search interest or likes as market size, or assuming **correlation means causation**. Ice-cream sales and AC sales rise together, but neither causes the other; summer causes both.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* "Why do first-time users not return?" Qualitative or quantitative? **Answer: Qualitative first** (interviews, journey observation), then quantitative to size it.
2. *(Application)* Which landing page converts better, A or B? Best method? **Answer: A randomised A/B experiment.**
3. *(Mini case)* A manager says: "Our survey of 5,000 Instagram followers shows 90% love our new flavour, so let's launch nationally." What's wrong?
   **Answer:** **Sampling bias.** Followers already like the brand, so they don't represent the whole market. A large biased sample can still be confidently wrong.

## Card M12 · Marketing Ethics: Dark Patterns, Greenwashing & Disclosure

**In one line**
Legal isn't the same as ethical. Good marketing informs a free choice; unethical marketing exploits an information gap.

**Explain it simply**
The key issues:
- **Deceptive advertising:** claims like "100% effective" with hidden conditions.
- **Manipulation:** deliberately exploiting fear, insecurity or vulnerability.
- **Dark patterns:** app designs that push you into choices you didn't intend: false urgency,
  pre-ticked add-ons, hard-to-cancel subscriptions, charges that appear only at checkout.
- **Privacy:** customers often don't know what data is collected, why, or who sees it.
- **Influencer disclosure:** paid posts must say so (#ad); otherwise people read them as honest opinions.
- **Greenwashing:** vague "eco-friendly" claims without evidence.

**Five tests for any decision:** Legal? Truthful? Transparent? Fair to the consumer? Would it survive the **front page** of a newspaper?

**The everyday example**
You add a ₹199 order to your cart. At checkout, a **₹99 membership is pre-ticked** and a
**₹15 "handling fee"** appears, so your total is now **₹313**. Nothing was hidden in the
small print, so it may be legal, but you were steered into spending **57% more** than you chose
to. That's a dark pattern, and India's consumer rules now prohibit it.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (June 2025)
On **5 June 2025**, India's consumer regulator, the **CCPA**, told all e-commerce platforms to
**self-audit for dark patterns within three months** and remove them. Its 2023 guidelines list
**13 banned practices**, including false urgency, basket sneaking (adding items you didn't choose)
and subscription traps. Pre-ticked consent boxes are not allowed.
*Sources: Storyboard18 and Outlook Money, June 2025; CCPA Guidelines on Dark Patterns, 2023.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"If something is legal, is it ethical enough for a brand to do it?"*

**Strong answer:** "No. Compliance is the floor, not the standard. A pre-ticked add-on or a
countdown timer that resets may have been legal once, but it exploits the customer and destroys
trust, which costs more than the extra revenue. I'd use five tests: legal, truthful, transparent,
fair, and the front-page test. India's 2025 dark-patterns crackdown shows the law is catching up
with what was already unethical."

**Common trap**
Mixing up **CSR** (a company's responsibility to society), **social marketing** (using marketing to change behaviour for good, like road-safety campaigns) and **cause-related marketing** ("₹1 from every purchase goes to…").

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* A shopping app adds an item to your cart that you didn't choose. Which dark pattern? **Answer: Basket sneaking.**
2. *(Application)* A brand says "eco-friendly packaging" with no details or evidence. What's the issue? **Answer: Greenwashing.** Claims must be specific and substantiated.
3. *(Mini case)* A fitness creator praises a protein powder without mentioning the brand paid them ₹50,000. The post is popular. What's the problem, and what's the fix?
   **Answer:** **Undisclosed paid promotion.** Followers think it's an independent opinion, which is misleading. Fix: a clear "#ad / paid partnership" label at the start, as advertising standards require.

**Source:** `Ethics and Social Responsibility in Marketing.pdf` slides 02–16 · `Emerging Trends in Marketing.pdf` slides 04, 10–11
