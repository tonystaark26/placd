## Card L17 · Contract of Sale: Sale vs Agreement to Sell

**In one line**
In a sale, ownership passes now. In an agreement to sell, it passes later or on a condition, and risk usually follows ownership.

**Explain it simply**
**Sec 4:** a contract of sale is one where the seller transfers, or agrees to transfer, ownership ("property") in **goods** to the buyer **for a price** in money.

**Goods (Sec 2(7))** means movable property, including shares, growing crops and things attached to land that are to be cut off before the sale. It **excludes** money and actionable claims, such as a debt owed to you.

**Kinds of goods:**
- **Existing goods:**
  - **specific:** identified and agreed at the time, like "this red scooter";
  - **unascertained:** described but not yet picked out, like "50 kg out of the 500 kg in the godown".
- **Future goods:** to be made or acquired after the contract.
- **Contingent goods:** future goods whose acquisition depends on an uncertain event.

| | Sale | Agreement to sell |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Executed | Executory |
| Ownership | Passes now | Passes later or on a condition |
| Risk (Sec 26) | On the buyer, even if the goods are still with the seller | On the seller |
| If the buyer won't pay | Seller can sue **for the price** | Seller can only sue **for damages** |
| Future goods? | Can't be "sold" now | Only an agreement to sell is possible |

**The everyday example**
- You pay **₹1,200** for a specific kurta at a New Market stall and ask the seller to keep it till evening. That's a **sale**. If the stall catches fire at 4 pm, the loss is **yours**, because ownership and risk have passed.
- You book a custom-made kurta for **₹1,800**, to be stitched by Friday. That's an **agreement to sell future goods**. If the tailor's shop burns down on Wednesday, the loss is the tailor's.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jul 2025)
On **15 July 2025**, **Tesla** opened its first Indian showroom in Mumbai's BKC.
- It listed the **Model Y** from **₹59.89 lakh**.
- It took bookings with a token payment of about **₹22,000**, plus a further payment to confirm.
- Deliveries were to start later.

A booking like this is an **agreement to sell**. Ownership, and with it risk, passes only when the car is made, appropriated to the buyer and delivered, not on the day the token is paid.
*Sources: CarDekho, Free Press Journal, e-vehicleinfo, Jul 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Why does it matter when ownership passes?"*

**Strong answer:** "Three things follow ownership:
1. **Risk:** under Section 26, risk usually passes with ownership, so whoever owns the goods bears the loss if they're destroyed.
2. **Remedies:** a seller can sue for the full price only after ownership has passed. Before that, only for damages.
3. **Insolvency:** if the buyer or seller goes bust, ownership decides whose creditors get the goods.

In supply contracts I'd always write down exactly when ownership and risk pass, and insure accordingly."

**Common trap**
Assuming the person holding the goods bears the risk. Risk usually follows **ownership**, not **possession**. A buyer who owns goods kept in the seller's shop bears the loss if they're destroyed.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Under Sec 2(7), are actionable claims (like a debt owed to you) "goods"? **Answer: No.** Goods exclude money and actionable claims.
2. *(Application)* A farmer agrees in March to sell 10 quintals of the wheat he will harvest in May. What kind of contract is this? **Answer: An agreement to sell future goods.**
3. *(Mini case)* A buyer pays ₹50,000 for a specific sofa and leaves it at the shop for delivery next week. A flood destroys it. Who bears the loss?
   **Answer: The buyer.** It's a sale of specific goods in a deliverable state, so ownership passed when the contract was made (Sec 20), and risk follows ownership (Sec 26). That's unless the shop was negligent or the contract said otherwise.

## Card L18 · Conditions vs Warranties

**In one line**
A condition goes to the heart of the deal: if broken, you can reject the goods. A warranty is a side promise: if broken, you only get damages.

**Explain it simply**
- **Condition (Sec 12(2)):** essential to the main purpose. Breach lets the buyer **reject the goods and end the contract**, and claim damages.
- **Warranty (Sec 12(3)):** collateral to the main purpose. Breach gives **only damages**.
- A buyer can choose to treat a breach of condition **as a breach of warranty** (Sec 13). If the buyer has **accepted** the goods, they usually can only claim damages.

**Implied conditions** (they apply even if no one says them):
- **Title (Sec 14):** the seller has the right to sell. *Rowland v Divall*: a buyer of a stolen car got his money back.
- **Description (Sec 15):** goods must match the description. *Varley v Whipp.*
- **Fitness for purpose (Sec 16(1)):** if you relied on the seller's skill for a stated purpose. *Grant v Australian Knitting Mills.*
- **Merchantable quality (Sec 16(2)):** fit to be sold under that description.
- **Sample (Sec 17):** the bulk must match the sample.
- **Wholesomeness** for food. *Frost v Aylesbury Dairy*: milk carrying typhoid germs.

**Implied warranties:** quiet possession, freedom from charges, and disclosure of dangerous goods.

**The everyday example**
You order an **iPhone 15** for ₹70,000 online and receive an **iPhone 13**. That's a breach of the **condition** as to description, so you can reject it and get a full refund.
If the right phone arrives but the free ₹1,000 earphones promised with it are missing, that's more like a **warranty**. You'd claim the earphones' value, not return the phone.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sep 2025)
On **18 September 2025**, the **Delhi State Consumer Commission** ordered **Mercedes-Benz India** and its dealer to **refund over ₹1.78 crore** for an **EQS 580** electric car, plus ₹5 lakh for mental agony.
- The battery pack had to be replaced about **five months** after purchase.
- Other defects followed, and the car sat in the workshop unrepaired.
- The Commission held them liable for a **manufacturing defect**.

A brand-new luxury car that can't be fixed fails the most basic condition, that goods are of **merchantable quality**. So the buyer got the remedy for a breach of condition: rejection and a refund, not just repairs.
*Sources: PTI via The Week, LiveLaw, IndiaLaw, Sep 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the difference between a condition and a warranty?"*

**Strong answer:** "A condition is essential to the contract's main purpose. Breach lets the buyer reject the goods and end the contract.
A warranty is collateral. Breach only gives damages.
Whether a term is a condition depends on the contract as a whole, not on the label. For a ₹70,000 phone, getting the right model is a condition; a missing promotional freebie is a warranty. And once a buyer has accepted the goods, a breach of condition usually drops to a damages claim."

**Common trap**
Thinking the word used in the contract decides it. A term called a "warranty" can still be a **condition** if it goes to the root of the deal (Sec 12(4)).

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What remedy does a breach of warranty give? **Answer: Only damages.** The buyer can't reject the goods.
2. *(Application)* You buy "basmati rice" and get ordinary rice in a basmati-labelled bag. Which implied condition is broken? **Answer: Correspondence with description** (Sec 15).
3. *(Mini case)* A buyer discovers that the second-hand car he bought for ₹4 lakh was stolen, and the police return it to the owner. What can he claim from the seller?
   **Answer: The full price back.** That's a breach of the implied condition as to title (Sec 14), as in Rowland v Divall. He's entitled to a refund even though he used the car for a while.

## Card L19 · Caveat Emptor: Let the Buyer Beware

**In one line**
The buyer must check that goods suit their purpose. But the law makes big exceptions when the buyer relies on the seller's skill, buys by description, or the goods aren't of merchantable quality.

**Explain it simply**
**Sec 16:** as a general rule there's **no implied condition** that goods are fit for any particular purpose. The buyer should examine the goods and decide.

**Exceptions** (where the seller *is* responsible):
1. **Fitness for purpose:** the buyer told the seller the purpose, relied on the seller's skill, and the seller deals in such goods.
2. **Merchantable quality:** goods bought by description from a dealer must be of saleable quality, unless the buyer examined them and the defect should have been obvious.
3. **Usage of trade:** the custom of the trade implies a quality.
4. **Fraud or concealment:** the seller hid a defect.
5. **Sale by sample, or by description and sample**, and wholesomeness for food.

Today, consumer protection law adds more protection on top of the Sale of Goods Act.

**The everyday example**
- At a Sunday second-hand market you buy a **₹600** table fan after looking at it. It turns out noisy. That's **caveat emptor**: you inspected it and the defect was discoverable.
- At an electronics shop you say "I need a fan that runs quietly in a baby's room" and buy the **₹2,800** fan the owner recommends. It's very noisy. That's the **fitness-for-purpose exception**, because you relied on his skill, so the shop is liable.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr–May 2024)
On **5 April 2024**, Hong Kong's food-safety regulator found **ethylene oxide**, a pesticide, above permitted limits in three **MDH** spice mixes and **Everest Fish Curry Masala**. Singapore ordered a recall of the Everest product. India's FSSAI then tested samples nationwide. In **May 2024** it reported **no ethylene oxide** in the 28 results it had received.
A shopper can't test spice powder at home, so caveat emptor gives way: food must be **merchantable and wholesome**, and regulators enforce it.
*Sources: The Week, The South First, BusinessWorld, Apr–May 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is 'let the buyer beware' still relevant today?"*

**Strong answer:** "The rule still exists in Section 16. Buyers should inspect what they can, especially in second-hand deals. But its exceptions now cover most everyday buying:
- if I rely on the seller's expertise for a stated purpose;
- if I buy by description from a dealer;
- if the item is food that must be wholesome.

Then the seller is responsible. Add the Consumer Protection Act, and in practice the burden has moved a long way toward 'let the seller beware'."

**Common trap**
Saying caveat emptor means the buyer is never protected. It only applies where the buyer had a fair chance to examine the goods and didn't rely on the seller. The exceptions are the heart of the topic.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What does caveat emptor mean? **Answer: "Let the buyer beware"**: the buyer must satisfy themselves that the goods suit their purpose (Sec 16).
2. *(Application)* A buyer tells a paint dealer "I need paint for an outdoor wall in heavy rain", buys what the dealer suggests, and it peels in a month. Does caveat emptor protect the dealer? **Answer: No.** It's the fitness-for-purpose exception: the buyer disclosed the purpose and relied on the dealer's skill.
3. *(Mini case)* A buyer inspects a used car, misses an obvious dent, buys it and then complains. Who wins?
   **Answer: Most likely the seller.** The buyer examined the car, and the defect was one the examination should have revealed. Caveat emptor applies, unless the seller actively hid the defect.

## Card L20 · Rights of an Unpaid Seller

**In one line**
A seller who hasn't been paid in full can hold the goods (lien), stop them in transit if the buyer is insolvent, resell in some cases, and sue the buyer.

**Explain it simply**
**Unpaid seller (Sec 45):** the whole price hasn't been paid or tendered, or a cheque or bill given as payment has bounced.

**Rights against the goods** (even after ownership has passed):
- **Lien (Sec 47):** keep possession until paid. This applies when the goods were sold without credit, when the credit period has expired, or when the buyer becomes insolvent. It's lost once the goods are delivered to the buyer.
- **Stoppage in transit (Sec 50):** if the **buyer becomes insolvent**, the seller can stop the goods while they're **still with a carrier**, before they reach the buyer.
- **Resale (Sec 54):** allowed if the goods are **perishable**, or the seller gives **notice** and the buyer doesn't pay within a reasonable time, or the contract reserves the right. The seller can recover any loss from the buyer.

**Rights against the buyer personally:**
- **Suit for the price** (Sec 55), once ownership has passed or payment is due on a fixed date.
- **Damages for non-acceptance** (Sec 56). The usual measure is the contract price minus the market price.
- **Damages for repudiation** before the due date (Sec 60).
- **Interest** (Sec 61).

**The everyday example**
A Burrabazar cloth wholesaler sells **₹80,000** of fabric to a Durgapur shop on 30 days' credit and books it with a transporter.
On day 10, he learns the shop owner has gone insolvent while the truck is still on NH-19. He can tell the transporter to **stop** the goods (stoppage in transit).
If the fabric is still in his own godown, he can simply **keep** it (lien), give notice, and **resell** it if he isn't paid.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jun 2025–Apr 2026)
In **June 2025**, during the Israel–Iran conflict, Indian **basmati rice exporters** said shipments to Iran, their second-largest market, had **nearly stopped**. About **1 lakh tonnes** were stuck at ports, and industry estimates put **₹1,500–2,000 crore** of payments as held up.
By **April 2026**, exporters had decided to **hold back consignments already at ports** and stop fresh dispatches.
Not letting goods go to a buyer who may not pay is exactly the instinct behind an unpaid seller's lien and right of stoppage.
*Sources: Outlook Business, Tribune, Daily Star, Jun 2025–Apr 2026.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Your buyer has gone bankrupt but your goods are still on a truck. What can you do?"*

**Strong answer:** "As an unpaid seller, I can exercise stoppage in transit under Section 50. The buyer is insolvent and the goods are still with the carrier, so I instruct the transporter to stop and redeliver them to me.
Once I have them back, I hold them under lien. Then I give the buyer notice, and if I'm not paid within a reasonable time, I resell and claim any shortfall.
I'd act fast: once the buyer takes delivery, those rights are gone."

**Common trap**
Mixing up lien and stoppage in transit. **Lien** is used while the goods are still in the **seller's** possession. **Stoppage in transit** applies only when the goods are with a **carrier** *and* the buyer is **insolvent**.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* When can an unpaid seller exercise stoppage in transit? **Answer: When the buyer has become insolvent and the goods are still in transit with a carrier** (Sec 50).
2. *(Application)* A seller still holds goods sold on 15 days' credit; the credit period has expired and the buyer hasn't paid. Which right can the seller use? **Answer: Lien.** The seller can keep the goods until paid (Sec 47).
3. *(Mini case)* A buyer refuses to take 100 cartons of mangoes he agreed to buy at ₹500 each. The mangoes will spoil in days. What can the seller do?
   **Answer: Resell them, because they're perishable** (Sec 54), and claim any shortfall from the buyer. If they fetch ₹400 each, he claims ₹10,000: (500 − 400) × 100.
