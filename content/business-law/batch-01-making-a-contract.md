## Card L1 · Agreement vs Contract

**In one line**
An agreement is a set of promises. It becomes a contract only when the law will enforce it.

**Explain it simply**
- **Offer (proposal), Sec 2(a):** one person shows willingness to do, or not do, something.
- **Acceptance, Sec 2(b):** the other person agrees. The offer is now a **promise**.
- **Agreement, Sec 2(e):** promises that are consideration for each other. Offer + acceptance = agreement.
- **Contract, Sec 2(h):** an agreement **enforceable by law**.

So: **contract = agreement + enforceability.** All contracts are agreements, but not all agreements are contracts.

A promise to meet a friend for lunch is an agreement, but no court will enforce it. **Balfour v Balfour (1919)** held that domestic and social promises usually lack the **intention to create legal relations**.

**The everyday example**
- You tell your roommate "I'll pay you ₹200 if you clean the room this week." That's a social agreement, and neither of you expects to go to court.
- You sign up with a cleaning service at ₹1,500 a month, with written terms. That's a contract, and either side can enforce it.

Same work, different intention, so different legal results.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Mar 2024)
In **2024**, **Elon Musk** sued OpenAI, claiming it broke a **"founding agreement"** to stay a non-profit that develops AI for humanity.
OpenAI replied in court papers that there was **no founding agreement, or any agreement at all**, with Musk. Legal experts pointed out that no formal written contract had been signed by all parties.
Shared intentions and emails may show an *understanding*. Whether they form an *enforceable contract* is a separate question, and that difference is this whole card.
*Sources: CNBC, NBC, Mar 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is every agreement a contract?"*

**Strong answer:** "No. A contract is an agreement the law will enforce. Offer plus acceptance gives an agreement. For it to become a contract, it also needs:
- an intention to create legal relations;
- lawful consideration and object;
- parties with capacity;
- free consent.

A social promise, like inviting a friend to dinner, is an agreement but not a contract, as Balfour v Balfour shows. In business I'd never rely on an understanding. I'd get the key terms in writing."

**Common trap**
Saying "a written document makes it a contract". Writing helps prove a contract, but most contracts can be oral. What matters is enforceability, not paper.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Under Section 2(h), what is a contract? **Answer: An agreement enforceable by law.**
2. *(Application)* A father promises his daughter ₹5,000 for topping her class, then doesn't pay. Can she sue? **Answer: No.** It's a domestic promise with no intention to create legal relations (the Balfour principle).
3. *(Mini case)* Two founders agree over WhatsApp to split profits 50:50, but sign nothing. Later one denies it. What must the other prove?
   **Answer: That it was an enforceable contract, not just an understanding.** That means a clear offer and acceptance, an intention to be legally bound, and consideration. The messages can be evidence, but vague or social wording weakens the claim.

**Source:** `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slides 3–5, 8 (Balfour v Balfour)

## Card L2 · The Essentials of a Valid Contract (Section 10)

**In one line**
A valid contract needs a proper offer and acceptance, an intention to be bound, capable parties, free consent, lawful consideration and object, and certainty.

**Explain it simply**
The checklist:
1. **Offer and acceptance**, properly communicated.
2. **Intention to create legal relations.**
3. **Consensus ad idem (Sec 13):** both parties agree on the same thing in the same sense.
4. **Lawful consideration:** something in return.
5. **Capacity:** major, sound mind, not disqualified by law.
6. **Free consent:** no coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake.
7. **Lawful object:** not illegal, immoral or against public policy.
8. **Certainty and possibility of performance.**
9. **Writing or registration**, only where a specific law demands it, such as selling land.

If even one is missing, the agreement may be void, voidable or unenforceable (see Card L9).

**The everyday example**
Riya rents a ₹6,000-a-month PG room near Salt Lake.
- **Offer and acceptance:** the owner offers, she accepts.
- **Intention:** both expect to be bound.
- **Consideration:** ₹6,000 for the room.
- **Capacity:** she's 21.
- **Free consent:** nobody forced her.
- **Lawful object:** renting a room is legal.
- **Certainty:** room number, rent and term are all clear.

All ticked, so it's a valid contract.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Dec 2024)
On **20 December 2024**, the Supreme Court set aside a 2008 NCDRC ruling that had treated **credit-card interest above 30% a year** as an unfair trade practice.
- The Court said the NCDRC could not **rewrite contract terms** that banks and cardholders had **agreed** to.
- It found no misrepresentation by the banks.
- It said regulating interest rates is the RBI's job.

The reasoning rests on two essentials: **free consent** and a **lawful object**. When terms are freely agreed and lawful, courts generally enforce them.
*Sources: LiveLaw, Bar & Bench, Business Standard, Dec 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What makes a contract valid?"*

**Strong answer:** "Section 10 says an agreement is a contract if it's made by the free consent of competent parties, for a lawful consideration and lawful object, and isn't declared void.
Add offer and acceptance, an intention to be bound, and certainty of terms. When I review a vendor agreement, I run through exactly this list:
- Who's signing, and do they have authority?
- Is anything one-sided enough to suggest pressure?
- Are the price and deliverables clear?"

**Common trap**
Forgetting that consideration must be **lawful**, not just present. Paying ₹50,000 to get someone a government job is consideration, but it's unlawful, so the agreement is void.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What does "consensus ad idem" mean? **Answer: A meeting of minds**: both parties agree on the same thing in the same sense (Sec 13).
2. *(Application)* A 16-year-old signs a ₹30,000 phone EMI agreement. Which essential is missing? **Answer: Capacity.** A minor can't contract, so the agreement is void.
3. *(Mini case)* A supplier agrees to sell "good-quality cement at a fair price" with no quantity or price stated. Is it a valid contract?
   **Answer: Probably not.** It fails the certainty requirement, because quality, quantity and price are vague (Sec 29: agreements void for uncertainty). Fix it by stating the grade, quantity, price and delivery date.

**Source:** `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slide 6; Indian Contract Act Sec 10, 13, 29
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card L3 · Offers and Their Types (Carlill's Smoke Ball)

**In one line**
An offer is a definite willingness to be bound. It can be made to one person or to the whole world.

**Explain it simply**
- **Express** (in words) or **implied** (by conduct). A bus running on its route is an implied offer to carry passengers.
- **Specific offer:** made to a particular person; only they can accept.
- **General offer:** made to the public. Anyone who performs the conditions accepts it.
- **Cross offers:** identical offers that cross in the post. There's no contract until one is accepted.
- **Counter offer:** a reply that changes the terms. It **kills** the original offer and becomes a new one.
- **Standing offer:** open for a period, such as a tender to supply goods as and when ordered.

**Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893):** the company promised £100 to anyone who caught flu after using its smoke ball as directed. Mrs Carlill used it, caught flu and claimed. The court held the ad was a **general offer** accepted **by performance**, so there was no need to tell the company in advance.

**The everyday example**
A lost-dog poster in Ballygunge says "**₹2,000** to whoever returns Bruno."
- That's a **general offer**. Anyone who finds and returns Bruno accepts it by doing so, and is entitled to the ₹2,000.
- If the owner had told only his neighbour "I'll pay *you* ₹2,000 to find Bruno", that's a **specific offer**, and only the neighbour could accept.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov 2024)
In **October–November 2024**, Elon Musk's **America PAC** promised **$1 million a day** to registered voters in swing states who signed its petition. It handed out about $16–17 million.
- Philadelphia's District Attorney sued, calling it an illegal lottery.
- In court on **4 November**, the PAC's lawyer said winners were **"not chosen by chance"**; they were picked as spokespeople and signed contracts.
- The judge allowed it to continue.

A public promise of money is a classic general offer, as in Carlill. Its exact terms (random draw, or selected spokesperson?) decide what people actually accepted.
*Sources: ABC News, Breaking News IE, Nov 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is a cashback advertisement legally binding?"*

**Strong answer:** "It can be. Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball says a clear public promise with definite terms is a general offer, and anyone who meets the conditions accepts it by performance.
So if a brand advertises '₹500 cashback on every purchase above ₹5,000 this week', a buyer who meets that can enforce it. That's why marketing teams must write offer terms carefully, with clear conditions, limits and end dates."

**Common trap**
Thinking a **counter offer** keeps the first offer alive. It doesn't. "I'll take it for ₹900 instead of ₹1,000" rejects the original offer, and you can't go back and accept ₹1,000 unless the seller agrees again.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Which case established that an advertisement can be a general offer accepted by performance? **Answer: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893).**
2. *(Application)* A seller offers a bike for ₹40,000; the buyer replies "₹35,000, final." What has the buyer done? **Answer: Made a counter offer**, which rejects the original ₹40,000 offer.
3. *(Mini case)* A coaching centre advertises "₹10,000 to any student who scores 99 percentile after our course", then refuses to pay a student who did. What's the student's argument?
   **Answer: It was a general offer, accepted by performance.** The student met the conditions, so a contract was formed (Carlill). The centre is bound unless its terms clearly said otherwise.

**Source:** `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slides 7, 23–27 (Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card L4 · Offer vs Invitation to Offer

**In one line**
A price list, an ad, an auction notice or a quote is usually an **invitation** to make offers, not an offer itself.

**Explain it simply**
An **offer** shows final willingness to be bound if accepted.
An **invitation to offer (invitation to treat)** only starts negotiations. *You* make the offer; the seller decides whether to accept.
Examples: goods on a shop shelf with price tags, catalogues, auction ads, tender notices and company prospectuses.

**Cases from the slides:**
- **Harvey v Facey (1893):** "Lowest price for Bumper Hall Pen £900" was just information about price, not an offer to sell. There was no contract.
- **Harris v Nickerson (1873):** an auction ad is an invitation, so a buyer who travelled to an auction that was then cancelled couldn't recover his expenses.
- **Taylor v Portington (1855):** terms like "handsomely decorated" are too vague. An offer must be **definite** (Sec 29).

**The everyday example**
- A Gariahat shop tags a kurta at **₹899**. You take it to the counter and offer ₹899. The cashier accepts by billing it.
- If the tag was wrong and the shop refuses, generally there's no breach. The tag invited your offer; it wasn't a promise to sell.
- "Lowest price for my scooter is ₹45,000" is only information. "I offer to sell you my scooter for ₹45,000" is an offer.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Oct 2024)
In **October 2024**, **Hyundai Motor India** ran India's **biggest IPO** till then, about **₹27,870 crore**. Bids came in for about **2.37 times** the shares on offer.
The prospectus is an **invitation to offer**. Each investor's application is the **offer**, and allotment is the company's **acceptance**. That's why, when an issue is oversubscribed, many bidders get fewer shares or none, and no one has a breach claim.
*Sources: Business Standard, Angel One, IANS, Oct 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"An e-commerce site lists a ₹70,000 phone at ₹7,000 by mistake. Must it deliver?"*

**Strong answer:** "Usually a product listing is an invitation to offer. My order is the offer, and the site accepts when it confirms. So if it cancels before confirming, there's usually no contract.
Once it has confirmed the order, though, Indian consumer forums have held platforms liable for cancelling. The answer depends on the timing of acceptance and the site's terms.
As a business, I'd make sure our terms say clearly when an order is accepted."

**Common trap**
Calling every price display an offer. The law treats most displays and ads as invitations, so shops aren't forced to sell to everyone or honour every typo.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* In Harvey v Facey, why was there no contract? **Answer: A statement of the lowest price is not an offer to sell.** It's just information.
2. *(Application)* A company invites tenders for supplying uniforms. Is the tender notice an offer? **Answer: No, it's an invitation to offer.** Each tender submitted is an offer, which the company may accept or reject.
3. *(Mini case)* An auction house advertises a vintage car, then withdraws it on the day. A collector who flew in from Delhi sues for the ₹18,000 airfare. Will he win?
   **Answer: Unlikely.** An auction advertisement is an invitation to offer (Harris v Nickerson). There's no offer, so no contract and no claim for expenses.

**Source:** `Cases.pptx` (Harvey v Facey, Harris v Nickerson, Taylor v Portington); `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slide 28
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card L5 · Rules of Acceptance

**In one line**
Acceptance must be absolute, communicated, in the right mode and on time. You can't accept an offer you don't know about, and silence isn't acceptance.

**Explain it simply**
1. **Absolute and unconditional:** any change is a counter offer.
2. **Communicated** to the offeror. A mental decision isn't enough.
3. **In the mode the offeror prescribed**, or a reasonable one if none was named.
4. **Within the time allowed**, or within a reasonable time.
5. **By the right person:** anyone for a general offer, only the named person for a specific one.
6. **With knowledge of the offer.** In **Fitch v Snedaker (1868)**, people who gave information about a crime *before* knowing of a reward couldn't claim it.
7. **Silence is not acceptance.** "If I don't hear back, the deal's on" doesn't bind the other side.

**The everyday example**
A gym emails: "Your **₹999** monthly plan will auto-renew at **₹1,499** unless you reply."
You ignore it. Under contract principles, your **silence** isn't acceptance of the higher price.
If you reply "OK, renew at ₹1,499", you've accepted. If you reply "Renew, but at ₹999", that's a counter offer.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jun 2025)
On **5 June 2025**, India's **Central Consumer Protection Authority** asked e-commerce and other platforms to **self-audit for dark patterns within three months** and remove them. The advisory went to over 50 platforms, including streaming, food delivery and fintech apps.
Dark patterns include **subscription traps** and free trials that silently turn into paid plans. The idea is simple: real agreement needs a clear, informed "yes", not inertia or pre-ticked boxes.
*Sources: Outlook Money, Storyboard18, Bar & Bench, Jun 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Can a company treat a customer's silence as acceptance?"*

**Strong answer:** "No. Acceptance has to be communicated, and silence generally isn't acceptance. The offeror can't impose it by saying 'if you don't reply, you've agreed.'
That's why auto-renewals and price increases need clear consent, and India's dark-pattern rules target subscription traps and pre-ticked boxes. As a product manager I'd design for an explicit 'yes', with easy cancellation. It's both legal hygiene and good for trust."

**Common trap**
Mixing up **conditional acceptance** with acceptance. "I accept, provided you deliver by Friday" adds a new term, so it's a counter offer, not acceptance.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What did Fitch v Snedaker decide? **Answer: You can't accept an offer you don't know about.** Acting in ignorance of a reward offer isn't acceptance.
2. *(Application)* A seller writes: "If I don't hear from you by Monday, I'll assume you're buying my laptop for ₹35,000." The buyer says nothing. Is there a contract? **Answer: No.** Silence is not acceptance.
3. *(Mini case)* A company offers a job "accept by email by 5 pm Friday". The candidate decides yes on Friday but emails on Monday. Is there a contract?
   **Answer: No.** Acceptance must be communicated, in the prescribed mode, within the time allowed. A mental decision on Friday doesn't count. Monday's email is, at best, a fresh offer the company may accept.

**Source:** `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slides 30–32; `Cases.pptx` (Fitch v Snedaker)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card L6 · Consideration and Privity

**In one line**
Consideration is the "something in return" for a promise. In India it can come from the promisee **or any other person**.

**Explain it simply**
**Sec 2(d):** when, at the promisor's desire, the promisee or *any other person* does, or promises to do, something, that is the consideration.

Rules:
- It must move **at the promisor's desire** (Durga Prasad v Baldeo).
- It may move from the promisee **or a third party**. In **Chinnaya v Ramayya (1882)**, a mother gifted property to her daughter on condition that she pay the mother's sister an annual sum. The sister could sue, even though she gave nothing herself.
- It can be **past, present or future**.
- It **need not be adequate**, but it must be real and lawful.

**No consideration, no contract.** The exceptions under Sec 25:
- written and registered promises out of natural love and affection;
- compensation for past voluntary services;
- a written promise to pay a time-barred debt;
- completed gifts.

**Privity:** only parties to a contract can sue on it. Exceptions include trusts, family arrangements, agents and acknowledgement.

**The everyday example**
- You sell your old cycle to a classmate for **₹1,500**, though it's worth ₹4,000. The contract is still valid; consideration need not be adequate, as long as you agreed freely.
- Your uncle promises in a registered letter to pay your **₹60,000** fees out of love. That's enforceable under the love-and-affection exception.
- An oral "I'll pay your fees" with nothing in return isn't enforceable.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jan 2025)
In **January 2025**, in *Urmila Dixit v Sunil Sharan Dixit*, the Supreme Court **quashed a gift deed** a mother had made to her son.
- The son had signed a promise to **care for her for life**, with the gift linked to that promise.
- He failed to do so. The Court held that tribunals under the Senior Citizens Act can order possession returned.
- It ordered possession restored to the mother by 28 February 2025.

Like Chinnaya, the transfer came with a condition, and the person who failed to honour it lost the benefit.
*Sources: Bar & Bench, Verdictum, Moneylife, Jan 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Is a contract valid if the price is far below market value?"*

**Strong answer:** "Yes, if consent was free. Consideration must be real and lawful, but it need not be adequate, so courts don't judge whether you got a good bargain.
But if the price is shockingly low, the court may ask whether consent was really free, for example whether there was undue influence or fraud. So inadequacy isn't fatal by itself, but it can be evidence of a consent problem."

**Common trap**
Applying the English rule that consideration must come *from the promisee*. Indian law (Sec 2(d)) allows it to come from **any other person**. That's exactly why Chinnaya won.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Does consideration under Indian law have to move from the promisee? **Answer: No.** It may move from the promisee or any other person (Sec 2(d), Chinnaya v Ramayya).
2. *(Application)* A sells his ₹2 lakh car to B for ₹50,000, willingly. Is the contract void for inadequate consideration? **Answer: No.** Consideration need not be adequate if consent is free.
3. *(Mini case)* Your neighbour promised you ₹10,000 "because you're like my son", but signed nothing. Later he refuses. Can you sue?
   **Answer: No.** There's no consideration, and the love-and-affection exception needs the promise to be in writing, registered, and between near relatives. An oral promise between neighbours doesn't qualify.

**Source:** `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slides 34–38; `Cases.pptx` (Chinnaya v Ramayya)
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card L7 · Capacity: Minors and Unsound Minds

**In one line**
Only a major of sound mind, not disqualified by law, can contract. An agreement with a minor is void from the start.

**Explain it simply**
**Who can contract (Sec 11):** a person who is **18 or over** (the Indian Majority Act), of **sound mind**, and **not disqualified** by law (for example an alien enemy or an insolvent).

**Minors:**
- An agreement with a minor is **void ab initio**. In **Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose (1903)**, a minor's mortgage was void, and he didn't have to repay the loan.
- A minor **can't ratify** it on turning 18.
- **No estoppel:** a minor who lied about his age can still plead minority.
- A minor **can be a promisee or beneficiary**. For example, he can receive a gift or be admitted to partnership benefits.
- **Necessaries** (food, clothes, education) supplied to a minor: the supplier can recover a reasonable price from the minor's **property**, not from the minor personally (Sec 68).

**Unsound mind (Sec 12):** a person can contract when they're able to understand it and judge its effect on their interests. That covers someone usually sound when sound, and someone usually unsound in a lucid interval. Drunkenness can count as unsound mind at that moment.

**The everyday example**
A 17-year-old buys a **₹25,000** gaming laptop on a pay-later app and stops paying. The app generally can't enforce the agreement against him, because it's void.
If a shop had sold him **₹800** of school uniforms on credit, it could recover a reasonable price from his property, because those are necessaries.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Nov 2025)
In **November 2025**, the government notified the **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules**. Platforms now need **verifiable consent from a parent or guardian** before processing personal data of anyone **under 18**. Tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted ads aimed at children are barred.
It's the same thinking as contract law: the law treats under-18s as not yet able to give binding consent, so an adult must step in.
*Sources: Seclore, Anantam IAS, law-firm summaries, Nov 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"A 17-year-old lies about his age and takes a loan. Can the lender recover it?"*

**Strong answer:** "Generally no. An agreement with a minor is void ab initio (Mohori Bibee). Estoppel doesn't apply, so his lie doesn't make him liable on the contract.
The court may, in equity, order him to return any benefit he still has, such as the goods themselves. For a lender, the real lesson is to verify age properly. KYC isn't paperwork; it's what makes the contract enforceable."

**Common trap**
Saying a minor's contract is "voidable". It's **void**, not voidable. The minor doesn't need to cancel it, because it never had legal force.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What did Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose decide? **Answer: An agreement with a minor is void ab initio.**
2. *(Application)* A minor gets ₹3,000 of textbooks on credit. Can the seller recover the money? **Answer: Yes, from the minor's property, but not from him personally**, because textbooks are necessaries (Sec 68).
3. *(Mini case)* A start-up signs up 16- and 17-year-olds to a paid ₹499-a-month app subscription with no parental involvement. What's the legal risk?
   **Answer: The subscriptions are void contracts with minors, and the start-up can't enforce payment.** Under the DPDP Rules, processing their data without verifiable parental consent is also non-compliant. The fix is age checks and parent-led sign-up.

**Source:** `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slides 40–47
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card L8 · Free Consent: Coercion vs Undue Influence

**In one line**
Consent isn't free if it's obtained by threat (coercion) or by abusing a position of trust (undue influence). Such contracts are voidable by the victim.

**Explain it simply**
Consent is **free (Sec 14)** when it isn't caused by:
- coercion;
- undue influence;
- fraud;
- misrepresentation;
- mistake.

| | Coercion (Sec 15) | Undue influence (Sec 16) |
|---|---|---|
| How | Threats: committing, or threatening to commit, an act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code (now the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita), or unlawfully detaining property | Misusing a position of trust or authority to dominate the other's will |
| Pressure | Physical or threat-based | Mental or moral |
| Example | "Sign or I'll harm you" | A doctor gets an ill patient to sell property cheaply |
| Can be by | Anyone, even a stranger | Someone in a relationship of dominance |

**Chikham Ammiraju v Chikham Seshamma (1917):** a husband threatened suicide to make his wife and son sign a release deed. The court held that a threat of an act forbidden by the penal code (attempted suicide was then punishable) was **coercion**.

Both make the contract **voidable** at the victim's option (Sec 19, 19A).

**The everyday example**
- A landlord says "pay ₹15,000 extra or I'll lock your stuff in the room." That's **coercion**, through unlawful detention of property.
- A college warden pressures a dependent student into renting his relative's flat at **₹12,000**, twice the market rate. That's likely **undue influence**, because the warden has authority over the student.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Oct 2024)
On **27 October 2024**, PM Modi used *Mann Ki Baat* to warn about **"digital arrest" scams**.
- Fraudsters pose as police or CBI on video calls and threaten victims with arrest until they transfer money.
- He stressed that **no law allows "digital arrest"**.
- Official data put losses at about **₹120 crore** in January–April 2024 alone.

Money handed over under such threats isn't given with free consent. It's the textbook picture of coercion, and a crime as well.
*Sources: Business Today, Scroll, I4C data via MHA, Oct 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the difference between coercion and undue influence?"*

**Strong answer:** "Coercion is pressure by threat: committing or threatening an act forbidden by the penal code, or unlawfully holding someone's property, to make them agree. Anyone can coerce, even a stranger.
Undue influence is subtler. Someone in a position of trust or authority, like a doctor, guardian or senior, uses it to dominate the other person's decision.
Both make the contract voidable by the victim. The difference matters for proof: undue influence needs you to show the relationship of dominance."

**Common trap**
Calling a coerced contract **void**. It's **voidable**: valid until the victim chooses to cancel it, and the victim can even choose to keep it.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is the legal effect of a contract obtained by coercion? **Answer: It is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was coerced** (Sec 19).
2. *(Application)* A spiritual guru persuades a devoted follower to transfer her ₹40 lakh flat to his trust. Which vitiating factor is most likely? **Answer: Undue influence**, a misuse of a position of trust and dominance.
3. *(Mini case)* A transporter refuses to release a trader's goods unless the trader signs a new contract at a 30% higher rate. The trader signs. Can he get out of it?
   **Answer: Yes. It's likely coercion**, through unlawful detention of goods, so the contract is voidable at the trader's option. He should act promptly and keep evidence of the threat.

**Source:** `Indian Contract Act.ppt` slide 9 (Chikham Ammiraju v Chikham Seshamma); Indian Contract Act Sec 14–16, 19
**Status:** DRAFT

## Card L9 · Valid, Void, Voidable, Illegal and Unenforceable

**In one line**
Contracts differ in legal strength: valid ones bind, voidable ones bind until cancelled, void ones never bind, and illegal ones also taint related deals.

**Explain it simply**
| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Valid** | Meets every essential | A signed rental agreement |
| **Void agreement** | No legal effect from the start | An agreement with a minor |
| **Becomes void** | Valid at first, but later impossible or illegal | A singer falls seriously ill before the show |
| **Voidable** | Valid until the wronged party cancels it | Consent obtained by coercion or fraud |
| **Illegal** | Forbidden by law; related deals are also tainted | An agreement to smuggle goods |
| **Unenforceable** | Valid but can't be enforced for a technical reason, until the defect is fixed | An unstamped agreement |

**By performance:**
- **Executed:** both sides have done their part.
- **Executory:** something is still to be done, by one side or both.

**By formation:**
- **Express:** in words, written or spoken.
- **Implied:** by conduct.
- **Quasi-contract:** the law creates an obligation to prevent unjust enrichment, for example paying for goods delivered to you by mistake (Sec 68–72).
- **Contingent (Sec 31):** depends on an uncertain event, like an insurance payout.

**The everyday example**
- **Valid:** a ₹3,000 tuition deal with a junior.
- **Void:** a ₹500 bet on a cricket match. Wagering agreements are void (Sec 30).
- **Voidable:** a ₹20,000 bike sale where the seller hid that it had been in an accident.
- **Unenforceable:** a ₹8 lakh flat agreement that should have been stamped, until it's stamped.
- **Illegal:** paying ₹10,000 for a fake attendance certificate.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Aug 2025)
In **August 2025**, Parliament passed the **Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025**, banning **real-money online games** across India.
Within days, **Dream11** paused all paid "pay to play" contests, which reportedly made up over 90% of its revenue, and moved to free-to-play. It told users their balances were safe to withdraw.
Once the law forbids an activity, agreements to play it for money lose their lawful object. A once-valid business model becomes unlawful, which is why the platforms stopped at once.

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the difference between a void and a voidable contract?"*

**Strong answer:** "A void agreement has no legal effect at all; it's as if it never existed, like an agreement with a minor or a wager. A voidable contract is valid until the party whose consent was affected chooses to cancel it, as with coercion, fraud or undue influence.
Practically, with a voidable contract the wronged party has a choice and should act quickly. With a void one, neither side can enforce anything."

**Common trap**
Treating "illegal" and "void" as the same. Every illegal agreement is void, but not every void agreement is illegal. A wager is void but not a crime in itself; smuggling is both void and illegal, and taints connected deals too.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is a voidable contract? **Answer: A contract that is valid until one party, usually the one whose consent wasn't free, chooses to cancel it.**
2. *(Application)* Two friends bet ₹1,000 on who will win the IPL final. What kind of agreement is it? **Answer: Void.** It's a wagering agreement (Sec 30).
3. *(Mini case)* A caterer contracts to serve a wedding, but a government order bans gatherings that week. What happens to the contract?
   **Answer: It becomes void** because of supervening impossibility or illegality (Sec 56, frustration). Any advance received for performance that can't now happen must be returned (Sec 65).
