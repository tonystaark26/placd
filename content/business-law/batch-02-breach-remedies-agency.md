## Card L10 · Puffery vs Misleading Claims (the Red Bull "Wings" Case)

**In one line**
Vague boasting ("the world's best pizza") is puffery and isn't actionable. A specific, checkable claim about what a product does must be true.

**Explain it simply**
- **Puffery:** exaggerated, subjective praise that no reasonable buyer takes literally, such as "unbeatable taste" or "gives you wings".
- **Factual claim:** a measurable promise, such as "improves concentration by 30%", "cures diabetes" or "2× more protein". It must be **substantiated**. If false, it can be:
  - a **misleading advertisement** (in India: the Consumer Protection Act 2019 and CCPA guidelines);
  - a **breach of an express warranty** (Card L18).

**Careathers v Red Bull (US, 2014):** a buyer claimed "Red Bull gives you wings" implied better performance than a cheap cup of coffee. Red Bull denied wrongdoing but **settled for $13 million**, offering buyers $10 cash or $15 of product. Brands then added disclaimers that slogans are figurative.

The test is what a **reasonable consumer** would understand from the ad.

**The everyday example**
- A Kolkata coaching centre's ad says "**the city's best faculty**". That's puffery.
- Another says "**100% selection guaranteed, or full ₹45,000 refund**". That's a factual promise. If a student isn't selected and the centre won't refund, it's both a misleading ad and a breach of its promise.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Apr–Aug 2024)
In **2024**, the Supreme Court took up **Patanjali Ayurved's** ads that claimed to treat serious diseases. The company had assured the Court in November 2023 that it would stop such claims.
- In **April 2024**, the Court refused Ramdev and Balkrishna's early apologies as lip service.
- They then apologised in person.
- In **August 2024**, the Court closed the contempt case with a warning.

Health claims like "cures" aren't puffery. They're factual claims that need proof, and courts treat them seriously.
*Sources: Exchange4media, Scroll, The News Minute, Apr–Aug 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"As a brand manager, how do you write bold ads without legal risk?"*

**Strong answer:** "I separate puffery from claims.
- Emotional, obviously subjective lines, like 'taste the happiness', are fine.
- Anything measurable, like '2× faster', 'clinically proven' or 'cures', needs evidence on file before the ad runs, plus clear disclaimers.

India's consumer law and the CCPA's misleading-ads guidelines make the brand, and sometimes endorsers, accountable. Red Bull paid $13 million over 'wings', and Patanjali ended up before the Supreme Court over health claims."

**Common trap**
Thinking "it's just marketing" protects any claim. Once a claim is specific and checkable, the law treats it as a statement of fact.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is puffery? **Answer: Vague, exaggerated praise that no reasonable consumer takes literally**, so it isn't treated as a factual promise.
2. *(Application)* A shampoo ad says "Reduces hair fall by 90% in 2 weeks." Puffery or factual claim? **Answer: A factual claim.** It's specific and measurable, so it must be substantiated.
3. *(Mini case)* Red Bull settled for $13 million without admitting wrongdoing. Why might a company settle when it believes its slogan is puffery?
   **Answer: To avoid litigation costs, uncertainty and brand damage.** A class action could cost more than the settlement. Settling also let Red Bull add disclaimers and move on.

## Card L11 · Breach of Contract: Anticipatory vs Actual

**In one line**
A breach is breaking a contractual promise. It can happen before the due date (anticipatory) or on or during performance (actual).

**Explain it simply**
**Anticipatory breach (Sec 39)** happens before performance is due, by:
- **express refusal:** "I won't deliver next month";
- **conduct that makes performance impossible:** selling the promised item to someone else.

The innocent party can choose to:
1. **treat the contract as ended** and sue for damages straight away; or
2. **keep it alive** and wait for the due date. The risk is that if it then becomes impossible for some other reason, the right to damages may be lost.

**Actual breach** happens:
- on the due date, when a party fails to perform; or
- **during** performance, when a party stops halfway or performs badly.

**The everyday example**
- On 1 March, Peter agrees to sell his guitar to John for **₹5,000** on 10 March. On 7 March he sells it to Oliver. That's anticipatory breach by **conduct**, and John can sue right away.
- A tailor promises a sherwani for **₹12,000** by 15 December for a wedding on the 16th, and doesn't deliver on the 15th. That's **actual breach**.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jan–Aug 2024)
On **22 January 2024**, **Sony** called off its roughly **$10 billion merger with Zee**. It said Zee had failed to meet the **closing conditions**, and there was also a dispute over who would lead the merged company.
- Each side claimed a **$90 million termination fee** from the other.
- Sony went to arbitration in Singapore within two days; Zee went to the NCLT.
- In **August 2024**, they settled and **withdrew all claims**.

When a deal collapses before completion, the fight is about who failed to perform, and when.
*Sources: LiveLaw, YourStory, Social Samosa, Jan–Aug 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"A supplier tells you in March that it won't deliver in June as contracted. What can you do?"*

**Strong answer:** "That's anticipatory breach by express refusal. I have two options:
- treat the contract as ended now, sue for damages, and immediately line up another supplier; or
- keep it alive and wait.

In business I'd usually do the first. I'd mitigate by sourcing elsewhere and claim the extra cost from the supplier. Waiting only increases the risk and the loss."

**Common trap**
Thinking you must wait for the due date before suing. With anticipatory breach, you can sue immediately.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What is anticipatory breach? **Answer: A breach before the performance date**, by express refusal or by conduct that makes performance impossible.
2. *(Application)* A seller agrees to sell you a specific painting on 30 June but sells it to someone else on 10 June. What kind of breach? **Answer: Anticipatory breach by conduct.**
3. *(Mini case)* An event company was paid ₹2 lakh to run a fest. Halfway through day 1 it pulls its crew out. What breach is this, and what can the college claim?
   **Answer: Actual breach during performance.** The college can end the contract and claim damages, such as the extra cost of a replacement vendor and losses arising naturally from the breach. It must return any benefit it received if it rescinds.

## Card L12 · Damages: The Main Remedy (Section 73–74)

**In one line**
Damages are money for the loss a breach causes, but only for losses that arise naturally or that both sides could foresee when they contracted.

**Explain it simply**
**Sec 73:** the injured party gets compensation for loss that:
- **arose naturally in the usual course of things** from the breach; or
- both parties **knew, when contracting, was likely** to result from it.

**Remote, indirect loss isn't covered.** This is the rule in *Hadley v Baxendale*.
The injured party must also **mitigate**, that is, take reasonable steps to reduce the loss.

**Liquidated vs unliquidated:**
- **Liquidated:** the amount is fixed in the contract ("₹5,000 per day of delay"). Under **Sec 74**, Indian courts award **reasonable compensation up to that amount**. India doesn't strictly separate penalty clauses from genuine pre-estimates, as English law does.
- **Unliquidated:** the court assesses the amount after the breach.

**Other kinds:**
- **Nominal damages:** a token amount where a right was breached but there was no real loss.
- **Exemplary (punitive) damages:** rare in contract cases.
- **Special damages:** for unusual losses, only if the other side knew of them when contracting.

**The everyday example**
A caterer agrees to serve 200 guests for **₹80,000** at a wedding, then cancels a day before. The family hires another caterer at **₹1,05,000**.
- **Damages:** the extra **₹25,000**. That's a natural, foreseeable loss.
- The family can't claim the honeymoon they say they "had to postpone because of stress". That's too remote.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Sep 2024)
In **September 2024**, **Disney Star (Star India)** sought **$940 million in damages** from **Zee** at the London Court of International Arbitration.
- Star said Zee failed to pay the **first instalment of about $203.6 million** under their 2022 deal to sublicense ICC cricket TV rights.
- Zee denied the claims. It argued the contract had become void because Star hadn't met certain conditions, and filed an $8 million counterclaim.

The claim is the classic Sec 73 question: what loss flowed naturally from the alleged breach?
*Sources: Bloomberg via BNN, SportBusiness, Exchange4media, Sep 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Your vendor delivers late and you lose a big client. Can you claim the client's lifetime value?"*

**Strong answer:** "Only if that loss was foreseeable when we contracted. Under Section 73 we recover losses arising naturally from the breach, or ones both sides knew were likely.
If the vendor knew this delivery was for that specific client launch, we have a stronger claim. Otherwise lost future profits may be too remote.
That's why important contracts should state the stakes, or have a liquidated-damages clause. Under Section 74 we'd then get reasonable compensation up to the agreed figure."

**Common trap**
Assuming a liquidated-damages clause gets you the full stated amount automatically. In India, the court awards **reasonable compensation not exceeding** that amount, and you may still need to show some loss or harm.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Under Section 73, which losses are recoverable? **Answer: Losses that arise naturally from the breach, or that both parties knew were likely when they contracted.** Remote, indirect losses are not.
2. *(Application)* A contract says "₹2,000 for each day of delay"; the builder is 10 days late. Will the buyer automatically get ₹20,000? **Answer: Not automatically.** Under Sec 74 the court awards reasonable compensation up to ₹20,000.
3. *(Mini case)* A seller fails to deliver 100 bags of rice at ₹2,000 a bag; the buyer buys elsewhere at ₹2,300. What damages?
   **Answer: ₹30,000**, the difference between the market price and the contract price: (2,300 − 2,000) × 100. The buyer mitigated by buying promptly.

## Card L13 · Other Remedies: Rescission, Specific Performance, Injunction, Quantum Meruit

**In one line**
Besides damages, a court can cancel the contract, order it to be performed, stop a party from doing something, or pay for work already done.

**Explain it simply**
- **Rescission (Sec 39, 64, 65, 75):** the innocent party cancels the contract. They must **return any benefit** received (Sec 65), and can still claim compensation for loss (Sec 75).
- **Specific performance (Specific Relief Act):** the court orders the party to actually perform. It's typical for **unique things like land** or rare goods, where money isn't enough. Since the **2018 amendment**, courts must grant it unless an exception in the Act applies, but they still refuse it for personal services.
- **Injunction:** a court order.
  - **Prohibitory:** stops a party from doing something. It's often used to enforce a **negative promise**, like "won't work for a rival during the contract".
  - **Mandatory:** orders a party to do something to put right a breach, such as pulling down a wall built in breach of a covenant.
- **Quantum meruit ("as much as earned"):** when one party is stopped from finishing by the other, they're paid a reasonable amount for the work already done.

**The everyday example**
- **Specific performance:** a seller agreed to sell you a particular **₹40 lakh** flat in Salt Lake and backs out. Each flat is unique, so you can ask the court to order the sale.
- **Quantum meruit:** a web designer is hired for **₹30,000**, finishes 60%, and the client cancels without reason. The designer can claim about **₹18,000** for the work done.
- **Injunction:** your employment contract says you won't work for a competitor *while employed*. The employer can get a prohibitory injunction to stop you breaking that promise during the term.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Dec 2024)
On **10 December 2024**, US courts blocked **Kroger's $24.6 billion takeover of Albertsons** with **injunctions**:
- a federal judge issued a **preliminary injunction**;
- a Washington state judge issued a **permanent** one.

The next day Albertsons **walked away** and sued Kroger for breach. It claimed a **$600 million termination fee** plus billions in damages, saying Kroger hadn't tried hard enough to win approval. Kroger called the claims baseless.
In one week: injunctions, termination and a damages claim, several remedies in play together.
*Sources: AP via ABC News, Philadelphia Inquirer, Dec 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"When would you ask for specific performance instead of damages?"*

**Strong answer:** "When money can't put me back where I'd be: unique assets like a particular plot of land, a rare machine or shares in a private company.
For ordinary goods I'd take damages and buy elsewhere. After the 2018 Specific Relief amendment, specific performance in India is no longer just discretionary; courts must grant it unless an exception applies. But courts won't force personal services, like making a singer perform. There, an injunction enforcing a negative promise during the contract term is the better tool."

**Common trap**
Thinking a court will force an employee to keep working. Courts don't order specific performance of personal service contracts. At most they enforce a reasonable **negative** promise during the contract term. And under Sec 27, restraints **after** the job ends are generally void.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What does quantum meruit mean? **Answer: "As much as earned"**: reasonable payment for work already done when the other party prevents completion.
2. *(Application)* A seller refuses to hand over a rare 1965 vintage car you'd agreed to buy for ₹6 lakh. Which remedy fits best? **Answer: Specific performance.** The car is unique, so money may not be an adequate remedy.
3. *(Mini case)* A rescinds a contract after B's breach, but A has already received ₹50,000 of goods from B. What must A do?
   **Answer: Restore the benefit**, by returning the goods or their value (Sec 65), while claiming compensation for loss caused by the breach (Sec 75).

## Card L14 · Agency: Agent, Principal and Kinds of Authority

**In one line**
An agent acts for a principal with third parties. Whatever the agent does within their authority binds the principal as if the principal did it.

**Explain it simply**
- **Agent (Sec 182):** someone employed to act for another, or represent them in dealings with third parties. The person represented is the **principal**.
- **No consideration is needed** to create an agency (Sec 185).
- **The principal must be a major of sound mind** (Sec 183). Anyone, even a minor, can be an agent, but a minor agent isn't personally liable to the principal (Sec 184).

**Kinds of authority:**
- **Express (Sec 187):** given in words, such as a power of attorney or a board resolution.
- **Implied (Sec 187):** inferred from conduct or the usual course of dealing.
- **Extent (Sec 188):** authority to do a thing includes everything lawful and necessary to do it.
- **Emergency (Sec 189):** an agent can act as a prudent person would to protect the principal from loss, such as selling perishable goods that would rot in transit.
- **Ostensible or apparent (Sec 237):** if the principal's words or conduct made a third party believe the agent had authority, the principal is bound.

**Common agents:** general agents, special agents, brokers (no possession of goods), factors (hold goods and sell in their own name), auctioneers, and del credere agents (they guarantee the buyer will pay, for extra commission).

**The everyday example**
Mr Sen owns a hardware shop in Serampore but lives in Kolkata. His manager Bablu regularly orders **₹20,000** of paint a month from a supplier in Mr Sen's name, and Mr Sen always pays.
Bablu has **implied authority**. If Bablu orders this month's stock and Mr Sen refuses to pay, the supplier can still recover from Mr Sen.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Aug 2024)
From **17 August 2024**, under the **National Association of Realtors' $418 million settlement** in the US, home buyers' agents must sign a **written agreement** with the buyer before touring homes. Offers of commission between agents were also removed from listing systems.
That turns an often-implied relationship into **express authority**, with the agent's role and pay written down for every buyer.
*Sources: RISMedia, Bisnow, NAR, Mar–Aug 2024.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"Can a company be bound by an employee who exceeded their authority?"*

**Strong answer:** "Yes, through ostensible authority, Section 237. If the company's words or conduct led a third party to reasonably believe the employee had authority, the company is bound, even if internally the employee broke a limit.
That's why firms need clear authority matrices, and need to tell counterparties who can sign what. Silence and past practice create implied authority too."

**Common trap**
Thinking an agency needs a formal contract and payment. It can arise from conduct, and needs **no consideration** (Sec 185). A friend helping you sell your bike for free is still your agent.

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Is consideration needed to create an agency? **Answer: No** (Sec 185).
2. *(Application)* A transporter carrying mangoes for a trader finds the truck stuck for days and sells the mangoes before they rot. Was he authorised? **Answer: Yes, under emergency authority** (Sec 189): he acted as a prudent person would to protect the principal from loss.
3. *(Mini case)* A company lets its sales head sign supply orders for two years, then claims his last ₹8 lakh order was unauthorised under an internal limit the supplier never knew about. Who wins?
   **Answer: Probably the supplier.** Through conduct, the company gave the sales head ostensible authority (Sec 237). The internal limit doesn't bind a third party who didn't know of it.

## Card L15 · Sub-Agents and Ratification

**In one line**
An agent usually can't delegate the job to someone else. A principal can later approve an unauthorised act, which then counts as authorised from the start.

**Explain it simply**
**Delegation (Sec 190):** *delegatus non potest delegare*, meaning a delegate can't delegate. An agent must act personally unless **trade custom** or the **nature of the work** requires a sub-agent.
- **Properly appointed sub-agent (Sec 192):** binds the principal. The sub-agent answers to the **agent**, except for fraud or wilful wrong.
- **Improperly appointed sub-agent (Sec 193):** doesn't bind the principal. The agent bears full responsibility.
- **Substituted agent (Sec 194–195):** someone the agent names under express authority becomes the principal's **own agent**. The original agent is only liable if they chose carelessly.

**Ratification (Sec 196–200):** the principal approves an act done without authority, and it then **relates back** to the original date. Conditions:
- it must have been done **on the principal's behalf**;
- the principal must have **full knowledge** of the facts (Sec 198);
- the principal must have had **capacity** both then and now;
- the act must be **lawful**;
- the principal ratifies the **whole** transaction, not just the good parts (Sec 199);
- it can't **harm a third party's vested rights** (Sec 200).

**The everyday example**
Your friend Arka, without asking, books a **₹2,400** Darjeeling toy-train ticket for you while you're unreachable. If you later say "great, thanks", or simply use the ticket, you've **ratified** it. It's as if you'd authorised it from the start, and you owe him ₹2,400.
If you'd refused, he'd bear the cost himself.

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jun 2024–Dec 2025)
In **January 2024**, a Delaware judge struck down Elon Musk's 2018 Tesla pay package, finding the board's process flawed.
- In **June 2024**, Tesla shareholders voted again to **ratify** the package, by a large margin.
- In **December 2024**, the judge said a shareholder vote alone couldn't ratify a deal involving a conflicted controller.
- In **December 2025**, Delaware's Supreme Court **reversed** the cancellation, reinstated the package and awarded $1 in nominal damages.

It's the classic ratification question: can a later approval cure an act that lacked proper authority at the start?
*Sources: AP, Hollywood Reporter, Arab News, Jun 2024–Dec 2025.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What does it mean when a board 'ratifies' a decision?"*

**Strong answer:** "It means approving, after the fact, an act someone took without proper authority. Under Sections 196–200, once ratified, the act is treated as authorised from the start.
But ratification needs full disclosure of the facts, the whole transaction must be approved, and it can't harm third parties' existing rights. The Tesla case shows the limits: courts also look at *how* the approval was obtained, especially when there's a conflict of interest."

**Common trap**
Thinking a principal can ratify the profitable part and reject the rest. Ratifying **part** of a transaction ratifies **all** of it (Sec 199).

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* What does "delegatus non potest delegare" mean? **Answer: A delegate cannot delegate.** An agent can't appoint a sub-agent unless trade custom or the nature of the agency allows it (Sec 190).
2. *(Application)* A buys goods for B without authority. B later sells those goods to C. Has B ratified A's purchase? **Answer: Yes, by conduct** (implied ratification, Sec 197).
3. *(Mini case)* An agent, without authority, signs a ₹5 lakh deal for his principal that includes a bad penalty clause. The principal wants to keep the deal but drop the clause. Can he?
   **Answer: No.** Ratifying part ratifies the whole transaction (Sec 199). He must accept the whole deal, penalty clause included, or reject all of it.

## Card L16 · Duties of Agent and Principal, and Ending an Agency

**In one line**
An agent owes loyalty, skill and honest accounts. The principal owes payment and indemnity. An agency ends by revocation, completion, death, insanity or insolvency, unless the agent has an interest in it.

**Explain it simply**
**Agent's duties:**
- Follow instructions, or the custom of the trade (Sec 211).
- Use reasonable skill and diligence (Sec 212).
- Render proper accounts (Sec 213).
- Communicate with the principal when in difficulty (Sec 214).
- **Make no secret profit:** if the agent deals on their own account without disclosing it, the principal can repudiate the deal and claim the benefit (Sec 215–216).
- Pay over money received, after deducting dues (Sec 217–218).

**Agent's rights:** remuneration on completing the work, but none for misconducted business (Sec 219–220), and a **lien** on the principal's goods until paid (Sec 221).

**Principal's duties:**
- Indemnify the agent for lawful acts (Sec 222) and good-faith acts (Sec 223), but **never for criminal acts** (Sec 224).
- Compensate the agent for injury caused by the principal's own neglect (Sec 225).

**Termination (Sec 201):** by revocation, renunciation, completion, the death or insanity of either party, or the principal's insolvency.
- **Irrevocable agency (Sec 202):** if the agent has an **interest** in the subject matter, the principal can't revoke to that interest's prejudice.
- Once authority has been **partly exercised**, revocation can't undo acts and obligations already arising from it (Sec 204). Revocation needs **reasonable notice** (Sec 206).
- It isn't effective against third parties **until they know** of it (Sec 208).

**The everyday example**
You ask a property broker to find you a flat to buy for up to **₹50 lakh**.
He finds one at ₹45 lakh, secretly buys it himself, then offers it to you at ₹49 lakh. That's a **secret profit**.
You can make him sell it to you at the **₹45 lakh** he paid (Sec 216), and he loses his commission for misconduct (Sec 220).

**Seen in the news** ✅ Verified (Jul 2026)
On **24 July 2026**, SEBI passed a **final order** in the **Axis Mutual Fund front-running case**.
- It barred former chief dealer **Viresh Joshi** from the markets for **seven years** and fined him **₹3 crore**.
- Twenty others got bars of three to seven years.
- The roughly **₹30.56 crore** impounded in 2023 is to be treated as disgorgement, plus interest.

Front-running means trading ahead of the fund's own orders. It's the purest breach of an agent's duty: using a position of trust to make a secret profit.
*Sources: Business Today, Moneylife, Cafemutual, Jul 2026.*

**How interviewers ask it**
> *"What's the most important duty an agent owes the principal?"*

**Strong answer:** "Loyalty: no conflict of interest and no secret profit. Under Sections 215–216, if an agent deals on their own account without full disclosure, the principal can cancel the deal and claim the agent's profit.
Every fund manager, broker or procurement head is in this position. That's why firms have trading restrictions, disclosure rules and audits. Skill and following instructions matter too, but a breach of loyalty is what destroys trust."

**Common trap**
Assuming a principal can always fire an agent mid-way at no cost. If the agency is coupled with the agent's interest (Sec 202), revocation is barred. Acts already done stay binding on the principal (Sec 204), and sudden revocation without notice can mean paying compensation (Sec 205–206).

**Quiz**
1. *(Concept)* Must a principal indemnify an agent for a criminal act done on the principal's instructions? **Answer: No** (Sec 224).
2. *(Application)* A gives B authority to sell A's land and pay off a debt A owes B from the proceeds. Can A revoke this authority? **Answer: No.** It's an agency coupled with interest (Sec 202), so A can't revoke it to B's prejudice.
3. *(Mini case)* A principal revokes his agent's authority but doesn't tell a supplier the agent usually deals with. The agent then places a ₹1 lakh order with that supplier. Is the principal bound?
   **Answer: Yes.** Termination doesn't take effect against third parties until they know of it (Sec 208). The principal should have informed regular counterparties.

**Source:** `Law-of-Agency-under-the-Indian-Contract-Act-1872.pdf` pages 11–19
